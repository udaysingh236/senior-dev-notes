## The question

Your manager asks: "How reliable is our checkout?" You could say "very", or "it was down for a
bit on Tuesday". Neither answer helps anyone decide anything. Then the next question: "Can we
release the new payment feature this week, or is it too risky?" How do you turn "reliable" into
a number, and how does that number answer the second question?

The short answer: agree on three things. First, what you measure, from the user's side: for
example, the share of checkout requests that succeed in under a second. That measurement is the
**SLI**. Second, the target for it: for example, 99.9% over 30 days. That target is the **SLO**.
Third, the gap between the target and perfect, 0.1%, is the number of failures you are allowed
this month: your **error budget**. If there is budget left, ship the feature. If it is used up,
spend the next days making checkout more reliable instead.

## The idea in plain words

We will follow one service through one month. Your shop's **checkout** takes orders. It gets 25
requests a minute, day and night (an illustrative, steady number, to keep the arithmetic clean).
A month of 30 days has 43,200 minutes: 30 days × 24 hours × 60 minutes = 43,200. So checkout
handles 43,200 × 25 = 1,080,000 requests a month.

1. **Decide what "working" means to a customer.** A customer is happy when checkout succeeds and
   answers quickly. So you call a request **good** if it succeeded and answered in under 1 second,
   and **bad** otherwise: an error, or a success that took too long.
2. **Measure the share of good requests.** This share is your **SLI**, the service level
   indicator. For example, if 1,079,000 of the 1,080,000 requests were good, the SLI is
   1,079,000 ÷ 1,080,000 = 99.907%.
3. **Pick a target.** You decide that 99.9% of requests over any 30 days should be good. This is
   your **SLO**, the service level objective. It is lower than 100% on purpose; Part 2 explains why.
4. **Work out what the target allows.** 99.9% good means up to 0.1% bad. 0.1% of 1,080,000
   requests is 1,080 bad requests a month. That allowance is the **error budget**.
5. **Spend the budget, and watch it.** Every bad request uses one unit of budget. In our month,
   four things went wrong:
   - a few requests failed every day for no single reason (0.02% of them): 194 bad requests,
     18% of the budget;
   - on day 3, a 5-minute blip where 5% of requests failed: 6 bad requests, 1% of the budget;
   - on day 10, a 20-minute outage where every request failed: 500 bad requests, 46% of the budget;
   - from day 18, a slow leak for 3 days where 0.3% of requests failed: 324 bad requests, 30%.
6. **Read the result.** Adding them up gives about 1,025 bad requests (194 + 6 + 500 + 324 =
   1,024; the difference of 1 comes from rounding). Of the 1,080 allowed, 1,080 − 1,025 = 55 are
   left, about 5% of the budget. The SLO was met, barely.
7. **Use the budget to decide.** On day 11, after the outage, 46% of the budget is gone in one go.
   With half the month to go, the team should be careful: fix what caused the outage before
   shipping risky changes. If the budget runs out before the month ends, the team agreed ahead of
   time to stop releasing new features and work on reliability until it recovers.

The idea that makes this work is step 7. Without an error budget, "should we ship?" is an
argument between the people who want features and the people who get woken up at night when
things break, and
whoever argues louder wins. With an error budget, it is a number both sides agreed on in advance.
Budget left means the service is reliable enough, so moving fast is fine. Budget gone means users
are already feeling it, so reliability comes first.

There is one more word you will hear: **SLA**, the service level agreement. That is a contract
with a customer that says what happens, usually money back, if the service is less reliable than
promised. Your SLO is your own, stricter target, set so that you notice problems long before you
break the contract.

An everyday picture: a phone plan with 10 GB of data a month. The 10 GB is your budget. Using it
is not bad; that is what it is for. Using half of it in one afternoon is a warning, because the
month is long. And using it all by the 20th means changing how you use the phone until the plan
renews. The picture breaks in one place: your phone company sets your data limit, but you choose
your SLO, and choosing it well is half the skill.

## The building blocks

Five ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A percentage of a percentage, and "nines"

**What it is.** Reliability targets are written as percentages close to 100, such as 99.9%, and
people name them by counting the nines: 99.9% is "three nines", 99.99% is "four nines".

**Why it matters.** The numbers look almost the same, but the room for failure between them is
huge. Going from 99.9% to 99.99% does not add a little reliability; it cuts the allowed failures
to a tenth.

**Example.** A percentage is a share out of 100, so 0.1% means 0.1 out of 100, or 1 out of 1,000.
For the allowed failures, take what is left after the target: 100% − 99.9% = 0.1%. Over 30 days
of 43,200 minutes, 0.1% of the time is 43,200 × 0.001 = 43.2 minutes. Here is the same working for
each number of nines:

| Target | Allowed to fail | Over 30 days (43,200 minutes) |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | 1 in 100 | 432 minutes, about 7.2 hours |
| 99.9% | 1 in 1,000 | 43.2 minutes |
| 99.95% | 1 in 2,000 | 21.6 minutes |
| 99.99% | 1 in 10,000 | 4.32 minutes |
| 99.999% | 1 in 100,000 | about 26 seconds |

The thing to notice: each extra nine divides the allowed failure by 10. At five nines, one slow
restart of one server uses the whole month.

**The technical version.** "Minutes of outage" is the simplest way to picture a target, but
most services count requests, not minutes, because a service is rarely fully down: usually some
requests fail and others work. The two views agree when traffic is steady, as in our example.

### SLI: what you measure

**What it is.** A **service level indicator** (SLI) is a measurement of how well the service
serves users, written as the share of things that went well: good events divided by all events.

**Why it exists.** "Is the server up?" is not what users care about. A server can be up and
answering every request with an error. The SLI measures the thing a user would complain about,
so that when the number drops, users are actually unhappy, and when users are unhappy, the
number drops.

**Example.** Checkout's SLI is "the share of checkout requests that succeed in under 1 second".
In the month above, 1,080,000 requests were made and 1,025 were bad, so the SLI was
(1,080,000 − 1,025) ÷ 1,080,000 = 99.905%.

**The technical version.** An SLI is always a ratio between 0% and 100%:
good events ÷ valid events. "Valid" lets you leave out requests that should not count, such as a
customer's request with a wrong card number, which fails correctly. The most common kinds:

- **Availability:** the share of requests that succeed (for HTTP, did not return a `5xx` status).
- **Latency:** the share of requests faster than a threshold, such as 1 second. Writing latency
  this way, rather than as an average, means one number can describe "fast enough" and add up
  correctly over a month.
- **Freshness:** for data that updates, the share of reads that see data newer than, say, 1
  minute.
- **Correctness:** the share of results that are right, for example checked by comparing a sample
  against a slower, trusted calculation.

### SLO: the target

**What it is.** A **service level objective** (SLO) is a target for an SLI over a period of time:
"99.9% of checkout requests succeed in under 1 second, measured over 30 days".

**Why it exists.** A measurement alone does not tell you whether to worry. The SLO is the line
between "users are fine" and "users are hurting", agreed on before anything goes wrong.

**Example.** At 99.9% over 30 days, the checkout month above ended at 99.905%: a little above the
line. If the slow leak had lasted 4 days instead of 3, it would have spent another 108 bad
requests (one more day: 1,440 minutes × 25 requests × 0.3% = 108), and the month would have
ended below 99.9%.

**The technical version.** An SLO has three parts: the SLI, the target, and the **window**, the
stretch of time it is measured over. Part 2 covers how to choose each one.

### Error budget

**What it is.** The **error budget** is the number of bad events the SLO allows in its window:
everything between the target and 100%.

**Why it exists.** It turns the SLO into something you can spend and plan with. New releases,
experiments and risky changes all spend some budget. Knowing what is left tells you how much
risk you can still afford this month.

**Example.** Checkout's budget is 0.1% of 1,080,000 requests, which is 1,080 bad requests, or
43.2 minutes of full outage. The 20-minute outage on day 10 spent 500 of them: 46%. You can see
the whole month in the chart in Part 2.

**The technical version.** Error budget = (1 − SLO) × valid events in the window. It is usually
shown as "budget remaining", from 100% at the start of the window to 0% when the SLO is exactly
met, and negative when it is missed.

### Burn rate

**What it is.** The **burn rate** is how fast you are spending the error budget, compared with
the speed that would use exactly all of it by the end of the window.

**Why it exists.** "We have 40% of the budget left" does not say whether to worry. 40% left on
day 29 is fine; 40% left on day 2, after losing 60% in one morning, is an emergency. The burn
rate tells you how fast the budget is going right now.

**Example.** A burn rate of 1 means bad requests are arriving at exactly the allowed speed, 0.1%
of requests, so the budget lasts exactly 30 days. During the slow leak, 0.3% of requests failed,
three times the allowed share: 0.3% ÷ 0.1% = a burn rate of 3, which empties a full budget in
30 ÷ 3 = 10 days. During the outage, 100% failed: 100% ÷ 0.1% = a burn rate of 1,000, which
empties it in 43.2 minutes.

**The technical version.** Burn rate = error rate in a recent window ÷ (1 − SLO). It is always
measured over a window, such as "the last hour", and Part 2 shows why the alerts built on it look
at two windows at once.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- The **SLI** is what you measure, from the user's side: the share of requests that were good.
- The **SLO** is the target for it over a window, such as 99.9% over 30 days. It is less than
  100% on purpose.
- The **error budget** is what the target allows to fail: 0.1% of 1,080,000 requests is 1,080 bad
  requests, or 43.2 minutes of full outage, a month.
- Budget left: ship. Budget gone: fix reliability first. The decision is agreed in advance.
- The **SLA** is a contract with money attached, looser than your SLO.
:::

## Going deeper

Your checkout was 99.97% available this month, comfortably above its 99.9% target. Yet customers
complained, sales dropped on Saturday evening, and one large customer is asking for money back
under the SLA. How can every number be green while users are unhappy? And how do you choose an SLI,
a target and alerts so that the numbers turn red exactly when users hurt, and not before?

The answer has four parts, and Part 2 takes them in order.

- **Choosing the SLI**: where you measure, what counts as good, and which requests count at all.
- **Choosing the target and the window**: why not 100%, why the targets of what you depend on
  limit yours, and rolling versus calendar windows.
- **Alerting on the budget**: why alerts on a raw error rate wake people up for nothing and miss
  slow leaks, and how burn-rate alerts with two windows fix both.
- **Using the budget**: the error budget policy, and the SLA on top.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

Teams usually start with one of two measurements.

**"Uptime": ping the server every minute.** A monitor sends one request a minute to a health page
(a small address on the server that answers "OK" when the server is running) and counts the
minutes it answered. It is simple, and it measures the wrong thing. The health page
can answer "OK" while every real checkout fails, because the database is down and the health page
does not touch it. And one request a minute cannot see a problem that hits 5% of requests: 95%
of the pings still succeed. In the opening question, the 99.97% came from exactly this kind of
check, while real customers saw Saturday's failures.

**Average response time.** "Checkout averages 300 ms" sounds healthy. Averages hide the people
who suffer. If 98% of requests take 200 ms and 2% take 5 seconds, the average is
0.98 × 200 + 0.02 × 5,000 = 196 + 100 = 296 ms, which looks fine, while 2 customers in every 100
wait 5 seconds and many give up. The SLI form, "the share of requests faster than 1 second",
reports 98% for the same traffic, which is clearly below a 99.9% target.

### Its neighbours: SLI, SLO, SLA and KPI

| | What it is | Who it is for | What happens when it is missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| SLI | a measurement: share of good events | the team | nothing by itself; it is a number |
| SLO | a target for the SLI over a window | the team and its stakeholders | the error budget policy applies: slow down, fix reliability |
| SLA | a contract based on a target | customers | money back, usually as credits on the next bill |
| KPI (key performance indicator) | a business number, such as orders per day | the business | depends on the business |

The row to notice is the SLA. It is usually *looser* than the SLO. Amazon EC2's region-level SLA,
for example, promises 99.99% monthly uptime and gives a 10% credit on that month's EC2 bill in
the region below that (as of November 2026). A team running on top of it sets its own SLO
tighter than anything it promises customers, so the SLO turns red first and leaves time to fix
things before the contract costs money.

## How it works, piece by piece

### Choosing the SLI: measure what users feel

Three choices decide whether an SLI tells the truth.

**Where to measure.** The closer to the user, the more truthful, and the harder to collect.

- **Inside the service** (its own counters): quick to collect, but blind to anything in front of it. If the
  load balancer in front of checkout fails, the service sees no requests at all and reports 100%.
- **At the load balancer** (its logs or metrics): sees every request that reached your systems,
  including those your service never answered. This is where most teams start.
- **In the user's browser or app**: sees the real experience, including slow networks, but also
  problems you cannot fix, such as a customer's bad Wi-Fi.

In practice I'd measure at the load balancer, and watch browser measurements as a second opinion.

**What counts as good.** Write it down precisely: "status below 500 and answered in under 1,000
ms". Choose the latency threshold from what users notice, not from what the service does today.
A threshold set at today's speed makes every slowdown look like failure; a threshold set too
generously never fires.

**Which requests count.** Leave out what should not count: health checks from your own monitors,
requests from test accounts, and requests that failed correctly (a wrong password returning
`401` is the service working). Include what users really sent, even if it failed before reaching
your code.

### Choosing the target: why not 100%

A target of 100% sounds right and is wrong, for three reasons.

**Users cannot tell.** A customer's own Wi-Fi, mobile network and phone fail far more often than
0.01% of the time. Between 99.99% and 100% on your side, they see no difference.

**Each nine costs much more than the last.** The table in Part 1 shows 99.9% allows 43.2 minutes
a month and 99.99% allows 4.32. Deploying a new version safely, letting a database switch to its
copy after a failure (see [replication](/databases/replication/)), or restarting a server can
each take minutes. At four nines, all of that must happen with no failed requests at all, which
needs much more engineering. At five nines, 26 seconds a month, a person cannot even be woken by an alert
and log in within the budget.

**What you depend on limits you.** Suppose checkout calls three things in turn, the database,
Payments and the stock service, and fails whenever any one of them fails. Each is 99.9%
available. A quick refresher on combining shares: if 999 out of every 1,000 calls to the database
succeed, and 999 out of every 1,000 calls to Payments succeed, then for a request that needs
both, you multiply the shares: 0.999 × 0.999 = 0.998, so about 998 in every 1,000 requests get
through both. With a third dependency: 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 0.997. So checkout, on its own
perfect code, can reach about 99.7%, not 99.9%. Either the dependencies must be better than your
target, or your service must survive their failures (retries, fallbacks, caching), or the target
must be lower.

The practical rule: set the SLO at the point where users start to notice and complain, check it
against what you depend on, and start a little looser than you think. Tightening a target later
is painless; explaining why you missed an over-ambitious one every month is not.

**Rolling or calendar window.** The window is how far back the SLO looks.

- A **rolling** window, "the last 30 days", moves forward every minute. A bad day hurts for
  exactly 30 days and then leaves the window. It matches what users remember.
- A **calendar** window, "this month", resets on the 1st. It is convenient for reports, but an outage
  on the 30th and another on the 2nd fall in different months, so each month looks fine while
  users had two bad weeks.

Rolling 28 or 30 days is the common choice for the SLO itself; calendar months are common for
SLAs and reports. 28 days has the advantage of containing exactly 4 of each weekday, so weekend
traffic does not tilt the comparison between windows.

**Requests or time slices.** So far each request counted once. Some tools instead split time into
slices, say 1 minute each, call a minute good if it met the target, and count good minutes. That
is a **windows-based** SLO (Google Cloud's name; OpenSLO calls it `Timeslices`), as opposed to a
**request-based** one (`Occurrences`). Request-based follows users more closely, because a busy
minute counts more than a quiet one. Time slices are useful when traffic is very low and a single
failed request in a quiet hour should not count as a big share.

### Alerting on the budget: burn rates and two windows

Alerts come in two strengths. A **page** wakes up the engineer on call, day or night. A
**ticket** adds a task to the team's to-do list for working hours. The first alert most teams
write is: "page someone if more than 1% of requests failed in the last 5 minutes." The runnable example checks that rule against the month, together with three
burn-rate alerts, and records how long after each problem started each alert first fires:

```text
When does each alert fire? (counted from the start of each problem)
  5-minute blip, 5% errors
    naive: errors > 1% over 5 min   fires after 1 min
    page: 14.4x over 1 h and 5 min   never fires
    page: 6x over 6 h and 30 min     never fires
    ticket: 1x over 3 days and 6 h   never fires
  20-minute outage, 100% errors
    naive: errors > 1% over 5 min   fires after 1 min
    page: 14.4x over 1 h and 5 min   fires after 1 min
    page: 6x over 6 h and 30 min     fires after 3 min
    ticket: 1x over 3 days and 6 h   fires after 4 min
  3-day slow leak, 0.3% errors
    naive: errors > 1% over 5 min   never fires
    page: 14.4x over 1 h and 5 min   never fires
    page: 6x over 6 h and 30 min     never fires
    ticket: 1x over 3 days and 6 h   fires after 1235 min
```

The naive rule is wrong twice. It woke someone up for the blip, which spent 1% of the budget and
had ended before anyone could look. And it never fired for the leak, which spent 30%. Fixed
error-rate thresholds behave like this everywhere: short spikes cross them, and slow damage stays
under them.

**Burn-rate alerts** ask a better question: "at the speed we are failing right now, how soon
will the budget be gone?" Each alert has a burn rate and a window, and the numbers are chosen by
how much budget a problem spends before the alert fires.

- **14.4× over 1 hour pages.** One hour is 1/720 of 30 days (30 × 24 = 720 hours). At a burn rate
  of 14.4, one hour spends 14.4 × 1/720 = 0.02, which is 2% of the whole month's budget. Losing 2%
  in an hour is worth waking someone for, because at that speed the whole budget is gone in
  720 ÷ 14.4 = 50 hours, about two days.
- **6× over 6 hours pages.** Six hours is 6/720 of the month; 6 × 6/720 = 0.05, so 5% of the
  budget. Slower, but it would still empty the budget in 720 ÷ 6 = 120 hours, five days.
- **1× over 3 days opens a ticket.** A burn rate of 1 over 3 days means the service is failing
  at exactly the allowed speed or worse; no one needs to wake up, but someone should look this
  week. It is the alert that caught the leak, after about 20.6 hours (1,235 minutes ÷ 60), once
  three days of history held enough failures to average out above 0.1%.

**Why two windows.** Each alert also has a **short window**, about a twelfth of the long one: 5
minutes for the 1-hour alert, 30 minutes for the 6-hour one. The alert fires only if the burn
rate is too high over *both*. The long window makes sure enough budget was spent to matter; the
short window makes sure it is still happening. Without the short window, the 1-hour alert would
keep firing for up to an hour after a problem was fixed, because the bad minutes stay inside the
last hour. With it, the alert stops within minutes of the fix.

These numbers come from Google's SRE Workbook (2018, kept as history), and are the defaults in
current tools such as Grafana's SLO feature and Splunk's burn-rate alerts (as of November 2026).
They are a starting point, not a law: a service with little traffic, where one failed request is
1% of an hour, needs looser alerts or time-slice SLOs.

### Using the budget: the error budget policy

A budget nobody acts on is a dashboard. The **error budget policy** is a short document, agreed
between the team that builds features and the people responsible for reliability (often the same
team) *before* anything goes wrong, that says what happens at each level of budget. For example:

1. **More than 50% left:** ship normally.
2. **Less than 50% left:** every release needs a tested way to roll back; risky changes wait.
3. **Budget used up:** stop releasing features, except security fixes and changes that improve
   reliability, until the rolling window is back above 0.
4. **One incident spent more than 20% of the budget on its own:** it gets a written review
   (a postmortem, coming soon) with at least one action that prevents it from happening again.

Two rules make a policy work. It is agreed in advance, so nobody argues about it during an
incident. And it is not a punishment: a team that runs out of budget is not in trouble; it
learned that the service is less reliable than users need, and it now has the time to fix that.

The budget also works the other way. A service that ends every month with 90% of its budget
unspent is probably being too careful: releases could go out faster, or the target is looser than
users need, or both.

## Choosing between them

The choices this page covered, and a starting point for each.

| Choice | Options | Start with |
|---|---|---|
| SLI | availability, latency, freshness, correctness | availability and latency ("succeeded in under N ms"), as one SLI or two |
| Where to measure | inside the service, load balancer, browser | load balancer; browser as a second opinion |
| Target | 99% to 99.99% | where users start to complain, checked against your dependencies; usually 99.5%–99.9% |
| Window | rolling 28/30 days, calendar month | rolling 28 or 30 days; calendar months for reports and SLAs |
| Counting | requests, time slices | requests; time slices for very low traffic |
| Alerts | error-rate thresholds, burn rates | page at 14.4× (1 h + 5 min) and 6× (6 h + 30 min); ticket at 1× (3 days + 6 h) |
| SLA | none, or looser than the SLO | only if customers pay for one; always looser than the SLO |

How to choose: pick one SLI for the one journey users care most about (here, checkout), set a
modest target, add the three burn-rate alerts, and write a one-page budget policy. Review the
target every few months with real data. Adding more SLIs before the first one is trusted only
adds noise.

/** Error rate over the last `window` minutes, divided by the allowed error rate (1 - SLO). */
function burnRate(prefix, end, window) {
  const start = Math.max(0, end - window);
  const errors = prefix[end] - prefix[start];
  const requests = (end - start) * PER_MINUTE;
  return errors / requests / (1 - SLO);
}

// Burn-rate alerts: name, burn rate, long window, short window (minutes).

  ['ticket: 1x over 3 days and 6 h', 1, 3 * DAY, 360],
];

</Tabs>

`prefix` holds a running total of bad requests, one entry per minute, so the failures in any
window are one subtraction: the total at the end minus the total at the start. That keeps each
check fast even over a 3-day window of 4,320 minutes. The first part of the output is the budget
for the month:

```text
Requests this month: 1,080,000. SLO 99.9%: error budget = 1,080 failed requests
  as time: 43,200 minutes x 0.1% = 43.2 minutes of full outage
  background 0.02%:    194 failed ( 18% of budget)
  5-minute blip, 5% errors       :      6 failed (  1% of budget)
  20-minute outage, 100% errors  :    500 failed ( 46% of budget)
  3-day slow leak, 0.3% errors   :    324 failed ( 30% of budget)
  month total: 1,025 failed, SLI = 99.9051%, budget left = 5%
```

In production you would not write this yourself. Teams that use Prometheus, a popular
open-source system for collecting metrics, describe an SLO in a short file and let tools such as
Sloth or Pyrra generate the burn-rate alerts; Grafana, Datadog, Google Cloud and Splunk have SLO features that do the same.

## How real systems do it

**Google Cloud Monitoring** lets you define an SLO on a service as request-based (good requests ÷
total requests) or windows-based (good time slices ÷ all slices), over a rolling or calendar
period, and alert on its burn rate with a dedicated selector. Its documentation recommends
pairing a fast-burn and a slow-burn alert (as of November 2026).

**OpenSLO** is an open, vendor-neutral file format for SLOs. A version 1 SLO names its indicator,
its `objectives` (targets), a `timeWindow` (rolling, such as `28d`, or calendar-aligned) and a
`budgetingMethod`: `Occurrences` (count requests) or `Timeslices` (count good slices of time).

**Grafana** (with Prometheus data) creates SLOs from a good-events query and a total-events
query, and generates multi-window burn-rate alerts with the 14.4×, 6× and 1× tiers above. Grafana
Labs published a walkthrough of exactly this setup in February 2025.

**Amazon EC2's SLA** is a good example of a contract built on a target: 99.99% monthly uptime per
region, with service credits on future bills (not refunds) when uptime falls below it, starting at
10% of that month's EC2 bill in the region (as of November 2026). Most cloud services publish an
SLA like this; read them as the floor of what you can build on, not as your own target.

## Gotchas and war stories

:::caution[An SLO nobody looks at]
The most common failure is a dashboard with an SLO that was set once and never discussed again.
The fix is a routine: look at the budget in a weekly team meeting, and review the target every
few months against real complaints. Google's 2020 report on SLO adoption found the same: SLOs stay
useful only when teams revisit them.
:::

**Low traffic makes every failure huge.** At 10 requests an hour, one failure is 10% of that
hour, and a 1-hour burn-rate alert fires at the first failed request. Use longer windows, time
slices, or add steady synthetic requests (a program that makes a real checkout every minute) so
there is enough traffic to measure.

**Planned maintenance still spends budget.** Users cannot tell a planned outage from an unplanned
one. If the database upgrade takes checkout down for 20 minutes, that is 20 minutes of budget.
This is one more reason to keep budget in reserve, and to make upgrades that do not need downtime.

**Too many SLOs.** Twenty SLIs per service, each with alerts, recreates the noisy pager that
error budgets were meant to replace. Start with one or two per user journey that matters.

:::danger[Setting the SLO to what the service already does]
If the service ran at 99.97% last quarter and you set the SLO to 99.97%, every normal bad day
breaks it, the budget is always empty, and the policy freezes all releases. Set the target from
what users need, with room below today's performance.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

SLOs cost work before they pay back: agreeing on what "good" means, collecting the data where users
meet the service, building alerts, and writing a policy that people actually follow. They also
cost a kind of honesty: a team must accept that some failure is normal and planned for.

They do not replace everything else.

- **Debugging still needs logs and traces.** The SLO says users are hurting; it does not say why.
  The [logging](/observability/logging/) and tracing pages cover finding the cause.
- **Some failures must never happen.** Charging a customer twice, losing an order or leaking data
  are not "within budget" at any rate. Those need correctness checks and safeguards, such as
  [idempotency keys](/messaging/idempotency-keys/), not a percentage.
- **Small, internal tools** used by five people may not need formal SLOs at all. A simple alert
  when they are down, and a conversation when they are slow, is enough.

## Interview corner

SLOs come up in system design interviews when the interviewer asks "how would you know this
system is healthy?" and in SRE interviews directly.

**"Define SLI, SLO and SLA."** A strong answer gives one example of each for the same service,
notes that the SLI is a ratio of good to valid events, and that the SLA is a contract that is
looser than the SLO.

**"What does 99.9% mean in practice?"** 43.2 minutes a month of full outage (43,200 minutes ×
0.001), or 1 bad request in 1,000. Bonus: each extra nine divides that by 10.

**"How would you alert on an SLO?"** Not on a fixed error rate. Alert on burn rate with a long
and a short window: page at 14.4× over 1 hour (2% of a 30-day budget gone in an hour), page at
6× over 6 hours, ticket at 1× over 3 days. Explain what the short window is for.

**"Your service depends on three services at 99.9% each. What can you promise?"** About 99.7%
if any failure fails the request (0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999), unless you add retries, fallbacks or
caching that let you survive their failures.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A service handles 2,000,000 requests in 30 days with an SLO of 99.5%. How many bad requests is the error budget?</summary>

10,000. The budget is 100% − 99.5% = 0.5% of the requests: 2,000,000 × 0.005 = 10,000.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. The SLO is 99.9% over 30 days. Over the last hour, 2% of requests failed. What is the burn rate, and how long would a full budget last at that speed?</summary>

The burn rate is 2% ÷ 0.1% = 20. At 20 times the allowed speed, a 30-day budget lasts
30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 days, 36 hours. The 14.4× page fires.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why is an average response time a poor SLI?</summary>

It hides the slow minority. 98% of requests at 200 ms and 2% at 5 seconds average about 300 ms,
which looks fine while 2 in every 100 customers wait 5 seconds. "The share of requests faster than
1 second" shows 98%, clearly below a 99.9% target.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A burn-rate alert looks only at the last hour, with no short window. A 10-minute outage is fixed at 3:10. Why might the alert still be firing at 3:50?</summary>

The 10 bad minutes are still inside the last hour until 4:10, so the 1-hour burn rate stays high
after the fix. A 5-minute short window drops back to normal by 3:15, and since both windows must
be above the rate, the alert stops.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Your team ends every month with 85% of its error budget unspent. Is that good news?</summary>

Not necessarily. Either the target is looser than users need, or the team is releasing more
slowly and carefully than it has to. The budget is meant to be spent on shipping and experiments;
consistently unspent budget is a sign to move faster or to tighten the target.

</details>

## Recap

- SLI: the share of good events, measured where users meet the service (usually the load
  balancer). Write latency as "the share faster than N ms", never as an average.
- SLO: a target for the SLI over a window, usually 99.5%–99.9% over rolling 28 or 30 days. Not
  100%; what you depend on caps what you can reach.
- Error budget = (1 − SLO) × events: 99.9% of 1,080,000 requests leaves 1,080 bad requests, or
  43.2 minutes a month. Each extra nine divides it by 10.
- Burn rate = recent error rate ÷ (1 − SLO). Page at 14.4× over 1 h + 5 min and at 6× over
  6 h + 30 min; ticket at 1× over 3 days + 6 h. Fixed error-rate thresholds page on blips and miss
  slow leaks.
- An error budget policy, agreed in advance, turns the number into decisions: ship while budget
  remains, fix reliability first when it is gone.
- An SLA is a contract with money attached, always looser than your SLO.
