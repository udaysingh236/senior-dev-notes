## Who this is for

You are in your final year of a CS degree, fresh out of a bootcamp, or a year or two into your
first job. You can write Python or JavaScript, you know roughly what an HTTP request is, and you
have created a database table or two. Then someone says "put a rate limiter in front of it" or
"make that endpoint idempotent", and everyone nods except you.

Every page here is written the way a patient senior engineer would explain it at your desk. We
start with the problem that made people invent the thing, look at what people try first and why
it breaks, then go deep on how it really works. No hand-waving, no assumed background beyond the
basics, and every term is defined the first time it shows up.

## How to read a topic page

Every topic follows the same shape, so after the first one you always know where you are.

<Steps>

1. **The story.** A realistic production moment that makes you feel the problem. It ends with
   the question the page answers.

2. **The problem, properly.** Why the problem exists at all, who it hurts, and the key terms.

3. **The obvious fix, and why it breaks.** What most people try first, and the neighbouring
   ideas it gets confused with.

4. **The core idea.** One everyday analogy, where that analogy stops working, and the overview
   diagram.

5. **How it works.** The longest part: each mechanism step by step, with numbers, diagrams and
   a simulation you can drive. Then a side-by-side comparison and code you can build yourself.

6. **The real world.** How well-known systems do it, the gotchas, and the trade-offs, including
   when not to use it.

7. **Check yourself.** Interview questions, a short quiz with hidden answers, a recap you can
   revise from, and where to go next.

</Steps>

If a page lists topics under **Read first**, those are the ideas it builds on. Skim them if any
feel unfamiliar; the page will still make sense without them, but it will make more sense with
them.
