## The question

When your code uses an AI model through its API (the web address your code sends requests to),
the company running the model, the **provider**, does not bill by words or characters. It bills by
**tokens**. The same sentence can cost five tokens in English and, depending on the model, up to
almost thirty in Hindi. And if you chat with the model long enough, it starts to behave as if it
has forgotten the first things you told it. What is a token, and why do these three things happen?

The short answer: a model cannot read letters. Before your text reaches it, a program called a
**tokenizer** cuts the text into small pieces, called tokens, and swaps each piece for a number
from a fixed list. The list was built from huge amounts of text, mostly English, so English needs
few pieces and many other languages need more. The model can only look at a fixed number of tokens
at once, called its **context window**. When a long chat no longer fits, the app has to leave the
oldest messages out, and the model never sees them again.

## The idea in plain words

We will follow one sentence through the whole page: **"I love reading books."** Later we will put
its Hindi translation, **"मुझे किताबें पढ़ना पसंद है।"**, next to it.

1. **The model only works with numbers.** A language model is a very large calculation that takes
   a list of numbers in and gives numbers out. Letters have to become numbers first.
2. **The tokenizer cuts the text into pieces.** It cuts "I love reading books." into five pieces:
   `I`, `␣love`, `␣reading`, `␣books` and `.`. (The `␣` marks a space. The space before a word
   travels with the word, so `␣love` is one piece, space included.)
3. **Each piece becomes a number.** The tokenizer looks each piece up in its fixed list, its
   **vocabulary**, and writes down the piece's position in that list. OpenAI, the company behind
   ChatGPT, names its models GPT-4, GPT-4o and so on. With the tokenizer that GPT-4o uses, our
   sentence becomes `[40, 3047, 6085, 7187, 13]`. Those five numbers are what the model actually
   receives. Each piece, with its number, is a **token**.
4. **Common words are one piece, rare words are several.** The list holds about 200,000 pieces:
   common words, common word-parts, single letters and symbols. "reading" is common, so it is one
   piece. A word the list has never seen, such as "rereading", is built from smaller pieces that
   are on it: `␣rere` + `ading`. Nothing is ever unreadable; rare text costs more pieces.
5. **The model answers in tokens too.** It writes its answer one token at a time, and the
   tokenizer turns those numbers back into text for you.
6. **You pay per token.** An API bill counts the tokens you sent in and the tokens the model wrote
   out. Our sentence costs 5 tokens to send.
7. **Other languages can cost more.** The list was built mostly from English text, so English words
   got their own pieces. The Hindi sentence, which means the same thing, needs 29 pieces with the
   tokenizer of GPT-4 (2023) and 9 with the newer one of GPT-4o (2024). Same meaning, more tokens,
   bigger bill.
8. **The model can look at only so many tokens at once.** That limit is the **context window**:
   everything in one request, meaning your instructions, the whole chat so far, your new message,
   and the answer being written, must fit inside it.
9. **The model remembers nothing between requests.** A chat app sends the whole conversation again
   with every new message. When the chat grows past the window, the app must drop or shorten the
   oldest messages, and the model can no longer see them. That is the "forgetting".

Look at the picture once more: text goes in at the top, numbers come out in the middle, and the
model at the bottom works only inside its window. Each of the three surprises from the question
lives in one of these boxes. The bill counts the pieces, the language changes how many pieces a
sentence needs, and the window decides how many pieces can be in view at once.

An everyday picture: think of the vocabulary as a box of printing stamps. A printer who prints
mostly English has a stamp for every common English word, so "reading" is one stamp. For a word
with no stamp, they set it from smaller stamps, a few letters at a time. A Hindi sentence printed
with that English-heavy box takes many more stamps. The picture stops working in one place: real
tokenizers do not choose stamps by hand. A program picks them by counting which pieces appear most
often in huge amounts of text, and the byte-pair encoding section further down shows exactly how.

## The building blocks

Four ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, an
example with numbers, and then the technical version.

### Token

**What it is.** A **token** is one piece of text, the smallest unit a language model reads or
writes.

**Why it exists.** A model needs every input to be a number from a fixed, limited set. Whole words
are too many to list (and new ones appear every day), while single letters make texts very long.
Pieces of words sit in between: a limited set that can still spell anything.

**Example.** "I love reading books." is 21 characters, 4 words plus a full stop, and 5 tokens.
Here is how they line up:

| Piece | `I` | `␣love` | `␣reading` | `␣books` | `.` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Token ID (GPT-4o) | 40 | 3047 | 6085 | 7187 | 13 |

The thing to notice: the space belongs to the word after it, and the full stop is a token of its
own.

**The technical version.** A token is an integer ID into the tokenizer's vocabulary. The model
never sees text, only the sequence of IDs. A rule of thumb from OpenAI and Google: in English, one
token is about 4 characters, or about three quarters of a word (as of November 2026, [OpenAI
help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/4936856),
[Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/tokens)). Turned around: if a token is three
quarters of a word, a word is 1 ÷ 0.75 ≈ 1.33 tokens, so 100 English words come to roughly
100 × 1.33 ≈ 133 tokens. The rule is for English only; later sections show why.

### Tokenizer and vocabulary

**What it is.** A **tokenizer** is the program that turns text into token IDs and back, and its
**vocabulary** is the fixed list of every piece it knows.

**Why it exists.** The model and the app must agree, exactly, on which number means which piece.
The tokenizer is that agreement, written down once and shipped with the model.

**Example.** OpenAI publishes its tokenizers in a library called tiktoken. The one used by GPT-4
and GPT-3.5 is called `cl100k_base` and has 100,277 pieces; the one used by GPT-4o and later
models is `o200k_base` and has 200,019 pieces (counted in this page's research, November 2026).
Turning text into IDs is **encoding**; turning IDs back into text is **decoding**, and it gives
back exactly the original text, every space included.

**The technical version.** Each model maker builds its own tokenizer from its own collection of
text, so the same text gives different tokens and different counts on different models. A model
can only be used with the tokenizer it was trained with, because during training it learned what
each ID means: ID 3047 means `␣love` to GPT-4o, and something else entirely to any other model.

### Context window

**What it is.** The **context window** is the largest number of tokens a model can look at in one
request.

**Why it exists.** For every token it writes, the model looks back over every token in the
request, and it keeps working notes in memory for each of them. Both the work and the memory grow
with the number of tokens, so every model has a ceiling it was built and tested for. The section
on why the window has a limit shows the numbers.

**Example.** Imagine a chat model with an 8,000-token window. (The first Llama 3 models from Meta,
in April 2024, had 8,192.) The app's instructions to the model take 500 tokens. The app keeps
1,000 tokens free for the answer. Each exchange is a 200-token question and its 200-token answer,
so 400 tokens.

- Room left for the chat: 8,000 − 500 − 1,000 = 6,500 tokens.
- After 16 exchanges, the history holds 16 × 400 = 6,400 tokens, still under the 6,500 of room.
- The 17th question adds 200 more: 6,400 + 200 = 6,600 tokens, which is 100 more than the 6,500 of
  room. It does not fit.

At that moment the app has to choose: refuse, or leave out the oldest exchange. Leaving out
exchange 1 frees 400 tokens: 15 exchanges of history (6,000 tokens) plus the new question
(200) make 6,200, and the request fits again. From now on the model never sees what you said first.

<Figure caption="An 8,000-token window as a fixed bar. Instructions and room for the answer always take their share; the chat fills the rest. At question 17 the chat no longer fits, and the oldest exchange has to go.">
  <WindowBudget />
</Figure>

**The technical version.** The window counts the input (system instructions, every message, tool
descriptions, meaning the descriptions of functions your app lets the model call, documents and
images, all as tokens) plus the output the model generates in that
request. As of November 2026, Anthropic's current Claude models have a 1,000,000-token window and
can write up to 128,000 tokens in one answer ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows));
many open models (models whose files anyone can download and run, such as Meta's Llama) have
128,000.

### Input tokens and output tokens

**What it is.** **Input tokens** are the tokens you send in a request; **output tokens** are the
tokens the model writes back.

**Why it exists.** The two cost the provider very different amounts of work. Running the model
once over its input, called a **pass**, is billions of multiplications on expensive chips. All
input tokens are read together in one pass. Output tokens are written one at a time, and each one
needs a pass of its own. So providers count the two separately and charge more for output.

**Example.** With illustrative round prices of $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million
output tokens, a request that sends 2,000 tokens and gets back 500 costs:

- input: 2,000 × $3 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.006
- output: 500 × $15 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.0075
- total: $0.006 + $0.0075 = $0.0135, about one and a half US cents.

The output was a quarter of the size of the input, yet it cost more. Real prices change often and
differ by model, so check the provider's price page; the shape, output costing several times
input, is common across providers.

**The technical version.** The API response reports both counts, for example `usage.input_tokens`
and `usage.output_tokens`. A request also sets `max_tokens`, the most output tokens it allows; if
the model reaches that number, it stops mid-sentence and says so in a field of the response called
the **stop reason**. Some newer models write hidden working notes before they answer, called
**reasoning tokens**. These are billed as output and take up window space even though you never see them ([OpenAI reasoning guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning)).
Why writing is slower than reading is the subject of
[how an LLM writes, one token at a time](/ai-engineering/how-llms-generate-text/).

:::tip[If you stop here]
- A model never sees text. A tokenizer cuts text into pieces called tokens and replaces each with
  a number from a fixed list, its vocabulary.
- Common words are one token; rare words are split into several. In English, a token is about
  4 characters, but other languages often need more tokens for the same meaning.
- You pay per token: tokens in, and tokens out, which cost more.
- The context window is how many tokens one request can hold: instructions, the whole chat, and
  the answer, together.
- The model remembers nothing between requests. Apps resend the chat each time, and when it no
  longer fits they drop or shorten the oldest part. That is why long chats "forget" their start.
:::

## Going deeper

Here is the question for readers who already use these APIs: **if the vocabulary is a fixed list,
how can a tokenizer handle any text at all, an emoji, a new product name, a language it barely saw,
and never fail? And if a model accepts a million tokens, why does it still miss a fact you put on
page 400?**

The first half has a precise answer, and the price of that answer is what makes Hindi cost more.
The second half is about what the window costs: memory and work that grow with every token, bills
that grow faster than the chat, and models that use the middle of a long input less well than its
start and end.

These are not curiosities. Token counts decide your bill, your waiting time (more input tokens take
longer to read) and whether a request is accepted at all. A team that budgets a feature at
"about 4 characters per token" and then launches in Hindi, or moves to a model with a different
tokenizer, finds its numbers off by a factor of two or more.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

If you were designing a tokenizer from scratch, two ideas come first.

**Try 1: one token per word.** Make a list of words, give each an ID, and look words up. It works
until a word is missing from the list. Then the only thing you can do is replace it with a special
"unknown" ID, and the model loses that word completely. "I love rereading books." becomes
"I love [unknown] books." A whole Hindi sentence becomes five unknowns. Making the list bigger does
not end the problem: every name, typo, new product and new slang word is another missing entry,
and every word in every language would need its own row.

**Try 2: one token per character.** Nothing is ever unknown, because every text is made of
characters. But the texts get long: "I love reading books." becomes 21 tokens instead of 5. The
model must now do the work of following 21 positions to understand 4 words, and since the window
counts tokens, it holds about a quarter as much text (21 tokens instead of 5 is 21 ÷ 5 ≈ 4 times
as many).

The fix every modern model uses sits between the two: **sub-word tokens**. Start from the
smallest possible pieces so nothing is ever unknown, then learn which pieces appear together so
often that they deserve a single token. The rest of this section is how that learning works.

## How it works, piece by piece

### Bytes: the pieces everything is made of

**What it is.** A **byte** is a small number from 0 to 255, and every file, every string, every
character in a computer is stored as a row of bytes.

**Why it exists here.** A tokenizer that never fails needs a starting set of pieces that can spell
any text in any language, emoji included. Characters will not do: Unicode, the standard list of
characters, has over 150,000 of them and grows every year. Bytes are a set of exactly 256 values,
and every text is made of them.

**Example.** A byte is 8 bits (each bit a 0 or a 1), and 8 bits can be arranged in
2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 256 different ways, hence 0 to 255. We usually write bytes in
**hexadecimal** (hex), base 16, where the digits run 0–9 and then a–f for 10 to 15, so every byte
is exactly two hex digits. The left digit counts sixteens and the right one counts ones: `61` is
6 × 16 + 1 = 97, and `ff` is 15 × 16 + 15 = 255. The way text is turned into bytes is called
**UTF-8**, and it uses a different number of bytes for different characters:

<Figure caption="UTF-8 stores the letter a in one byte, the Hindi letter म in three, and an emoji in four. A tokenizer that starts from bytes can read any text, but a Hindi sentence starts out several times as long in bytes as an English one.">
  <Utf8Bytes />
</Figure>

The English sentence "I love reading books." is 21 characters and 21 bytes, because every English
letter, digit and space takes one byte. The Hindi sentence is 27 characters: Hindi stores each vowel sign, such as the ु in मु, as a
character of its own, so the count is higher than the letters you see. 4 of the 27 are spaces. The other 23 take three bytes each: 23 × 3 = 69 bytes, plus 4 bytes for the spaces, gives
73 bytes. Before any learning happens, the Hindi sentence is already 73 ÷ 21 ≈ 3.5 times as long.

**The technical version.** UTF-8 uses 1 byte for the first 128 characters (English letters,
digits, basic punctuation), 2 bytes for many European and Middle Eastern scripts, 3 bytes for most
Indian scripts, Chinese and Japanese, and 4 bytes for emoji. A **byte-level** tokenizer starts with
a vocabulary of the 256 single bytes. Any text can be written as bytes, so any text can be
tokenized, and decoding the bytes gives back the exact text. That is the first half of the answer
to the question that opened "Going deeper": nothing is ever unknown because the floor is bytes.

### Byte-pair encoding, step by step

**What it is.** **Byte-pair encoding (BPE)** builds the vocabulary by starting from single bytes
and repeatedly gluing together the two neighbouring pieces that appear together most often.

**Why it exists.** We want frequent text to be few tokens and rare text to still be spellable. BPE
gets both from one rule: whatever appears together most in the training text gets its own token
first.

**Example.** Train on a tiny made-up **corpus** (the collection of training text): the word "read" appearing 6 times, "reads" 3 times,
"reading" 4 times, "bread" 2 times and "red" 3 times. Every word starts as single letters (in
English, one letter is one byte).

1. Count every pair of neighbours across the corpus. The pair `r` `e` appears in all five words:
   6 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 18 times, more than any other pair. **Merge 1:** `r` + `e` → `re`.
2. Count again with `re` as one piece. Now `re` + `a` appears in read, reads, reading and bread:
   6 + 3 + 4 + 2 = 15 times. So does `a` + `d`. When two pairs tie, our code takes the one it
   counted first while reading the corpus in order, which here is `re` + `a`. **Merge 2:**
   `re` + `a` → `rea`.
3. `rea` + `d` also appears 15 times. **Merge 3:** `rea` + `d` → `read`.
4. Only "reading" has more after `read`: `read` + `i` appears 4 times, as do `i` + `n` and
   `n` + `g`. The tie rule picks the first counted. **Merge 4:** `read` + `i` → `readi`.
5. **Merge 5:** `readi` + `n` → `readin`.

A byte-level vocabulary always starts with all 256 possible bytes, even the ones this tiny corpus
never uses, so that any text can still be spelled. With the 5 new pieces it now has
256 + 5 = 261 entries. The list of
merges, in order, is the tokenizer.

<Figure caption="The five merges applied to the word reading: it shrinks from 7 single-letter tokens to 2, readin and g (five merges were not enough to join the g). A new word like thread splits into t, h and the learned piece read.">
  <MergeLadder />
</Figure>

To **encode** new text, the tokenizer starts from its bytes and replays the merges in the order
they were learned. "bread" becomes `b` + `read`: 5 bytes, 2 tokens. "ready", never seen in
training, becomes `read` + `y`. "thread" becomes `t` + `h` + `read`. The Hindi word "पढ़ना"
(reading) is 5 characters of 3 bytes each, 5 × 3 = 15 bytes, and none of the merges touch it, so it stays 15 tokens of one byte each,
none of them even a whole letter.

**The technical version.** Here is the trainer and encoder, about forty lines in each language. It
prints the five merges above.

```python title="bpe.py"
from collections import Counter

CORPUS = {"read": 6, "reads": 3, "reading": 4, "bread": 2, "red": 3}


def train(word_counts: dict[str, int], num_merges: int) -> list[tuple[bytes, bytes]]:
    """Learn merges: repeatedly join the most frequent pair of neighbouring pieces."""
    words = {w: [bytes([b]) for b in w.encode("utf-8")] for w in word_counts}
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs: Counter[tuple[bytes, bytes]] = Counter()
        for w, pieces in words.items():
            for a, b in zip(pieces, pieces[1:]):
                pairs[(a, b)] += word_counts[w]  # once per time the word appears
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)  # ties: the pair seen first wins
        merges.append(best)
        words = {w: merge_pair(p, best) for w, p in words.items()}
    return merges


def merge_pair(pieces: list[bytes], pair: tuple[bytes, bytes]) -> list[bytes]:
    """Replace every neighbouring (a, b) in pieces with the single piece a + b."""
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(pieces):
        if i + 1 < len(pieces) and (pieces[i], pieces[i + 1]) == pair:
            out.append(pieces[i] + pieces[i + 1])
            i += 2
        else:
            out.append(pieces[i])
            i += 1
    return out


def encode(word: str, merges: list[tuple[bytes, bytes]]) -> list[bytes]:
    """Start from bytes, replay the merges in the order they were learned."""
    pieces = [bytes([b]) for b in word.encode("utf-8")]
    for pair in merges:
        pieces = merge_pair(pieces, pair)
    return pieces

# merge 1: 'r' + 'e' -> 're'  (seen 18 times)  ... merge 5: 'readi' + 'n' -> 'readin'
# 'thread': 6 bytes -> 3 tokens ['t', 'h', 'read']
# 'पढ़ना': 15 bytes -> 15 tokens ['<e0>', '<a4>', '<aa>', ...]
```

```js title="bpe.mjs"
const CORPUS = { read: 6, reads: 3, reading: 4, bread: 2, red: 3 };
const enc = new TextEncoder();
const key = (bytes) => bytes.join(','); // compare byte arrays by value

/** Learn merges: repeatedly join the most frequent pair of neighbouring pieces. */
function train(wordCounts, numMerges) {
  let words = Object.keys(wordCounts).map((w) => [w, [...enc.encode(w)].map((b) => [b])]);
  const merges = [];
  for (let m = 0; m < numMerges; m++) {
    const pairs = new Map(); // in the order pairs are first seen
    for (const [w, pieces] of words) {
      for (let i = 0; i + 1 < pieces.length; i++) {
        const k = key(pieces[i]) + '|' + key(pieces[i + 1]);
        const entry = pairs.get(k) ?? { pair: [pieces[i], pieces[i + 1]], count: 0 };
        entry.count += wordCounts[w]; // once per time the word appears
        pairs.set(k, entry);
      }
    }
    if (pairs.size === 0) break;
    let best = null;
    for (const e of pairs.values()) if (!best || e.count > best.count) best = e; // ties: first seen
    merges.push(best.pair);
    words = words.map(([w, p]) => [w, mergePair(p, best.pair)]);
  }
  return merges;
}

/** Replace every neighbouring (a, b) in pieces with the single piece a + b. */
function mergePair(pieces, [a, b]) {
  const out = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < pieces.length; i++) {
    if (i + 1 < pieces.length && key(pieces[i]) === key(a) && key(pieces[i + 1]) === key(b)) {
      out.push([...pieces[i], ...pieces[i + 1]]);
      i++;
    } else out.push(pieces[i]);
  }
  return out;
}

/** Start from bytes, replay the merges in the order they were learned. */
function encode(word, merges) {
  let pieces = [...enc.encode(word)].map((b) => [b]);
  for (const pair of merges) pieces = mergePair(pieces, pair);
  return pieces;
}

// merge 1: 'r' + 'e' -> 're'  (seen 18 times)  ... merge 5: 'readi' + 'n' -> 'readin'
// 'thread': 6 bytes -> 3 tokens ['t', 'h', 'read']
// 'पढ़ना': 15 bytes -> 15 tokens ['<e0>', '<a4>', '<aa>', ...]
```
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

A production tokenizer is this same loop with three differences. It trains on terabytes of text (a terabyte is a million million bytes)
instead of five words. It learns 100,000 to 200,000 merges instead of five. And before BPE runs,
it splits the text into rough chunks with a fixed pattern (words with their leading space,
numbers, runs of punctuation), so a token never crosses from one word into the next. That
pre-splitting is why `␣love` carries its space, and why, in OpenAI's tokenizers, numbers are cut
into groups of at most three digits: `12345678` becomes `123` + `456` + `78`
([tiktoken](https://github.com/openai/tiktoken)). Real encoders also replay merges far faster than
our loop, by always applying the earliest-learned merge that fits, but the result is the same idea.

### Why some languages cost more tokens

Now we can explain the Hindi numbers from the start of the page. Two things stack up.

**First, Hindi starts longer.** As we worked out above, our Hindi sentence is 73 bytes and the
English one is 21. Before any merge, Hindi is about 3.5 times as long.

**Second, the merges are shared out by frequency.** BPE spends each merge on the most frequent pair
in the training text. If most of that text is English, most merges go to English pairs, and
English words end up as single tokens. A language that is a small share of the training text gets
fewer merges, so its words stay in smaller pieces, sometimes in pieces smaller than one letter.

The gap is not only a cost problem. A 2023 study measured the same texts translated into many
languages and found token counts differing by up to 15 times between languages, with three
effects: users of those languages pay more, wait longer for answers, and fit less text in the same
context window ([Petrov et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15425)). An Indian app
that budgets its context window for English and then serves Tamil or Telugu users runs out of room
several times sooner.

### Messages, special tokens and everything else that counts

A chat request is not only your text. The API wraps every message with **special tokens**: reserved
IDs, never produced by encoding ordinary text, that mark where a message starts and ends and who
wrote it. Each message therefore costs a few tokens on top of its text. Tool descriptions (the
lists of functions the model may call) are turned into tokens too, and so are images and documents
you attach ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows)).
This is why a count of your text alone always comes out lower than the `input_tokens` on the bill.

For exact numbers, use the counter that matches the model. For OpenAI models, tiktoken counts
locally: `tiktoken.get_encoding("o200k_base").encode(text)` returns the list of IDs. Anthropic
offers a free endpoint, `POST /v1/messages/count_tokens`, which takes the same body as a real
request (system prompt, messages, tools, images) and returns the input count, described in its
docs as an estimate; Gemini has a similar count-tokens call
([Anthropic token counting](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/token-counting)).
For a quick check without calling anything, our example code uses UTF-8 bytes ÷ 4, the average
of about 4 bytes per token that the tiktoken README reports, rounded up. For the English sentence,
21 ÷ 4 = 5.25, rounded up to 6 (real: 5). For the Hindi one, 73 ÷ 4 = 18.25, rounded up to 19
(real: 9 on o200k, 29 on cl100k), which tells you how rough "a quick check" is.

### What happens at the edge of the window

Now the second half of the question from "Going deeper": the window. Three things happen as a request
approaches it: the provider enforces it, the app has to make room, and the bill grows faster than
the chat.

**What the API does.** The window is input plus output. If the input alone is larger than the
window, the request is rejected before the model runs; Claude's API, for example, returns HTTP 400
with "prompt is too long". If the input fits but the answer runs out of room, the model stops
writing and the response says why: on recent Claude models the stop reason is
`model_context_window_exceeded`, and when the answer hits your own `max_tokens` setting first, it
is `max_tokens` (as of November 2026, [Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows)).
Either way, you get back half an answer. Code that does not check the stop reason will treat half
a JSON object as a whole one.

**What the app does.** Since the app builds every request itself, it decides what each one
contains. When a chat outgrows the window, there are four common choices:

1. **Refuse.** Tell the user the conversation is too long and start a new one. Honest, but abrupt.
2. **Drop the oldest messages.** Keep the instructions, keep as many recent messages as fit, leave
   the rest out. Cheap and predictable. This is the "forgetting" from the opening question.
3. **Summarise the oldest messages.** Ask a model to write a short summary of the old part, and
   send the summary instead. Anthropic calls this **compaction** and uses it in its own coding
   agent (a program where the model works in a loop, calling tools such as file reading): near the limit, the history is summarised, keeping decisions and open problems and
   dropping long tool outputs, and the work continues in a fresh window
   ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)).
   The summary loses detail, and writing it costs an extra request.
4. **Fetch only what is needed.** Keep the full history or documents outside the model, in a
   database, and pull in the few relevant pieces for each question. That is called **retrieval**, covered in
   [long context vs RAG](/ai-engineering/long-context-vs-rag/).

Whatever you choose, two rules hold. Never drop the instructions: they are what makes your app
behave like your app. And always reserve room for the answer, plus room for hidden reasoning on
models that think; OpenAI suggests reserving at least 25,000 tokens for reasoning and output when
you start with its reasoning models ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning)).

**What the bill does.** Because the app resends the whole chat every time, each new question costs
more input tokens than the last. Take the chat from the context window example: 500 tokens of instructions, exchanges of
200 tokens of question and 200 of answer.

- Question 1 sends the instructions and the question: 500 + 200 = 700 input tokens.
- Question 2 also resends exchange 1: 500 + 400 + 200 = 1,100.
- Each question after that sends 400 more than the one before. Question 16 comes 15 questions
  after question 1, so it sends 700 + 15 × 400 = 6,700.

Add up all sixteen requests. Pair the first with the last: 700 + 6,700 = 7,400. Pair the second
with the second-to-last: 1,100 + 6,300 = 7,400. Every pair adds up to 7,400, so the average
request is 7,400 ÷ 2 = 3,700. Sixteen requests at an average of 3,700 make
16 × 3,700 = 59,200 input tokens billed. The conversation itself is only 16 × 400 = 6,400 tokens. You paid for it more than nine times over (59,200 ÷ 6,400 ≈ 9.25), and the
longer the chat, the bigger that multiple gets. [Prompt caching](/ai-engineering/prompt-caching/)
exists largely because of this pattern.

Here is the "drop oldest" strategy written properly: keep the instructions, reserve room for the
answer, walk back from the newest message, never cut a message in half, and make sure the kept
history starts with a question. It takes the token counter as a parameter, so production code can
pass the provider's real counter.

```python title="fit_history.py"
import math
from typing import Callable


def estimate_tokens(text: str) -> int:
    """Rough count: about 4 bytes of UTF-8 per token. Use the provider's counter when it matters."""
    return math.ceil(len(text.encode("utf-8")) / 4)


def fit_history(system: str, messages: list[dict], window: int, reserve_output: int,
                count: Callable[[str], int] = estimate_tokens) -> tuple[list[dict], int]:
    """Return (the newest messages that fit, tokens used by system + kept messages)."""
    budget = window - reserve_output - count(system)
    newest = count(messages[-1]["text"])
    if newest > budget:
        # Never cut the user's new message silently: tell the caller.
        raise ValueError(f"new message needs {newest} tokens, only {budget} available")
    kept, used = [], 0
    for msg in reversed(messages):           # walk from newest to oldest
        cost = count(msg["text"])
        if used + cost > budget:
            break                             # this message and everything older is dropped
        kept.append(msg)
        used += cost
    kept.reverse()
    while kept and kept[0]["role"] != "user":  # a history should start with a question
        used -= count(kept[0]["text"])
        kept.pop(0)
    return kept, count(system) + used

# 20 exchanges of 200 + 200 tokens and a new 200-token question:
# messages in chat: 41, kept: 31, dropped: 10
# oldest kept message starts with: q6
# tokens sent: 6700, room left for the answer: 1300
```

```js title="fit-history.mjs"
/** Rough count: about 4 bytes of UTF-8 per token. Use the provider's counter when it matters. */
export const estimateTokens = (text) => Math.ceil(new TextEncoder().encode(text).length / 4);

/** Returns { kept: the newest messages that fit, used: tokens for system + kept messages }. */
export function fitHistory(system, messages, window, reserveOutput, count = estimateTokens) {
  const budget = window - reserveOutput - count(system);
  const newest = count(messages.at(-1).text);
  if (newest > budget) {
    // Never cut the user's new message silently: tell the caller.
    throw new Error(`new message needs ${newest} tokens, only ${budget} available`);
  }
  const kept = [];
  let used = 0;
  for (const msg of [...messages].reverse()) { // walk from newest to oldest
    const cost = count(msg.text);
    if (used + cost > budget) break;            // this message and everything older is dropped
    kept.unshift(msg);
    used += cost;
  }
  while (kept.length && kept[0].role !== 'user') used -= count(kept.shift().text); // start with a question
  return { kept, used: count(system) + used };
}
```

### Why the window has a limit, and why the middle gets lost

The building blocks said every token costs memory and work. Here are the numbers, and then the
part that surprises people: fitting in the window does not mean being used well.

**Memory: the model's notes per token.** When the model reads a token, it saves a set of numbers
about it, so that later tokens can look back at it without reading everything again. These saved
numbers are called the **key-value cache** (KV cache). They exist because without them, writing
each new token would mean re-reading the entire input from scratch. How big are they? For Meta's
Llama 3 8B model (8B means 8 billion learned numbers), the published design is a stack of 32
**layers**, rounds of calculation that each token passes through in turn. Each layer saves 8 groups of 128 numbers
twice per token (once as a "key", once as a "value"), each number taking 2 bytes
([Llama 3 paper, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)). Step by step:

- numbers saved per token: 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 65,536
- bytes per token: 65,536 × 2 bytes = 131,072 bytes, which is 128 KB (1 KB is 1,024 bytes)
- for an 8,192-token window: 8,192 × 128 KB = 1,048,576 KB, which is 1 GB (1,024 KB is 1 MB, and
  1,024 MB is 1 GB)
- for Llama 3.1's 131,072-token window (written "128K"): 131,072 ÷ 8,192 = 16 times as many
  tokens, so 16 × 1 GB = 16 GB

The model itself, its 8 billion learned numbers (called **weights**) at 2 bytes each, takes
8,000,000,000 × 2 = 16,000,000,000 bytes, about 15 GB counted the 1,024 way as above: close to
the 16 GB of notes. Both have to sit in the memory of
the graphics chip (**GPU**) that runs the model, which is fast but small and expensive. So one
conversation at full length needs about as much of that memory for its notes as the whole model does, and a server
running many conversations at once has to share that memory between them. How the KV cache is
used during writing is covered in [how an LLM writes](/ai-engineering/how-llms-generate-text/).

**Work: every token looks at every other token.** The basic design of these models, the
transformer, lets each token look at each earlier token to decide what matters. A quick refresher
on squaring first: a number "squared", written n², is the number times itself, so 4² = 4 × 4 = 16.
With n tokens, there are about n × n = n² pairs of tokens to compare
([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)).

<Figure caption="Each square is one pair of tokens the model compares. 4 tokens make 16 pairs; doubling to 8 tokens makes 64, four times as many. The work grows much faster than the text.">
  <PairGrid />
</Figure>

Doubling the input from 4 tokens to 8 doubles the text but makes 8 × 8 = 64 pairs instead of 16,
four times the work. At 1,000 tokens it is 1,000 × 1,000 = one million pairs. Real models use many
engineering tricks to keep this affordable, but the pressure is real, and it is one reason long
inputs are slow, and why some providers charge more per token for very long prompts. The details belong to
[transformers and attention](/ai-engineering/transformers-and-attention/).

**Quality: the middle gets lost.** A 2023 study gave models 10, 20 or 30 documents with only one of
them holding the answer, and moved that one document around. Models answered best when the useful document was at the start or
the end of the input, and noticeably worse when it was in the middle; accuracy also fell as inputs
got longer ([Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle", TACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/)).
In July 2025, Chroma tested 18 models on tasks that stayed equally hard while the input grew, and
found their answers became less reliable as the input got longer, even on simple tasks and well
before the window was full ([Chroma, "Context Rot"](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)). Anthropic's own docs now
use that name, **context rot**: as the token count grows, accuracy and recall (finding facts that are in the input) fall.

The practical rule that follows: the window is a hard limit, not a target. Put the instructions
and the most important material at the start or the end, send what the task needs rather than
everything you have, and test quality at the input sizes you will really use.

## How real systems do it

**OpenAI** publishes its tokenizers in tiktoken: `cl100k_base` for GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, and
`o200k_base`, which arrived with GPT-4o in May 2024, for the models since.
Counting locally with the right encoding gives exact text counts; the chat format adds a few tokens
per message on top. Reasoning models add hidden reasoning tokens, billed as output.

**Meta's Llama 3** (April 2024) moved from a 32,000-token vocabulary in Llama 2 to 128,000: about
100,000 pieces taken from OpenAI's tiktoken vocabulary plus 28,000 more for other languages. Meta
reports roughly 15% fewer tokens for the same text (15% fewer means 100 tokens become 85) and English compression rising from about 3.2 to
4 characters per token. The first models had an 8,192-token window; Llama 3.1, three months
later, had 128,000 ([Llama 3 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)). The vocabulary trade is
visible here: a bigger list means fewer tokens per sentence, at the cost of a bigger table inside
the model, which holds one row of learned numbers for every vocabulary entry.

**Anthropic's Claude** models have the 1,000,000-token window met earlier. Claude Opus 4.7 and later use a newer tokenizer that produces roughly 30%
more tokens for the same text than earlier Claude models; 30% more means 1,000 tokens become 1,300.
The docs translate the window: 1M tokens is about 555,000 English words on the new tokenizer,
against about 750,000 before (750,000 ÷ 555,000 ≈ 1.35, in line with "roughly 30%"). The count-tokens endpoint is free, and long conversations are handled
with server-side compaction ([Claude models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview),
[token counting](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/token-counting)).

**Google's Gemini** documents the same rule of thumb, about 4 characters per token and 60–80
English words per 100 tokens, offers a count-tokens call, and reports the real counts in each
response's usage data ([Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/tokens)).

The pattern across all four: each provider has its own tokenizer, counts change between model
versions, and every API reports the real counts per request. Log those reported counts; do not
rely on your own estimate for anything that matters.

## Gotchas and war stories

**The model cannot see letters.** Ask a model how many r's are in "strawberry", and in the question
"How many r letters are in strawberry?" the word arrives as one token, `␣strawberry`, in both of
OpenAI's tokenizers. The model is being asked about the letters inside a number. Numbers, as we
saw, arrive in groups of up to three digits. Counting letters, reversing strings and long arithmetic are weak spots for this reason; give the model a tool (code)
for them.

:::caution[A token budget is only valid for one tokenizer]
Limits written as tokens ("trim history to 6,500 tokens", "chunks of 500 tokens") silently change
meaning when you change model. Moving to a Claude model with the newer tokenizer turns the same text
into about 30% more tokens, so a prompt that used 80% of a budget now needs 80 × 1.3 = 104%. Recount
with the new model's counter before switching, and keep a margin.
:::

**Estimates break outside English.** Our bytes ÷ 4 estimate said 19 tokens for the Hindi sentence;
the real count is 9 on one OpenAI tokenizer and 29 on the other. A characters ÷ 4 estimate would say
27 ÷ 4 = 6.75, about 7, and against cl100k's 29 that is 29 ÷ 7 ≈ 4 times too low. If your users write in Hindi, Tamil, Arabic or Chinese, count
with the real tokenizer, and log the reported usage per language.

:::danger[Check the stop reason]
When the answer hits `max_tokens` or the window, the API still returns success, with a truncated
answer and a stop reason saying so. Code that parses the text without checking the stop reason
stores half a JSON object, half a SQL query or half a summary as if it were complete. Always check
it, and either retry with more room or fail loudly.
:::

**Glitch tokens.** In February 2023, researchers found tokens such as `␣SolidGoldMagikarp`, a
Reddit username, that made GPT-3 models produce nonsense or refuse to repeat them. The tokenizer had
learned them from text where they were frequent, but the model's training data barely contained
them, so the model never learned what they meant
([Rumbelow and Watkins, 2023](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aPeJE8bSo6rAFoLqg/solidgoldmagikarp-plus-prompt-generation)).
A 2024 study found such under-trained tokens in many open models and showed how to detect them from
the model's weights ([Land and Bartolo, EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.649)).
The lesson for engineers: the tokenizer and the model are trained separately, and odd behaviour on
one strange string can be a tokenizer issue, not a prompt issue.

**Agents fill the window with tool output.** An agent, a program where the model calls tools in a
loop, that reads files and runs commands adds every
result to its history. One large file or log can take tens of thousands of tokens and push the
instructions and the original task toward the lost middle. Trim tool outputs to what is needed, and
clear old ones; Anthropic describes clearing old tool results as the lightest form of compaction
([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)).

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**Vocabulary size is a trade.** A bigger vocabulary makes texts shorter (cheaper, faster, more fits
in the window) and treats more languages fairly. But every entry needs its own row of numbers in
the model, and rare entries are seen less often in training, which is how glitch tokens happen.
The field has moved from about 32,000 to 100,000–200,000 and more since 2023, which tells you which
side has been winning.

**A bigger window is not free.** Every extra token costs money, memory and time to read, and long
inputs are used less reliably. A large window is the right tool when the task truly needs to see a
whole document at once: reviewing a contract, finding a bug that spans files. It is the wrong tool
when the answer depends on a few pages out of thousands; then retrieval sends those pages only.

**When not to stuff the window.** Do not send the full chat history, every document and every tool
result because it fits. Send what this request needs, summarise or drop the rest, and keep the
instructions where the model uses them best, at the start. Choosing what goes into each request is
the subject of [prompt chaining and context engineering](/ai-engineering/prompt-chaining-and-context-engineering/).

## Interview corner

This topic shows up in AI-engineering interviews as warm-up questions and inside system design
("design a chat assistant", "design a document Q&A bot").

- **"What is a token, and why not use words?"** A strong answer: a sub-word piece with an ID from a
  fixed vocabulary; words fail on anything not on the list, characters make inputs several times
  longer; byte-level BPE gives both coverage and short sequences.
- **"How does BPE work?"** Start from bytes, count neighbouring pairs, merge the most frequent,
  repeat until the vocabulary is the size you want; encode by replaying merges in order. Mention
  pre-splitting on words and numbers.
- **"Your chatbot forgets things after a long chat. Why, and what would you do?"** The model is
  memoryless between requests; the app resends history; past the window something must go. Options: drop oldest,
  summarise (compaction), retrieve relevant past messages; always keep the instructions and reserve
  room for the answer; check stop reasons.
- **"Why is the same feature more expensive for Hindi users?"** Bytes per character plus merge
  allocation by training-data frequency; numbers for one sentence; fix by measuring per-language
  usage and choosing models with better multilingual tokenizers.
- **"Why not always use the 1M-token window?"** Cost grows with every token and is paid again on
  every turn, memory per token, latency, and lost-in-the-middle and context rot.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A sentence of 120 English words: about how many tokens, and why only "about"?</summary>

At about three quarters of a word per token, 120 ÷ 0.75 = 160 tokens. Only "about" because the rule
is an average for English. The real count depends on the model's tokenizer, on how common the words
are, and on numbers and punctuation. Count with the model's own tokenizer when it matters.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can a byte-level BPE tokenizer read any text, even an emoji it has never seen?</summary>

Its vocabulary starts with all 256 bytes, and every text in every language is stored as bytes in
UTF-8. In the worst case the emoji is written as its 4 single-byte tokens. Merges only make common
byte sequences shorter; they never make anything unreadable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A model has a 128,000-token window. You send 125,000 tokens and set max_tokens to 8,000. What can happen?</summary>

Input plus output can reach 125,000 + 8,000 = 133,000, which is more than the window. Depending on
the provider and model, the request is rejected up front, or it is accepted and the answer stops
when it reaches the window, after at most 128,000 − 125,000 = 3,000 tokens, with a stop reason
saying so. Either way, check the stop reason and keep enough room for the answer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your app trims chat history by dropping the oldest messages. A user complains the bot "forgot the rules". What went wrong?</summary>

Most likely the trimming treated the instructions (the system prompt) as an ordinary old message and
dropped it, or the instructions were part of the first user message. Keep instructions out of the
trimming, always first in every request, and trim only the conversation after them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Doubling the input from 2,000 to 4,000 tokens. What happens to the number of token pairs the basic design compares?</summary>

2,000 × 2,000 = 4,000,000 pairs before; 4,000 × 4,000 = 16,000,000 after. Twice the text, four times
the pairs. That is one reason long inputs cost more time and money per token than short ones.

</details>

## Recap

- Models read and write token IDs, never text. A tokenizer cuts text into sub-word pieces from a
  fixed vocabulary (100,000 to 200,000+ entries today) and back, losslessly.
- Byte-level BPE starts from the 256 bytes and merges the most frequent neighbouring pairs, so
  nothing is unknown and frequent text is short.
- English is about 4 characters per token; scripts that take more bytes and less training text cost
  more: our Hindi sentence is 29 tokens on GPT-4's tokenizer and 9 on GPT-4o's, against 5 in
  English.
- The context window holds instructions, history, tools, documents and the answer, all in tokens.
  Past it, requests fail or answers stop, so check the stop reason.
- Models remember nothing between requests: apps resend history, so the bill grows much faster than the chat, and long
  chats need dropping, summarising (compaction) or retrieval.
- Memory and work grow with every token, and models use the middle of long inputs less reliably:
  the window is a limit, not a target.
