## The question

A cache answers 99% of reads, and one morning the hit ratio reads 98%. Is the database doing 1%
more work, or twice as much? Twice: it used to see 1 read in 100 and now sees 2. A common cause
of a slip like that is a batch job that reads each of its keys exactly once. How does a key read
once push out a key read a million times? And every guide says that after a write you should
delete the cached key rather than update it. Why does deleting still leave stale values behind,
sometimes more often than updating, and what actually stops it?

## Why this problem exists

A **cache** is a smaller, faster copy of data whose source of truth lives somewhere else, usually
a database. A read the cache can answer is a **hit**; one it cannot is a **miss**, and goes to the
database. The **hit ratio** is the share of reads that hit. Its complement, the **miss ratio**, is
the number that matters, because it is the share of reads the database still has to serve:

`database reads = all reads × miss ratio`

At 10,000 reads a second, a 99% hit ratio leaves 100 reads a second for the database, 98% leaves
200 and 90% leaves 1,000. Going from 99% to 90% looks like a 9% change and is a tenfold change in
database load. Latency moves the same way. With illustrative numbers of 0.5 ms for a cache read and
10 ms for a database read, the average is 0.6 ms at 99% and 1.45 ms at 90%. The tail moves first:
once more than 1% of reads miss, the 99th-percentile request (the **p99**, slower than 99% of all
requests) is a database read, whatever the average says.

Caches work at all because popularity is uneven. A common model is the **Zipf distribution**: rank
the keys by popularity, and the key at rank k gets 1/k of the traffic of the top key. Rank 2 gets
half, rank 10 a tenth. Over 10,000 keys, that puts 53% of all reads on the top 100 keys and 76.5%
on the top 1,000 (our numbers, from
[examples/caching-strategies](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/caching-strategies)).
Twitter's study of 153 production cache clusters found skewed popularity of this kind in most of
them ([OSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/yang)).

The same numbers show the catch. Holding 1% of the keys can catch at most 53% of reads; 10 times
the memory raises that only to 76.5%. Each doubling of the cache buys less than the one before.
Memory runs out long before the misses do, so the cache is always smaller than the data. Two
consequences follow:

- Something must decide which keys stay. **Eviction** removes a key to make room; the
  **eviction policy** chooses which. **Admission** is the less known decision: whether a new key
  should get in at all.
- The cached copy goes out of date whenever the source changes. A **TTL** (time to live) expires
  an entry after a fixed time, which bounds how stale it can get. Everything faster than the TTL
  needs the write path to tell the cache.

The policy is worth more than most people expect. On our Zipf traffic with the cache holding 10%
of the keys, LRU (the default most people reach for) hits 67.6% and W-TinyLFU, the policy in the
Java library Caffeine, hits 74.2%. That is a miss ratio of 32.4% against 25.8%: LRU sends 26% more
reads to the database. W-TinyLFU with half the memory (5% of the keys, 67.1%) roughly matches LRU
with all of it.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

"Put Redis in front of the database. On a read, check Redis; on a miss, read the database and
`SET` the result with a one-hour TTL. On a write, update the database and `SET` the new value."
Four things go wrong, in roughly the order teams find them.

**It fills up and starts refusing writes.** Redis's default eviction policy is `noeviction`: once
`maxmemory` is reached, every command that needs memory, including your cache fills, fails with an
out-of-memory error, while reads keep working
([Redis docs](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/eviction/), as of October 2026; our
local Redis 7.0 reports the same). A cache needs an eviction policy set on purpose.

**Two writes land in the wrong order.** Writer A commits price 100, writer B commits price 95. B's
`SET` reaches Redis first, A's arrives a millisecond later. The database says 95, the cache says
100, and it will keep saying 100 for up to an hour.

**A batch job flushes the cache.** A nightly report reads every product once. Under LRU each of
those reads becomes the most recently used key, and the keys your users read thousands of times an
hour fall off the end. The next morning starts with a cold cache.

**The cache becomes load-bearing.** At a 98% hit ratio, the database is sized for 2% of reads. If
the cache restarts or a deploy empties it, the database sees 50 times its usual load at once. That
is the subject of the cache stampede page (coming soon).

Three neighbours get confused with caching. A **read replica** is a full copy of the database
that the database itself keeps up to date; a cache holds a subset, shaped by your keys, and nobody
updates it unless your code does. **Memoization** caches the result of a function of its inputs;
it never goes stale because the same inputs always give the same output. A **CDN** caches HTTP
responses close to users; its rules (cache headers, purges) have their own page (coming soon), but
the policies on this page apply inside it.

## The core idea

Every cache is three decisions. **Where it sits**: inside the application process, or across the
network in a shared service, or both. **How reads and writes reach it**: who fills it on a miss,
and what a write does to the cached copy. **What it keeps**: which key goes when it is full, and
whether a new key deserves a place. The first decision sets the latency and how many copies must be
kept in step. The second decides how stale the cache can be. The third decides the hit ratio for a
given amount of memory.

Each decision is made mostly independently of the others, which is why the section below takes
them one at a time.

## How it works, piece by piece

### Where the cache sits

An **in-process cache** is a hash map with a size limit inside your application: Caffeine in Java,
`functools.lru_cache` or `cachetools` in Python, an `lru-cache` package in Node. A lookup is a
memory access, well under a microsecond, with no serialisation and no network. A **remote cache**
(Redis, Valkey, Memcached) is a separate server: every lookup is a network round trip, typically a
few hundred microseconds inside one cloud zone, plus the cost of turning bytes back into objects.

The difference that matters most is not speed but **copies**. Fifty pods with an in-process cache
hold fifty copies of the hot data. A 2 GB cache per pod is 100 GB of memory holding the same 2 GB.
A write must reach all fifty to keep them fresh, and in practice usually reaches none, so
in-process caches rely on short TTLs. And every deploy restarts every pod with an empty cache.

<Figure caption="Five pods, five in-process copies, one remote cache. The in-process copies are the fastest to read and the hardest to keep in step: every write has five copies to fix, and every deploy empties all five.">
  <Tiers />
</Figure>

Large services use both, called a **near cache** or two-level cache: a small in-process cache with
a TTL of seconds in front of a shared remote cache with a TTL of minutes. Redis can keep the near
copies honest. With `CLIENT TRACKING` turned on, the server remembers which keys each connection
read and pushes an invalidation message when one of them changes
([Redis docs](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/client-side-caching/)). The rule of
thumb that falls out: in-process for small, hot, rarely changing data (feature flags,
configuration, a currency table); remote for everything that must look the same from every pod.

### Cache-aside, and the write that races it

**Cache-aside** (also called lazy loading) is the pattern most applications use. The application
owns both paths. On a read it asks the cache; on a miss it reads the database and fills the cache.
On a write it updates the database and then does something to the cached key.

The "something" is where the trouble is. Setting the new value has the two-writer problem from
above. So the standard advice is to **delete** the key and let the next read fill it. A delete is
the same whatever order it arrives in, so the two-writer problem goes away.

A different race remains, between a reader and a writer. It needs four events in one order, and
each step below is one of them:

That last point is the surprise. Delete-on-write empties the cache after every write, so the next
few reads all miss and all go to the database. Each of them is a reader in flight, and if the next
write commits while one is still reading, that reader fills an old value. On a hot key the race is
not rare. In our simulation of one key with 500 reads a second, 10 ms database reads and 20 writes
a second, the cache held an outdated version 7.14% of the time with delete-on-write and 0.96% with
set-on-write. At 1 write a second the share drops to 0.25%, but a single stale fill lasted 1.49 s,
until the next write came along.

What fixes it is **versions**. Give every row a version that only increases (a counter, or the
commit timestamp), and make the cache refuse to store anything older than what it already knows.
For that to work after a write, the cache must still know something after the write, so the writer
does not delete. It replaces the entry with a **marker**: "deleted, at version 2". A reader's fill
of v1 is now refused; the next reader misses, reads v2 and fills it. Uber's CacheFront does exactly
this in production: its invalidations write markers instead of deleting keys, and a Lua script in
Redis compares row timestamps so a stale value is never written over a newer one
([Uber, 2025](https://www.uber.com/blog/how-uber-serves-over-150-million-reads/)).

### Read-through and refresh-ahead

**Read-through** moves the miss handling out of the application and into the cache layer. The
application asks for a key; the cache library (or a caching proxy) calls a loader function on a
miss, stores the result and returns it. The reads are the same as cache-aside. The difference is
that one component now sees every miss, which lets it do two things cache-aside code rarely does:

- **Coalesce concurrent misses.** If 200 requests miss the same key in the same 10 ms, a
  read-through cache runs the loader once and hands the result to all 200. Caffeine's loading
  cache does this per key by default. Cache-aside code sends 200 identical queries.
- **Refresh before expiry.** **Refresh-ahead** reloads a hot entry in the background when it is
  old but not yet expired, and keeps serving the old value until the new one arrives. In Caffeine,
  `refreshAfterWrite(1 min)` with `expireAfterWrite(5 min)` means: after a minute, the next read
  triggers a reload and still gets the cached value; after five, the entry is gone and a read
  waits ([Caffeine wiki](https://github.com/ben-manes/caffeine/wiki/Refresh)). Hot keys then never
  miss on expiry; cold keys expire normally.

The cost is coupling: the cache must know how to load each kind of data, which is natural for an
in-process library and awkward for a shared Redis that many services use. Uber put the logic in the
database's own query layer instead, so every service gets read-through behaviour without writing
any (more on that below).

### Write-through

**Write-through** sends writes through the cache layer: the application writes to the cache, the
cache layer writes to the database synchronously, and the write is acknowledged once both are done.

The cache is always populated for keys that were written a moment ago, so a read right after a write hits.
The costs are equally direct. Every write now pays for two writes. The cache fills with data that
may never be read: a key written once and never read again takes the place of a key that was read
often ([AWS ElastiCache docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonElastiCache/latest/dg/Strategies.html)).
And "write both" is not atomic. If the database commit succeeds and the cache update fails (a
timeout, a restart), the two disagree, so write-through still needs a TTL as the safety net. The
same two-writer ordering problem applies if two cache nodes or two threads write the same key.

### Write-behind and write-around

**Write-behind** (also called write-back) acknowledges a write as soon as the cache has it, and
writes the database later, in batches. The cache keeps a set of **dirty** keys (changed since the
last flush) and flushes them every interval.

The gain is **write coalescing**. A view counter incremented 5,000 times a second on one key,
flushed every second, costs the database 1 write a second instead of 5,000. The loss is the same
number seen from the other side: if the cache node dies, every write since the last flush is gone,
up to 5,000 increments here. The client was told those writes succeeded.

Two more costs are often missed. Anything that reads the database directly (reports, other
services, a replica) sees data up to one interval old. And the flush must preserve order per key,
or a later value can be overwritten by an earlier one. Use write-behind where losing a few seconds
of writes is acceptable and the write rate is the problem: counters, analytics, rate-limit
buckets, "last seen" timestamps. Never for money or orders.

**Write-around** writes only the database and leaves the cache alone, so keys are cached only when
someone reads them. It is what cache-aside does on the write path, and it avoids write-through's
problem of caching what nobody reads.

### Eviction: LRU and the scan

Now the third decision, and its building blocks. When the cache is full and a new key arrives,
some key must go. Two signals predict which keys will be read again. **Recency**: a key read a
moment ago is likely to be read again soon. **Frequency**: a key read many times is likely to be
read again. Most policies are a mix of the two, plus some way of dealing with **one-hit wonders**,
keys that are read once and never again.

**LRU** (least recently used) evicts the key whose last read is oldest. It is built from a hash map
and a doubly linked list in recency order: a hit moves the key's node to the front, an insert adds
at the front, an eviction removes from the back, all in constant time. With room for three keys and
the reads `A B C A D`, the order after `A` is `A C B`, so `D` evicts `B`: `A` survived because it
was read again.

LRU's weakness is that it trusts every read equally. A key read once becomes "most recently used"
and pushes out a key read a thousand times. A batch job reading 10,000 one-time keys through a
cache of 8,000 entries leaves the cache holding the last 8,000 of them and nothing else.

<Figure caption="An LRU cache of eight before and after a batch job reads eight keys once each. Every one-time key enters as most recently used, so after eight reads the cache holds only keys nobody will read again.">
  <LruScan />
</Figure>

In our simulation, adding a batch job that reads one new key for every two normal reads drops
LRU's hit ratio on the normal traffic from 67.6% to 58.8% (cache of 10% of the keys), and leaves
the frequency-aware policies below within half a point of where they were.

LRU has a second cost that only shows under concurrency: every hit is a write, because it moves a
node in a shared list, so every hit takes a lock. That is why Redis does not run a real LRU. It
samples 5 random keys (`maxmemory-samples`) and evicts the one idle longest, keeping a small pool of
good candidates between evictions, which comes close to true LRU without a list
([Redis docs](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/eviction/)).

### Counting instead: LFU and W-TinyLFU

**LFU** (least frequently used) keeps a hit count per key and evicts the smallest. A batch job's
keys have count 1 and go first, so LFU ignores scans. Its own failure is the opposite one: counts
never fade. A key that was hot yesterday keeps its count of 50,000 today, while today's hot keys
start at 1 and are evicted before they can catch up. In our simulation, when popularity flips
halfway through (the coldest keys become the hottest), exact LFU's hit ratio in the second half
falls from 73% to 37%.

Redis's LFU mode fixes both problems in one byte per key. The counter is logarithmic: it starts at
5, and each hit increments it with probability 1 / ((counter − 5) × 10 + 1), so the first hits
always count, a key at 15 needs about 100 hits per step, and about a million hits reach the maximum
of 255. And it decays: every minute a key goes unread, its counter drops by one (`lfu-log-factor`
10 and `lfu-decay-time` 1 are the defaults, as of October 2026).

**W-TinyLFU**, the policy in Caffeine, takes a different route: it uses frequency for admission,
not for eviction. It needs one building block first. A **count-min sketch** estimates how often each
key was seen, in a fixed amount of memory, without storing the keys. It is a few rows of small
counters. To record a key, hash it once per row and add 1 to the counter it lands on in each row.
To estimate, read those counters and take the smallest: a key whose counters read 3, 4, 3 and 4 is
estimated at 3. Other keys share counters, so each counter can only overcount; the smallest is the
least wrong. Caffeine uses 4-bit counters, about 8 bytes per
cache entry ([Caffeine wiki](https://github.com/ben-manes/caffeine/wiki/Efficiency)), and halves every
counter after a number of increments ten times the cache size, which is how old popularity fades.

<Figure caption="A count-min sketch estimates a key's frequency as the smallest of its counters, here 3. W-TinyLFU uses that estimate to decide admission: a new key estimated at 1 does not replace a key estimated at 3.">
  <Sketch />
</Figure>

With the sketch in place, the cache has three parts:

1. **A small window** of about 1% of the capacity, plain LRU. Every new key lands here, so a key
   that is about to become popular gets a few reads to prove it.
2. **A main area** of the remaining 99%, split into a probation part (20%) and a protected part
   (80%). A key read again while in probation moves to protected.
3. **The admission duel.** When the window evicts a key and the main area is full, the sketch
   compares the window's evicted key with the main area's next victim. The more frequent one stays;
   on a tie the victim stays.

A batch job's key reaches the duel with an estimate of 1 and loses. Yesterday's hot key loses its
advantage after a few halvings. Caffeine also resizes the window with hill climbing, growing it
when recency pays and shrinking it when frequency does.

### S3-FIFO and SIEVE: drop the one-hit wonders fast

The newest policies start from an observation about real traces: many keys are read once, and the
most useful thing a policy can do is drop those quickly, which the S3-FIFO paper calls **quick
demotion**. They also avoid LRU's lock: nothing moves on a hit.

**S3-FIFO** (SOSP 2023) uses three FIFO queues, each first in, first out
([Yang et al., 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3600006.3613147)):

- A **small** queue holds 10% of the cache. Every new key enters here.
- A **main** queue holds the other 90%.
- A **ghost** queue holds only the names of keys recently dropped from small, no values.

A hit only increments a counter on the key, capped at 3. When a key reaches the end of the small
queue, it moves to main if it was read while in small; otherwise it is dropped and its name goes
into the ghost queue. A miss on a key whose name is in the ghost queue goes straight into main: it
was dropped too early. At the end of the main queue, a key with a counter above zero goes back to
the front with its counter lowered by one; a key at zero is evicted.

<Figure caption="S3-FIFO. A one-hit wonder lives only in the small queue and leaves after 10% of the cache has passed. Keys that are read again reach main, and main gives each key one more lap per recent hit before it is evicted.">
  <S3Fifo />
</Figure>

The paper reports lower miss ratios than state-of-the-art policies on 6,594 production traces from
14 datasets, the lowest mean miss ratio on 10 of the 14, and about six times the throughput of an
optimised LRU at 16 threads. **SIEVE** (NSDI 2024), by an overlapping group, goes further down the
same road with one FIFO queue, one "visited" bit per key and a pointer (the "hand") that sweeps
from the oldest key, clearing bits and evicting the first key whose bit is clear
([Zhang et al., 2024](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi24/presentation/zhang-yazhuo)). The
Twitter study had already found plain FIFO close to LRU on many production workloads; these designs
are FIFO with a cheap second chance.

### TTLs: a staleness bound, not an eviction policy

A TTL answers "how wrong can this get?", not "what should go when the cache is full?". Pick it from
the data: how long can a reader tolerate an old value? A product description might take an hour,
a price a few seconds, a permission check none at all (do not cache it, or cache it with
invalidation you trust). The Azure guidance puts it well: too short and the cache keeps reloading,
too long and it serves stale data
([Azure Architecture Center](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/architecture/patterns/cache-aside)).

TTLs also shape memory more than people plan for. In the Twitter study, TTLs were short enough in
many clusters that expiry, not eviction, decided what was in the cache: the working set was "keys
written in the last TTL". Two practical details follow. Expired keys still take memory until the
cache removes them, which Redis does lazily on access and with a periodic background sweep. And
keys written together expire together, so add a little random jitter to TTLs; why that matters is
on the cache stampede page (coming soon).

## Choosing between them

The write path, by what it guarantees and what it costs:

| Pattern | Reads after a write | Write cost | Stale window | Loses acked writes | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cache-aside, delete | miss, then fill | DB + 1 delete | reader race until next write or TTL | no | the default for read-heavy data |
| Cache-aside, versioned marker | miss, then fill | DB + 1 scripted write | none from races | no | hot keys, or when stale reads cost money |
| Read-through | same as cache-aside | same | same | no | in-process caches with loaders; coalescing misses |
| Write-through | hit | DB + cache, synchronous | if one of the two writes fails | no | read-after-write on data that is read often |
| Write-behind | hit | cache only; DB batched | readers of the DB lag one interval | yes, up to one interval | counters, analytics, high write rates |

The eviction policy, by how it behaves on the traffic that breaks policies:

| Policy | Hit cost | One-time keys (scans) | Popularity shifts | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LRU | list move, lock | flushed out | adapts fast | most libraries; Redis `allkeys-lru` (sampled) |
| LFU, no aging | count update | ignored | stuck on old keys | textbook; rarely used plainly |
| Redis LFU | counter in 8 bits | ignored | decays per minute | Redis `allkeys-lfu` |
| W-TinyLFU | sketch update, window LRU | rejected at admission | sketch halves | Caffeine and its ports |
| S3-FIFO, SIEVE | a bit or a counter | dropped from small queue | adapts | newer libraries and research caches |

How to choose: start with cache-aside and delete-on-write, a TTL from the data, and an explicit
eviction policy. Move a hot or money-sensitive key to versioned fills when the race starts to show
up. If you are choosing a library, prefer one with a frequency-aware or quick-demotion policy over
plain LRU; on skewed traffic it is worth more than doubling the memory. In Redis, `allkeys-lfu` is
usually the better fit for a pure cache; `allkeys-lru` when access is mostly recency-driven.

## Build it yourself

Three pieces: an LRU, an S3-FIFO, and a cache-aside fill that refuses old versions. The simulators
track keys only, since values do not affect what gets evicted. The full programs, including the
traffic generator and race simulation behind this page's numbers, are in
[`examples/caching-strategies`](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/caching-strategies).

An LRU in a few lines. Python's `OrderedDict` and JavaScript's `Map` both remember order, which is
all an LRU needs:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="policies.py (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/caching-strategies/python/policies.py
class Lru:
    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        self.capacity, self.items = capacity, OrderedDict()

    def access(self, key: int) -> bool:
        if key in self.items:
            self.items.move_to_end(key)  # most recently used goes to the back
            return True
        self.items[key] = True
        if len(self.items) > self.capacity:
            self.items.popitem(last=False)  # least recently used is at the front
        return False
```

```js title="policies.mjs (excerpt)"
    this.items.set(key, true);
```

```python title="policies.py (excerpt)"
class S3Fifo:
    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.small_cap = max(1, capacity // 10)
        self.small, self.main, self.freq = deque(), deque(), {}
        self.ghost, self.ghost_cap = OrderedDict(), capacity - self.small_cap

    def access(self, key: int) -> bool:
        if key in self.freq:
            self.freq[key] = min(self.freq[key] + 1, 3)
            return True
        while len(self.small) + len(self.main) >= self.capacity:
            if len(self.small) >= self.small_cap or not self.main:
                self.evict_small()
            else:
                self.evict_main()
        self.freq[key] = 0
        if key in self.ghost:  # seen recently and evicted too early: straight to main
            del self.ghost[key]
            self.main.append(key)
        else:
            self.small.append(key)
        return False

    def evict_small(self) -> None:
        key = self.small.popleft()
        if self.freq[key] > 0:  # used again while in small: keep it
            self.freq[key] = 0
            self.main.append(key)
        else:  # a one-hit wonder: drop it, remember only the key
            del self.freq[key]
            self.ghost[key] = True
            if len(self.ghost) > self.ghost_cap:
                self.ghost.popitem(last=False)

    def evict_main(self) -> None:
        while True:
            key = self.main.popleft()
            if self.freq[key] > 0:  # second chance: reinsert with one less
                self.freq[key] -= 1
                self.main.append(key)
            else:
                del self.freq[key]
                return
```

```js title="policies.mjs (excerpt)"
class S3Fifo {
  constructor(capacity) {
    this.capacity = capacity;
    this.smallCap = Math.max(1, Math.floor(capacity / 10));
    this.small = [];
    this.main = [];
    this.freq = new Map();
    this.ghost = new Map();
    this.ghostCap = capacity - this.smallCap;
  }
  access(key) {
    if (this.freq.has(key)) {
      this.freq.set(key, Math.min(this.freq.get(key) + 1, 3));
      return true;
    }
    while (this.small.length + this.main.length >= this.capacity) {
      if (this.small.length >= this.smallCap || !this.main.length) this.evictSmall();
      else this.evictMain();
    }
    this.freq.set(key, 0);
    if (this.ghost.has(key)) { // seen recently and evicted too early: straight to main
      this.ghost.delete(key);
      this.main.push(key);
    } else {
      this.small.push(key);
    }
    return false;
  }
  evictSmall() {
    const key = this.small.shift();
    if (this.freq.get(key) > 0) { // used again while in small: keep it
      this.freq.set(key, 0);
      this.main.push(key);
    } else { // a one-hit wonder: drop it, remember only the key
      this.freq.delete(key);
      this.ghost.set(key, true);
      if (this.ghost.size > this.ghostCap) this.ghost.delete(first(this.ghost));

  }
  evictMain() {
    for (;;) {
      const key = this.main.shift();
      if (this.freq.get(key) > 0) { // second chance: reinsert with one less
        this.freq.set(key, this.freq.get(key) - 1);
```

The versioned fill. A reader's fill is refused if the cache already holds this version or a newer
one, and a writer leaves a marker instead of deleting:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="race.py (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/caching-strategies/python/race.py
class Cache:
    """One key. An entry is (kind, version): kind 'value', or 'marker' for "deleted at version v"."""

    def __init__(self, mode: str) -> None:
        self.mode, self.entry = mode, None

    def fill(self, version: int) -> bool:
        """What a reader does after a miss. Returns False if the write was refused."""
        if self.mode == "versioned" and self.entry is not None:
            kind, current = self.entry
            # Refuse anything older than what the cache already knows about.
            if version < current or (kind == "value" and version == current):
                return False
        self.entry = ("value", version)
        return True

    def after_write(self, version: int) -> None:
        """What a writer does after committing `version` to the database."""
        if self.mode == "set":
            self.entry = ("value", version)
        elif self.mode == "delete":
            self.entry = None
        else:
            self.entry = ("marker", version)
```

```js title="race.mjs (excerpt)"
// runnable version: examples/caching-strategies/js/race.mjs
/** One key. An entry is { kind, version }: kind 'value', or 'marker' for "deleted at version v". */
class Cache {
  constructor(mode) {
    this.mode = mode;
    this.entry = null;
  }
  /** What a reader does after a miss. Returns false if the write was refused. */
  fill(version) {
    if (this.mode === 'versioned' && this.entry) {
      const { kind, version: current } = this.entry;
      // Refuse anything older than what the cache already knows about.
      if (version < current || (kind === 'value' && version === current)) return false;
    }
    this.entry = { kind: 'value', version };
    return true;
  }
  /** What a writer does after committing `version` to the database. */
  afterWrite(version) {
    if (this.mode === 'set') this.entry = { kind: 'value', version };
    else if (this.mode === 'delete') this.entry = null;
    else this.entry = { kind: 'marker', version };
  }
}
```

## How real systems do it

**Redis** (as of October 2026) offers eight eviction policies: `noeviction` (the default),
`allkeys-lru`, `allkeys-lfu`, `allkeys-random`, and `volatile-` versions of LRU, LFU and random that
only consider keys with a TTL, plus `volatile-ttl`, which evicts the key closest to expiring. The
`volatile-` policies behave like `noeviction` if no key has a TTL, a common surprise. LRU is
approximated by sampling and LFU uses the decaying logarithmic counter described above. Valkey,
the open-source fork, keeps the same policies.

**Caffeine**, the standard in-process cache for the JVM, runs W-TinyLFU with an adaptive window,
coalesces concurrent loads per key, and separates `expireAfterWrite` from `refreshAfterWrite` so hot
keys refresh in the background.

**Uber's CacheFront** puts the cache inside Docstore's query engine, so services get read-through
caching without writing cache code. It started with TTLs (5 minutes by default) and added
invalidation from **change data capture** (CDC): a service called Flux tails the MySQL binlog, the
database's own log of committed changes, and invalidates the affected rows within seconds. Shadow
reads compared against the database measured the cache as 99.99% consistent
([Uber, 2024](https://www.uber.com/blog/how-uber-serves-over-40-million-reads-per-second-using-an-integrated-cache/)).
The 2025 follow-up describes the versioned markers and the Lua script that deduplicates fills, as
covered above. Services can turn caching off per table or per request when they need strong
consistency.

**Meta** runs TAO, the cache in front of its social graph, at more than 10 trillion cache fills a
day, and reports raising its consistency by one measure from 99.9999% to 99.99999999%
([Meta, 2022](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/06/08/core-infra/cache-made-consistent/)). The
lesson in that post is about measurement: Meta first built Polaris, a service that checks cache
replicas against the database after invalidation events, tuned to report no false positives, so
that engineers would trust it. Their CacheLib engine, used by over 70 services, is described in its
own paper ([OSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/berg)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A full Redis refuses writes by default]
With `maxmemory` set and the default `noeviction`, a full cache rejects every fill with an
out-of-memory error while reads keep working. Applications that treat a failed `SET` as fatal start
failing requests; ones that ignore it quietly stop caching. Without `maxmemory` at all, Redis grows
until the operating system kills it. Set both, and alert on evictions and rejected writes.
:::

:::caution[Not caching "not found" lets one bad key hit the database every time]
A read for a key that does not exist misses, queries the database, finds nothing, caches nothing.
A client that keeps asking for that key, or a bot walking IDs, sends every request to the database.
**Negative caching** stores "not found" with a short TTL. The opposite mistake is caching an error
(a timeout, a 500) as if it were a value: keep errors out of the cache, or give them a TTL of
seconds.
:::

:::caution[The average hit ratio hides the endpoint that matters]
A 97% overall hit ratio can be 99.5% on product pages and 60% on checkout, because the product
pages have far more traffic. The database load and the p99 of checkout come from its own miss
ratio. Record hits and misses per key prefix or per caller, not only per cache.
:::

:::caution[Every deploy empties every in-process cache]
A rolling deploy of 40 pods restarts 40 empty caches, a few at a time. Each new pod sends its whole
miss stream to the remote cache or the database until it warms up, and with fast rollouts several
are cold at once. Size the backend for it, keep in-process caches small with the remote cache
behind them, or warm a pod before it takes traffic.
:::

:::caution[One huge value can stall everyone]
A 5 MB cached object costs a network transfer and a deserialisation on every hit, and in
single-threaded Redis a large `GET` delays every other client on that node. Cache the pieces readers
need, compress large values, and watch for keys that are both big and hot; the hot keys page (coming
soon) covers the second half.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

A cache adds a second copy of the data, and with it a second answer to "what is the price?". Every
strategy on this page narrows the time the two answers differ; none of them removes it, except by
giving up the cache for that read. It also adds a component whose failure moves load: a cache that
absorbs 98% of reads means a database sized for 2%, and a capacity plan that must say what happens
when the cache is cold.

Do not cache data that must be exactly current for the decision being made: an account balance
before a payment, inventory at the moment of reservation, a permission check after a revocation.
Read those from the source, or from a cache with versioned invalidation you have measured. Do not
cache data that is rarely read twice: the cache pays for every insert and earns only on hits. And do
not reach for a cache to hide a slow query that an index would fix; the cache hides it until the
first cold start.

## Interview corner

Caching appears in almost every system design interview, usually as "add a cache here". The strong
answer turns that box into the three decisions.

- **"How would you cache this read?"** Cache-aside with a remote cache, a TTL derived from how
  stale the data may be, an explicit eviction policy, and what happens on a write. Mention the
  near cache if latency matters and the copies problem that comes with it.
- **"What happens on a write?"** Delete or update, the two races, and versions as the fix. A
  strong answer says why delete is preferred (order-independent) and why it is not enough.
- **"Your hit ratio dropped from 99% to 95%."** Database load went up five times. Look for a new
  access pattern (a batch job, a crawler, a new feature reading cold data), a TTL change, an
  eviction policy that does not resist scans, or a cache that shrank.
- **"Design a cache for a feed or a product catalogue."** Size it from the popularity curve, pick a
  frequency-aware policy, plan the cold start, and say which data is never cached.

Quantify once: the miss ratio arithmetic (99% to 98% doubles the database reads) is the fastest way
to show you understand why caches matter.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A service does 20,000 reads a second at a 95% hit ratio. A change raises it to 97.5%. What happens to database reads, and to the p99?</summary>

Database reads go from 20,000 × 5% = 1,000 to 20,000 × 2.5% = 500 a second: halved. The p99 is
still a database read in both cases, because more than 1% of requests miss. To move the p99 into
cache territory, the miss ratio must drop below 1%.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. You switched from "update the cache on write" to "delete on write". Stale values still appear on hot keys. How?</summary>

A reader missed (the key had been deleted or had expired), read the old row, and was still in flight
when a writer committed and deleted the empty key. The reader then filled the old value. Deleting
makes misses more frequent, so on a key with many reads and writes this happens often. Versioned
fills with a marker instead of a delete stop it: the old fill is refused because the cache knows a
newer version.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does plain LFU do badly after popularity shifts, and how do Redis LFU and W-TinyLFU avoid it?</summary>

Old keys keep the high counts they earned, so new hot keys with low counts are evicted first and
never accumulate enough hits. Redis decays every counter by one per idle minute (by default);
W-TinyLFU's count-min sketch halves all counters after a fixed number of increments. Either way old
popularity fades.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A write-behind cache counts likes at 2,000 increments a second per hot post and flushes every 5 seconds. The node crashes. What is lost, and is that acceptable?</summary>

Up to 5 × 2,000 = 10,000 acknowledged increments per hot post. For a like counter that shows
"12.4K", usually yes. For anything a user paid for, never: use write-through or a database write
instead.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. After every deploy, p99 latency spikes for ten minutes. The service has 40 pods, each with a 1 GB in-process cache, and Redis behind them. Why, and what are two fixes?</summary>

Each restarted pod starts with an empty in-process cache, so its reads all go to Redis (and its
misses to the database) until it warms up. With a rolling deploy, several pods are cold at once.
Fixes: shrink the in-process tier so it warms in seconds and let Redis carry the hit ratio; warm
each pod (replay the hottest keys) before it takes traffic; or slow the rollout so few pods are cold
at the same time.

</details>

## Recap

- Database load is reads × miss ratio. 99% to 98% doubles it; past a 1% miss ratio the p99 is a
  database read.
- Three decisions: where the cache sits (in-process is fast but multiplies copies), how reads and
  writes reach it, and what it keeps.
- Cache-aside with delete removes the two-writer race but not the reader-writer race, which delete
  makes more frequent on hot keys. Versions plus a marker instead of a delete stop it.
- Read-through coalesces misses and enables refresh-ahead; write-through pays two writes for fresh
  reads; write-behind batches writes and can lose acknowledged ones.
- LRU is flushed by one-time keys, plain LFU is stuck on old ones. W-TinyLFU admits by sketch
  frequency with aging; S3-FIFO drops one-hit wonders from a small queue and moves nothing on a hit.
- TTLs bound staleness, not memory. Set an eviction policy on purpose: Redis's default refuses writes.
