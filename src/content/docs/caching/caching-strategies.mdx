## The question

An online shop shows the price of each product on its product page, and the page is opened 10,000
times a second. The price of a product changes perhaps twice a day. Why ask the database for the
same price 10,000 times a second? And if you stop asking it every time, how do you make sure
nobody sees yesterday's price?

The short answer: keep a copy of the prices you read often in a small, fast store next to your
app, called a **cache**, and answer most reads from the copy. Only when the copy is missing does
the app go to the database, and then it saves a copy for next time. Two rules keep the copy
useful. The cache has less room than the database, so when it is full it throws out the copy
least likely to be needed again. And when a price changes, the code that changed it tells the
cache to drop the old copy, while a time limit on every copy makes sure no mistake lives forever.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one shop and one product. The shop has 10,000 products in its database. Reading one price
from the database takes about 10 milliseconds (ms). The cache keeps copies in memory and answers in
about 0.5 ms, twenty times faster (10 ÷ 0.5 = 20). All numbers in Part 1 are illustrative.

1. **The first read.** A customer opens the page for product 42. The app asks the cache: "do you
   have the price of product 42?" The cache has never seen it, so the answer is no. That is a
   **miss**.
2. **Going to the database.** The app reads the price from the database, ₹499, which takes 10 ms.
   Before replying to the customer it saves a copy in the cache: "product 42 costs ₹499". It also
   attaches an expiry time to the copy: one hour from now.
3. **The second read.** A millisecond later another customer opens the same page. The app asks
   the cache again, and this time the copy is there. That is a **hit**. The customer gets the price
   in 0.5 ms and the database is not touched.
4. **Many customers.** Most visits go to a few popular products, so most reads find a copy. Say
   99 of every 100 reads hit. Then out of 10,000 reads a second, 1 in 100 misses:
   10,000 ÷ 100 = 100 reads a second reach the database, instead of 10,000.
5. **The cache fills up.** The cache has room for, say, 2,000 products, a fifth of the 10,000. A
   customer opens product 9,731, which is not cached, and there is no free room. One copy must go
   to make space. The cache throws out the one least likely to be read again, for example the
   product nobody has opened for the longest time.
6. **The price changes.** The seller lowers product 42 to ₹449. The app writes ₹449 to the
   database, then deletes the cached copy of product 42. The next read misses, reads ₹449 from the
   database and saves the new copy.
7. **A safety net.** If something goes wrong and the copy is never deleted (the app crashes between
   the two steps, say), the customer sees ₹499 for at most one hour, because the copy expires then
   and the next read fetches the real price.

</Figure>

Notice what the picture does not show: the database never tells the cache anything. The cache is
a copy that the app fills and the app cleans up. Everything on this page is about doing those two
jobs well.

Step 4 hides the most important number about any cache. The database only sees the reads that
miss. With 99 hits in 100, it sees 100 reads a second. Now suppose a change makes the cache hit
only 98 in 100. That sounds like a 1% change. Work it out: 2 reads in 100 now miss, and
10,000 ÷ 100 × 2 = 200 reads a second reach the database. The database does twice the work. So
the number to watch is not how often the cache hits, but how often it misses.

An everyday picture: a shopkeeper keeps the best-selling items on a small shelf behind the counter
and everything else in the storeroom at the back. Most customers are served from the shelf in
seconds; for the rest, the shopkeeper walks to the storeroom and, if the item is popular, puts one
on the shelf on the way back. When the shelf is full, the item nobody has asked for in a while goes
back. The picture stops working in one place: the shopkeeper can see when the storeroom label
changes, but a cache cannot see the database at all. If nobody tells it, it keeps the old price.

## The building blocks

Five ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A cache, a key and a value

**What it is.** A **cache** is a small, fast store that keeps copies of data whose real home is
somewhere slower, usually a database.

**Why it exists.** Reading from a database means a network trip, a query and often a disk read.
Reading from memory is much faster. When the same data is read again and again, keeping a copy in
memory saves that work every time.

**Example.** The shop's cache stores entries such as `product:42` → `₹499`. The left part, the name
you look the entry up by, is the **key**. The right part, the data, is the **value**. A Python `dict`
or a JavaScript `Map` works the same way.

**The precise version.** A cache is a key-value store with a size limit. The database stays the
**source of truth**: the one place whose answer counts when the two disagree. Filling the cache
with a value after a miss is called a **fill**. A cache never holds anything the source does not,
and nobody keeps it up to date except the code you write.

### Hit ratio and miss ratio

**What it is.** The **hit ratio** is the share of reads the cache answers. The **miss ratio** is the
share it cannot answer, which go to the database.

**Why it exists.** These two numbers tell you how much work the cache saves. The miss ratio is the
one that predicts the database's load.

**Example.** Out of 10,000 reads, 9,800 hit and 200 miss. The hit ratio is 9,800 ÷ 10,000 = 98%, and
the miss ratio is 200 ÷ 10,000 = 2%. Together they always make 100%.

**The precise version.** The load on the database is:

`database reads per second = all reads per second × miss ratio`

With the shop's numbers: 10,000 × 2% = 10,000 × 0.02 = 200 reads a second. Because the miss ratio is
small, a change of one point in it is a large change in load: from 1% to 2% doubles it.

### Stale data and the time to live

**What it is.** A cached value is **stale** when the database has changed and the cache still holds
the old value. A **time to live** (TTL) is an expiry time attached to each cached entry: after it,
the cache forgets the entry.

**Why it exists.** The cache cannot see the database, so it can be wrong. The TTL puts an upper
limit on how long it can stay wrong, even if every other mechanism fails.

**Example.** Product 42's copy was saved at 10:00 with a TTL of one hour. The price changes in the
database at 10:20, and the app forgets to delete the copy. Customers see the old price from 10:20
until 11:00, when the entry expires: 40 minutes of stale data, and never more than 60.

**The precise version.** A TTL bounds staleness; it does not prevent it. Anything faster than the
TTL needs the code that writes to the database to also tell the cache, by deleting or replacing the
entry. Part 2 shows that even this has holes, and what closes them.

### Eviction

**What it is.** **Eviction** means removing an entry from a full cache to make room for a new one.

**Why it exists.** The cache is always smaller than the data, because memory costs more than disk
and because most of the data is rarely read. Something has to decide what stays.

**Example.** A cache has room for three products and holds products 42, 7 and 13. Product 42 was read
a second ago, 7 a minute ago, 13 an hour ago. Product 99 is read and misses. If the rule is "throw
out the one read longest ago", product 13 goes and product 99 takes its place.

**The precise version.** The rule that picks the entry to remove is the **eviction policy**. The
example used the most common one, **LRU** (least recently used). Part 2 shows why LRU can be fooled,
and compares it with policies that count how often each key is read. A second, less known decision
is **admission**: whether a new key deserves a place at all.

### In-process and remote caches

**What it is.** An **in-process cache** lives in your application's own memory. A **remote cache** is
a separate server that every copy of your application talks to over the network.

**Why it exists.** In-process is the fastest possible: a lookup is a memory read. But big services run
many copies of the same app on many machines, and they need one cache that all of them share. That is
the remote cache.

**Example.** The shop runs its app on 5 machines. An in-process cache means 5 separate caches, each
holding its own copy of product 42's price. A remote cache means one cache server, and all 5 machines
ask it.

**The precise version.** The most common remote cache is **Redis**: a server that keeps keys and
values in memory and answers commands such as `GET product:42` and `SET product:42 499` over the
network. Valkey and Memcached are similar servers. In-process caches are
libraries: `functools.lru_cache` in Python, the `lru-cache` package in Node, Caffeine in Java.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- A **cache** is a small, fast copy of data that lives in a slower place, usually a database. The app
  asks the cache first; a **hit** is answered from the copy, a **miss** goes to the database and saves
  a copy.
- The database only sees the misses, so watch the **miss ratio**: going from 1% to 2% of reads
  missing doubles the database's work.
- The cache is smaller than the data, so when it is full it **evicts** the entry least likely to be
  needed again.
- The cache cannot see the database change. When you write, delete the cached copy; a **TTL** on
  every entry limits how long a wrong copy can live.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 described a cache that works. Here is where caches stop working. Part 1 showed that a slip
from 99% to 98% hits doubles the database's work. A common cause of such a slip is a batch job that
reads each of its keys exactly once: how does a key read once push out a key read a
million times? And every guide says that after a write you should delete the cached key rather than
update it. Why does deleting still leave stale values behind, sometimes more often than updating,
and what actually stops it?

### What a miss costs

Part 1 showed the database's load. The miss ratio also sets how long requests take. Use
illustrative numbers: 0.5 ms for a hit, 10 ms in all for a miss. The average time per read is each
time weighted by how often it happens:

- At 99% hits: 0.99 × 0.5 ms = 0.495 ms from hits, plus 0.01 × 10 ms = 0.1 ms from misses, which is
  0.595 ms, about 0.6 ms.
- At 90% hits: 0.9 × 0.5 ms = 0.45 ms from hits, plus 0.1 × 10 ms = 1 ms from misses, which is
  1.45 ms.

The average more than doubles (1.45 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 2.4), and the database's load goes up ten times (from
1% of reads to 10%). But the slowest requests change first, and to see that we need one more term.

A **percentile** is a way of describing the slow end of a set of timings. Averages hide slow
requests: one request in a hundred taking 10 ms barely moves the average, yet users notice it. So
engineers sort all the timings from fastest to slowest and read off a position. Take 100 reads
sorted by time: the 99th from the fastest is the **p99**, the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat.
If 2 of those 100 reads were misses, the two slowest are both 10 ms, so the 99th is a miss, and the
p99 is 10 ms. So once more than 1 read in 100 misses, the p99 is a database read, whatever the
average says.

### Why some keys are read far more than others

Caches work at all because popularity is uneven. A common model for this is the **Zipf
distribution**.

**What it is.** Rank the keys from most to least popular. In a Zipf distribution, the key at rank k
gets 1/k of the traffic of the top key: rank 2 gets half as many reads as rank 1, rank 10 gets a
tenth.

**Why it matters.** It tells you how much of the traffic a cache of a given size can catch, before
you build it.

**Example.** Over 10,000 keys, the top 100 keys get 53% of all reads. Here is where that comes from.
Give the top key 1 share of traffic, the second 1/2, the third 1/3, and so on. Adding up the shares
of the top 100 keys gives 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/100 = 5.19. Adding up all 10,000 keys gives 9.79. The top
100 get 5.19 ÷ 9.79 = 0.53 of the reads, which is 53%. The same sum for the top 1,000 keys is 7.49,
and 7.49 ÷ 9.79 = 76.5%.

**The precise version.** The share of rank k is proportional to 1/k<sup>s</sup>, where s sets how
lopsided the traffic is: a bigger s puts more of it on the top keys. The numbers above use s = 1.
A cache that somehow knew the most popular keys in advance and held exactly those is called the
**best static cache**: no real policy can beat it on this traffic, so it is the yardstick in the
simulations below. Twitter's study of 153 production cache clusters (a cluster is a group of cache
servers serving one use) found skewed popularity of this kind in most of them
([OSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/yang)).

The same numbers show the catch. Holding 100 keys (1% of them) can catch at most 53% of reads. Ten
times the memory, 1,000 keys, raises that only to 76.5%. Each doubling of the cache buys less than
the one before. Reaching Part 1's 99% on this traffic would take the top 9,068 keys, more than 90%
of all of them; real caches reach such numbers when their traffic is more lopsided than this model,
or when they hold most of the data that is read at all. Memory usually runs out before the misses
do, so the cache is smaller than the data, and the eviction policy matters.

How much? We ran the policies on this page against the same Zipf traffic, with the cache holding 10%
of the keys (the example code is linked in "Build it yourself"). LRU, the policy from Part 1, hits
67.6% of reads. W-TinyLFU, a policy Part 2 explains below, hits 74.2%. Turn those into miss ratios:
100% − 67.6% = 32.4% for LRU, and 100% − 74.2% = 25.8% for W-TinyLFU. Then 32.4 ÷ 25.8 = 1.26, so
LRU sends about 26% more reads to the database with the same memory. W-TinyLFU with half the memory
(5% of the keys) hits 67.1%, about what LRU does with all of it.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

The first design most teams write: "Put Redis in front of the database. On a read, check Redis; on a
miss, read the database and `SET` the result with a one-hour TTL. On a write, update the database and
`SET` the new value." Four things go wrong, in roughly the order teams find them.

**It fills up and starts refusing writes.** Redis has a memory limit, set with `maxmemory`, and what
it does when full is its eviction policy. The default policy is `noeviction`, which evicts nothing:
once the limit is reached, every command that needs more memory, including your cache fills, fails
with an out-of-memory error, while reads keep working
([Redis docs](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/eviction/), as of October 2026; our
local Redis 7.0 reports the same). A cache needs an eviction policy set on purpose.

**Two writes land in the wrong order.** First a word: a database **commit** is the moment a write
becomes permanent and visible to other readers. Writer A commits price 100, then writer B commits
price 95. Each then sends its `SET` to Redis. B's `SET` reaches Redis first and A's arrives a
millisecond later, because messages from two separate senders can overtake each other on the way. The
database says 95, the cache says 100, and it will keep saying 100 until the TTL, up to an hour.

**A batch job flushes the cache.** A nightly report reads every product once. Under LRU, each of
those reads makes that product the most recently used key, and the keys your customers read
thousands of times an hour slide to the end and are evicted. The next morning starts with a cache
full of products nobody is looking at.

**The cache becomes load-bearing.** At a 98% hit ratio, the database only ever sees 2% of reads, so
it gets sized for 2%. If the cache restarts, or a deploy (releasing a new version of the app) empties
it, every read misses: 100% of reads instead of 2%, and 100 ÷ 2 = 50 times the database's usual load,
all at once. That is the subject of
the cache stampede page (coming soon).

Three neighbours get confused with caching:

- A **read replica** is a full second copy of the database that the database itself keeps up to date.
  A cache holds a subset, shaped by your keys, and nobody updates it unless your code does.
- **Memoization** caches a function's result for given inputs, such as `price_with_tax(499)`. It never
  goes stale, because the same inputs always give the same output.
- A **CDN** (content delivery network) is a set of servers around the world that cache whole HTTP
  responses close to users. Its rules (cache headers, purges) have their own page (coming soon), but
  the eviction policies on this page apply inside it too.

## How it works, piece by piece

Every cache is three decisions. **Where it sits**: inside the application, across the network in a
shared server, or both. **How reads and writes reach it**: who fills it on a miss, and what a write
does to the cached copy. **What it keeps**: which key goes when it is full, and whether a new key
deserves a place. The first decision sets the speed and how many copies must be kept in step. The
second decides how stale the cache can get. The third decides the hit ratio for a given amount of
memory.

How reads and writes reach it: a read misses down to the database and fills the cache on the way back; a write goes to the database and then deletes, sets or marks the cached key. What it keeps: when the cache is full, eviction and admission decide what stays.">

</Figure>

The three are mostly independent of each other, so the sections below take them one at a time.

### Where the cache sits

Part 1 introduced in-process and remote caches. The speed difference is large. A **microsecond** is a
thousandth of a millisecond, and a **nanosecond** a thousandth of a microsecond. An in-process lookup
takes well under a microsecond. A remote lookup is a network round trip, typically a few hundred
microseconds inside one data centre, plus **deserialisation**: turning the bytes that came over the
network back into objects your code can use.

The difference that matters most is not speed but **copies**. Big services run many identical copies
of the app at once; in Kubernetes, the most common platform for running them, each running copy is
called a **pod**. Fifty pods with an in-process cache hold fifty copies of the hot data. At 2 GB per
pod, that is 50 × 2 GB = 100 GB of memory holding the same 2 GB. A write must reach all fifty copies to keep them
fresh, and in practice usually reaches none, so in-process caches rely on short TTLs. And every
deploy restarts every pod with an empty cache.

Look at the two sides of the next figure: the top row is the in-process copies, the bottom the one
shared remote cache.

<Figure caption="Five pods, five in-process copies, one remote cache. The in-process copies are the fastest to read and the hardest to keep in step: every write has five copies to fix, and every deploy empties all five.">
  <Tiers />
</Figure>

Large services use both, called a **near cache** or two-level cache: a small in-process cache with a
TTL of seconds in front of a shared remote cache with a TTL of minutes. Redis can keep the near copies
honest. With `CLIENT TRACKING` turned on, the Redis server remembers which keys each app connection
read, and sends that connection a message when one of them changes, so the app can drop its own copy
([Redis docs](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/client-side-caching/)). The rule of thumb:
in-process for small, hot, rarely changing data (feature flags, which switch features on and off;
configuration; a currency table); remote for everything that must look the same from every pod.

### Cache-aside, and the write that races it

**Cache-aside** (also called lazy loading) is the pattern from Part 1, and the one most applications
use. The application owns both paths. On a read it asks the cache; on a miss it reads the database
and fills the cache. On a write it updates the database and then does something to the cached key.

That "something" is where the trouble is. Setting the new value has the two-writer problem from the
previous section. So the standard advice is to **delete** the key and let the next read fill it. A
delete has the same result whichever order two of them arrive in, so the two-writer problem goes
away.

A different problem remains. A **race condition** is a bug that appears only when two things happen
at the same time in an unlucky order. It exists because the steps of two requests can interleave:
request A does step 1, request B does steps 1 and 2, then A does step 2. Our race is between a reader
and a writer, and it needs four events in one order. Each step below is one of them:

The last point of that summary is the surprise. Delete-on-write empties the cache after every write,
so the next few reads all miss and all go to the database. Each of them is a reader **in flight**:
it has started its database read and has not yet filled the cache. If the next write commits while
one of them is still reading, that reader fills an old value.

On a hot key this is not rare. We simulated one key with 500 reads a second, 10 ms database reads and
20 writes a second, for 10 minutes (600 seconds). With delete-on-write, the cache held an outdated
version 7.14% of the time: 600 s × 0.0714 = about 43 seconds of the 10 minutes. With set-on-write it
was 0.96%: 600 s × 0.0096 = about 6 seconds. At 1 write a second the delete lane was stale only 0.25%
of the time, but one stale fill lasted 1.49 s, until the next write came along to delete it.

What fixes it is **versions**.

**What a version is.** A number attached to each row that goes up by one with every change: the
first price is version 1, the next version 2.

**Why it helps.** If every cached value carries the version it came from, the cache can refuse to
store something older than what it already knows about.

**Example.** The cache knows about version 2. A slow reader arrives with version 1 and asks to store
it. The cache compares 1 with 2, sees the offer is older, and refuses.

**The precise version.** The version can be a counter column or the row's commit timestamp, as long
as it only ever increases. For the cache to know about version 2 after a write, the writer must not
delete the entry, because then there is nothing left to compare with. Instead it replaces the entry
with a **marker**: an entry with no value that says "deleted, at version 2". The slow reader's fill
of v1 is refused; the next reader misses, reads v2 and fills it.

There is one more requirement. The check ("is my version newer?") and the store must happen as one
step, with nothing in between; if they are two separate commands, another client can write between
them. An operation that cannot be split like this is called **atomic**. Redis runs small scripts
written in **Lua** (a small programming language Redis has built in) atomically: while a script runs,
no other command runs on that server. Uber's CacheFront does exactly this in production: its
invalidations write markers instead of deleting keys, and a Lua script in Redis compares row
timestamps so a stale value is never written over a newer one
([Uber, 2025](https://www.uber.com/blog/how-uber-serves-over-150-million-reads/)).

### Read-through and refresh-ahead

**Read-through** moves the miss handling out of the application and into the cache layer. The
application only ever asks the cache for a key. On a miss, the cache calls a **loader**, a function
you gave it that knows how to read that kind of data from the database, stores the result and returns
it.

The reads are the same as in cache-aside. The difference is that one component now sees every miss,
which lets it do two things cache-aside code rarely does.

**It can merge identical misses.** If 200 requests miss the same key within the same 10 ms, a
read-through cache runs the loader once and hands the result to all 200. This is called **coalescing**
the misses. Caffeine, the standard in-process cache library for Java, does it per key by default.
Cache-aside code sends 200 identical queries.

**It can refresh before expiry.** **Refresh-ahead** reloads a popular entry in the background (in a
separate task, while requests carry on) when the entry is old but not yet expired, and keeps serving
the old value until the new one arrives. In Caffeine, `refreshAfterWrite(1 min)` with
`expireAfterWrite(5 min)` means: once an entry is a minute old, the next read starts a reload and
still gets the cached value at once; once it is five minutes old, the entry is gone and a read waits
for the database ([Caffeine wiki](https://github.com/ben-manes/caffeine/wiki/Refresh)). Popular keys
then never miss because of expiry; rarely read keys expire normally.

The cost is coupling: the cache must know how to load each kind of data. That is natural for an
in-process library and awkward for a shared Redis that many services use. Uber put the logic in its
database's own query layer instead, so every service gets read-through behaviour without writing any
(more on that in "How real systems do it").

### Write-through

**Write-through** sends writes through the cache layer. The application writes to the cache; the
cache layer writes to the database straight away; and only when both are done does the application
get its **acknowledgement** (often shortened to "ack"): the reply that says "your write succeeded".

The cache always holds keys that were written a moment ago, so a read right after a write hits. The
costs are as direct:

- Every write now pays for two writes.
- The cache fills with data that may never be read: a key written once and never read again takes the
  place of a key that was read often
  ([AWS ElastiCache docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonElastiCache/latest/dg/Strategies.html)).
- "Write both" is not atomic. If the database commit succeeds and the cache update fails (a timeout, a
  restart), the two disagree, so write-through still needs a TTL as the safety net. The two-writer
  ordering problem also returns if two requests write the same key at once.

### Write-behind and write-around

**Write-behind** (also called write-back) acknowledges a write as soon as the cache has it, and writes
the database later. The cache keeps a list of **dirty** keys, the ones changed since they were last
written to the database, and every interval it **flushes** them: writes all of them to the database in
one batch.

The gain is **write coalescing**: many changes to the same key turn into one database write. A view
counter incremented 5,000 times a second on one key, flushed once a second, costs the database 1 write
a second instead of 5,000. The loss is the same number seen from the other side. If the cache server
dies, every change since the last flush is gone, up to 5,000 increments here, and the clients were
told those writes succeeded.

Two more costs are often missed. Anything that reads the database directly (reports, other services,
a read replica) sees data up to one interval old. And the flush must keep the order of changes per
key, or a later value can be overwritten by an earlier one. Use write-behind where losing a few
seconds of writes is acceptable and the write rate is the problem: counters, analytics, rate-limit
counters, "last seen" timestamps. Never for money or orders.

**Write-around** writes only the database and leaves the cache alone, so a key is cached only when
someone reads it. It is what cache-aside does on the write path, and it avoids write-through's problem
of caching what nobody reads.

### Eviction: LRU and the scan

Now the third decision. When the cache is full and a new key arrives, some key must go. Two signals
predict which keys will be read again:

- **Recency**: a key read a moment ago is likely to be read again soon.
- **Frequency**: a key read many times is likely to be read again.

Most policies mix the two, plus some way of dealing with **one-hit wonders**: keys that are read once
and never again.

**LRU** (least recently used) evicts the key whose last read is oldest. With room for three keys and
the reads `A B C A D`: after `A B C` the order from newest to oldest is `C B A`. Reading `A` again
moves it to the front: `A C B`. Then `D` arrives, the cache is full, and the oldest, `B`, is evicted.
`A` survived because it was read again.

To do this fast, LRU keeps two structures. A hash map (a `dict`) finds a key's entry. A **doubly linked
list** keeps the entries in recency order: each entry points to the one before and the one after it,
so an entry can be unlinked from the middle and moved to the front by changing a few of those links.
A hit moves the key to the front, a new key goes in at the front, and an eviction removes from the back.
Each of these takes the same time however big the cache is, which is called **constant time**.

LRU's weakness is that it trusts every read equally. A key read once becomes "most recently used" and
pushes out a key read a thousand times. A **scan**, a job that reads a long run of keys once each, is
the worst case: a batch job reading 10,000 one-time keys through a cache of 8,000 entries leaves the
cache holding the last 8,000 of them and nothing else.

The next figure shows a smaller version: a cache of eight, before and after a scan of eight keys.

<Figure caption="An LRU cache of eight before and after a batch job reads eight keys once each. Every one-time key enters as most recently used, so after eight reads the cache holds only keys nobody will read again.">
  <LruScan />
</Figure>

In our simulation, adding a batch job that reads one new key for every two normal reads drops LRU's
hit ratio on the normal traffic from 67.6% to 58.8% (cache of 10% of the keys), and leaves the
frequency-aware policies below within one point of where they were.

LRU has a second cost that shows only when many requests use the cache at once. A server handles
requests on several **threads**, separate lines of work running at the same time. Two threads must not
change the linked list at the same moment, or it breaks, so each takes a **lock** first: a flag that
lets only one thread in at a time while the others wait. In LRU every hit changes the list, so every
hit takes the lock. That is why Redis does not run a real LRU. It picks 5 keys at random
(`maxmemory-samples`) and evicts the one that has gone unread longest, keeping a small pool of good
candidates between evictions. That comes close to true LRU without a list
([Redis docs](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/eviction/)).

### Counting instead: LFU and W-TinyLFU

**LFU** (least frequently used) keeps a count of hits per key and evicts the key with the smallest
count. A batch job's keys have a count of 1 and go first, so LFU ignores scans.

LFU's own failure is the opposite one: counts never fade. A key that was hot yesterday keeps its count
of 50,000 today, while today's hot keys start at 1 and are evicted before they can catch up. In our
simulation, when popularity flips halfway through (the coldest keys become the hottest), plain LFU hits
only 37.2% of reads in the second half, against 73.2% on steady traffic.

Redis's LFU mode fixes both problems, scans and counts that never fade, with one byte per key. A
**bit** is a single 0 or 1, and a **byte** is 8 bits. Eight bits can be set in 2 × 2 × … × 2 (eight
times) = 256 ways, so a byte holds a whole number from 0 to 255, and the counter stops at 255. To make 255 enough for keys
read millions of times, the counter grows more slowly the higher it gets:

- A new key starts at 5.
- Each hit adds 1 only with a probability of 1 ÷ ((counter − 5) × 10 + 1). At 5, that is
  1 ÷ (0 × 10 + 1) = 1, so the first hit always counts. At 15, it is 1 ÷ (10 × 10 + 1) = 1 ÷ 101, so a
  key at 15 needs about 100 hits to move up one step.
- About a million hits reach the maximum of 255.
- The count also fades: every minute a key goes unread, its counter drops by one.

The 10 in the formula is the setting `lfu-log-factor`, and "one per minute" is `lfu-decay-time` set to
1; both are the defaults, as of October 2026. A counter like this, where each step up stands for many
more hits than the step before (one hit from 5 to 6, about a hundred from 15 to 16), is called
**logarithmic**.

**W-TinyLFU**, the policy in Caffeine, takes a different route: it uses frequency for admission, not
for eviction. It needs one building block first, and that block needs another: a hash.

A **hash function** turns a key into a number in a fixed range, always the same number for the same
key. It exists so you can find a slot for a key without searching. For example, a hash into 8 slots
might send `product:42` to slot 5 every time, and `product:7` to slot 1. Different keys can land in the
same slot; that is called a **collision**.

A **count-min sketch** estimates how often each key was seen, in a fixed amount of memory, without
storing the keys:

- It is a few rows of small counters.
- To record a key, hash it once per row, with a different hash function in each row, and add 1 to the
  counter it lands on in each row.
- To estimate a key's count, read the same counters and take the smallest. A key whose counters read
  3, 4, 3 and 4 is estimated at 3.
- Other keys collide into the same counters and add to them, so each counter can only overcount. The
  smallest is the least wrong.

Caffeine's counters use 4 bits each. Four bits give 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 values, so each counter counts
from 0 to 15, and the sketch costs about 8 bytes per cache entry
([Caffeine wiki](https://github.com/ben-manes/caffeine/wiki/Efficiency)). After a
number of recorded reads equal to ten times the cache size, it halves every counter. That is how old
popularity fades.

With the sketch in place, the cache has three parts:

1. **A small window** of about 1% of the capacity, run as plain LRU. Every new key lands here, so a key
   that is about to become popular gets a few reads to prove it.
2. **A main area** of the remaining 99%, split into a probation part (20% of it) and a protected part
   (80%). A key that is read again while in probation moves to protected, so keys with a record of
   repeat reads are the last to go.
3. **The admission duel.** When the window evicts a key and the main area is full, the sketch compares
   the window's evicted key (the candidate) with the key the main area would evict next (the victim).
   The more frequent one stays; on a tie the victim stays.

Read the figure top to bottom: the four rows of counters, the four the key lands on, then the duel
the estimate decides.

<Figure caption="A count-min sketch estimates a key's frequency as the smallest of its counters, here 3. W-TinyLFU uses that estimate to decide admission: a new key estimated at 1 does not replace a key estimated at 3.">
  <Sketch />
</Figure>

A batch job's key reaches the duel with an estimate of 1 and loses. Yesterday's hot key loses its
advantage after a few halvings. Caffeine also adjusts the window's size while it runs: it tries a
slightly bigger window, keeps the change if the hit ratio rose, and tries the other direction if it
fell (a method called **hill climbing**).

### S3-FIFO and SIEVE: drop the one-hit wonders fast

A **FIFO queue** (first in, first out) is a line: keys join at the back and leave from the front, in
the order they arrived, and nothing moves in between. The newest policies are built from FIFO queues.
They start from an observation about real **traces** (recordings of every request a production cache
received): many keys are read once, and the most useful thing a policy can do is drop those quickly.
The S3-FIFO paper calls this **quick demotion**. They also avoid LRU's lock, because nothing moves on a
hit.

**S3-FIFO**, from a 2023 paper at SOSP (a major systems research conference), uses three FIFO queues
([Yang et al., 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3600006.3613147)):

- A **small** queue holds 10% of the cache. Every new key enters here.
- A **main** queue holds the other 90%.
- A **ghost** queue holds only the names of keys recently dropped from small, with no values.

A hit only adds 1 to a counter on the key, capped at 3. When a key reaches the front of the small
queue:

- if it was read while in small, it moves to main;
- otherwise it is dropped, and its name goes into the ghost queue.

A miss on a key whose name is in the ghost queue sends it straight into main: it was dropped too
early. At the front of the main queue, a key with a counter above zero goes to the back with its
counter lowered by one; a key at zero is evicted.

<Figure caption="S3-FIFO. A one-hit wonder lives only in the small queue and leaves after 10% of the cache has passed. Keys that are read again reach main, and main gives each key one more lap per recent hit before it is evicted.">
  <S3Fifo />
</Figure>

The paper tests it on 6,594 production traces from 14 datasets and reports lower miss ratios than
state-of-the-art policies, the lowest average miss ratio on 10 of the 14 datasets. With 16 threads
using the cache at once, it handles about six times as many requests per second as an optimised LRU,
because hits take no lock.

**SIEVE**, from a 2024 paper at NSDI (another systems conference) by an overlapping group, goes
further down the same road
([Zhang et al., 2024](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi24/presentation/zhang-yazhuo)). It has one
FIFO queue and one "visited" bit per key, which a hit sets to 1. A pointer,
called the "hand", walks from the oldest key towards the newest. At each key it clears a visited bit
and moves on, and it evicts the first key whose bit is already 0. The Twitter study had already found
plain FIFO close to LRU on many production workloads; these designs are FIFO with a cheap second
chance.

### TTLs: a limit on staleness, not an eviction policy

A TTL answers "how wrong can this get?", not "what should go when the cache is full?". Pick it from
the data: how long can a reader live with an old value? A product description might take an hour, a
price a few seconds, a permission check none at all (do not cache it, or cache it only with
invalidation you trust). The Azure guidance names both sides: too short and the cache keeps reloading,
too long and it serves stale data
([Azure Architecture Center](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/architecture/patterns/cache-aside)).

TTLs also shape memory more than people plan for. In the Twitter study, TTLs were short enough in many
clusters that expiry, not eviction, decided what was in the cache: what the cache held was the keys
written in the last TTL. Two practical details follow. Expired keys still take memory until the cache
removes them, which Redis does when someone reads the key and with a periodic background sweep. And
keys written together expire together, so add **jitter**, a small random amount, to each TTL (an hour
plus or minus a few minutes); why that matters is on the cache stampede page (coming soon).

## Choosing between them

The write path, by what it guarantees and what it costs. The column to read first is "Stale window":
it is the one the patterns differ on most.

| Pattern | Reads after a write | Write cost | Stale window | Loses acknowledged writes | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cache-aside, delete | miss, then fill | DB + 1 delete | reader race until next write or TTL | no | the default for read-heavy data |
| Cache-aside, versioned marker | miss, then fill | DB + 1 scripted write | none from races | no | hot keys, or when stale reads cost money |
| Read-through | same as cache-aside | same | same | no | in-process caches with loaders; merging misses |
| Write-through | hit | DB + cache, before the reply | if one of the two writes fails | no | reading right after writing, on data that is read often |
| Write-behind | hit | cache only; DB in batches | readers of the DB lag one interval | yes, up to one interval | counters, analytics, high write rates |

The eviction policy, by how it behaves on the traffic that breaks policies. Notice that only the last
three rows handle both scans and shifts well.

| Policy | Work on a hit | One-time keys (scans) | Popularity shifts | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LRU | list move, under a lock | flush the cache | adapts fast | most libraries; Redis `allkeys-lru` (sampled) |
| LFU, counts never fade | count update | ignored | stuck on old keys | textbooks; rarely used plainly |
| Redis LFU | counter in one byte | ignored | fades one step a minute | Redis `allkeys-lfu` |
| W-TinyLFU | sketch update, window LRU | rejected at admission | sketch halves | Caffeine and its ports |
| S3-FIFO, SIEVE | a bit or a counter | dropped from small queue | adapts | newer libraries and research caches |

How to choose: start with cache-aside and delete-on-write, a TTL from the data, and an eviction policy
you chose on purpose. Move a hot or money-sensitive key to versioned fills when the race starts to show
up. If you are choosing a library, prefer one with a frequency-aware or quick-demotion policy over
plain LRU; on skewed traffic it is worth about as much as doubling the memory. In Redis, `allkeys-lfu`
is usually the better fit for a pure cache; `allkeys-lru` when reads mostly follow recency.

```python title="policies.py (excerpt)"
class Lru:
    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        self.capacity, self.items = capacity, OrderedDict()

    def access(self, key: int) -> bool:
        if key in self.items:
            self.items.move_to_end(key)  # most recently used goes to the back
            return True
        self.items[key] = True
        if len(self.items) > self.capacity:
            self.items.popitem(last=False)  # least recently used is at the front
        return False
```

```python title="policies.py (excerpt)"
class S3Fifo:
    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.small_cap = max(1, capacity // 10)
        self.small, self.main, self.freq = deque(), deque(), {}
        self.ghost, self.ghost_cap = OrderedDict(), capacity - self.small_cap

    def access(self, key: int) -> bool:
        if key in self.freq:
            self.freq[key] = min(self.freq[key] + 1, 3)
            return True
        while len(self.small) + len(self.main) >= self.capacity:
            if len(self.small) >= self.small_cap or not self.main:
                self.evict_small()
            else:
                self.evict_main()
        self.freq[key] = 0
        if key in self.ghost:  # seen recently and evicted too early: straight to main
            del self.ghost[key]
            self.main.append(key)
        else:
            self.small.append(key)
        return False

    def evict_small(self) -> None:
        key = self.small.popleft()
        if self.freq[key] > 0:  # used again while in small: keep it
            self.freq[key] = 0
            self.main.append(key)
        else:  # a one-hit wonder: drop it, remember only the key
            del self.freq[key]
            self.ghost[key] = True
            if len(self.ghost) > self.ghost_cap:
                self.ghost.popitem(last=False)

    def evict_main(self) -> None:
        while True:
            key = self.main.popleft()
            if self.freq[key] > 0:  # second chance: reinsert with one less
                self.freq[key] -= 1
                self.main.append(key)
            else:
                del self.freq[key]
                return
```

```js title="policies.mjs (excerpt)"
class S3Fifo {
  constructor(capacity) {
    this.capacity = capacity;
    this.smallCap = Math.max(1, Math.floor(capacity / 10));
    this.small = [];
    this.main = [];
    this.freq = new Map();
    this.ghost = new Map();
    this.ghostCap = capacity - this.smallCap;
  }
  access(key) {
    if (this.freq.has(key)) {
      this.freq.set(key, Math.min(this.freq.get(key) + 1, 3));
      return true;
    }
    while (this.small.length + this.main.length >= this.capacity) {
      if (this.small.length >= this.smallCap || !this.main.length) this.evictSmall();
      else this.evictMain();
    }
    this.freq.set(key, 0);
    if (this.ghost.has(key)) { // seen recently and evicted too early: straight to main
      this.ghost.delete(key);
      this.main.push(key);
    } else {
      this.small.push(key);
    }
    return false;
  }
  evictSmall() {
    const key = this.small.shift();
    if (this.freq.get(key) > 0) { // used again while in small: keep it
      this.freq.set(key, 0);
      this.main.push(key);
    } else { // a one-hit wonder: drop it, remember only the key
      this.freq.delete(key);
      this.ghost.set(key, true);
      if (this.ghost.size > this.ghostCap) this.ghost.delete(first(this.ghost));
    }
  }
  evictMain() {
    for (;;) {
      const key = this.main.shift();
      if (this.freq.get(key) > 0) { // second chance: reinsert with one less
        this.freq.set(key, this.freq.get(key) - 1);
        this.main.push(key);
      } else {
        this.freq.delete(key);
        return;
      }
    }
  }
```

Read it like this. `freq` holds a counter for every key in the cache, so "is it cached?" is one
lookup. A hit raises the counter (up to 3) and returns. A miss first makes room: it evicts from small
while small is over its 10% share, otherwise from main. Then it adds the new key to small, or to main
if its name is still in the ghost queue. `evict_small` promotes a key that was read while in small and
drops the rest, keeping their names in the ghost queue. `evict_main` gives keys with a positive counter
another lap, one less each time, until it finds one at zero.

The versioned fill. A reader's fill is refused if the cache already holds this version or a newer one,
and a writer leaves a marker instead of deleting:

```python title="race.py (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/caching-strategies/python/race.py
class Cache:
    """One key. An entry is (kind, version): kind 'value', or 'marker' for "deleted at version v"."""

    def __init__(self, mode: str) -> None:
        self.mode, self.entry = mode, None

    def fill(self, version: int) -> bool:
        """What a reader does after a miss. Returns False if the write was refused."""
        if self.mode == "versioned" and self.entry is not None:
            kind, current = self.entry
            # Refuse anything older than what the cache already knows about.
            if version < current or (kind == "value" and version == current):
                return False
        self.entry = ("value", version)
        return True

    def after_write(self, version: int) -> None:
        """What a writer does after committing `version` to the database."""
        if self.mode == "set":
            self.entry = ("value", version)
        elif self.mode == "delete":
            self.entry = None
        else:
            self.entry = ("marker", version)
```

```js title="race.mjs (excerpt)"
// runnable version: examples/caching-strategies/js/race.mjs
/** One key. An entry is { kind, version }: kind 'value', or 'marker' for "deleted at version v". */
class Cache {
  constructor(mode) {
    this.mode = mode;
    this.entry = null;
  }
  /** What a reader does after a miss. Returns false if the write was refused. */
  fill(version) {
    if (this.mode === 'versioned' && this.entry) {
      const { kind, version: current } = this.entry;
      // Refuse anything older than what the cache already knows about.
      if (version < current || (kind === 'value' && version === current)) return false;
    }
    this.entry = { kind: 'value', version };
    return true;
  }
  /** What a writer does after committing `version` to the database. */
  afterWrite(version) {
    if (this.mode === 'set') this.entry = { kind: 'value', version };
    else if (this.mode === 'delete') this.entry = null;
    else this.entry = { kind: 'marker', version };
  }
}
```

In Redis the same check has to happen inside the server, or another client can write between your read
and your write. The Lua script below runs atomically. It stores the entry as a Redis **hash** (a small
map of fields inside one key; `HMGET` reads fields, `HSET` writes them) with the fields `version`,
`kind` and `payload`, and `PEXPIRE` sets the TTL in milliseconds:

```lua title="versioned_set.lua"
-- runnable version: examples/caching-strategies/redis/versioned_set.lua (demo.sh replays the race)
-- KEYS[1] cache key   ARGV[1] version   ARGV[2] 'value' or 'marker'   ARGV[3] payload   ARGV[4] TTL ms
local current = redis.call('HMGET', KEYS[1], 'version', 'kind')
local version = tonumber(ARGV[1])
if current[1] then
  local known = tonumber(current[1])
  if version < known or (version == known and current[2] == 'value') then
    return 0 -- refused: the cache already has this version or a newer one
  end
end
redis.call('HSET', KEYS[1], 'version', version, 'kind', ARGV[2], 'payload', ARGV[3])
redis.call('PEXPIRE', KEYS[1], ARGV[4])
return 1
```

## How real systems do it

**Redis** (as of October 2026) offers eight eviction policies. `noeviction` (the default) evicts
nothing. `allkeys-lru`, `allkeys-lfu` and `allkeys-random` choose among all keys. `volatile-lru`,
`volatile-lfu` and `volatile-random` choose only among keys that have a TTL, and `volatile-ttl` evicts
the key closest to expiring. The `volatile-` policies behave like `noeviction` if no key has a TTL, a
common surprise. LRU is approximated by sampling and LFU uses the fading logarithmic counter described
above. Valkey, a separate project started from a copy of Redis's code, keeps the same policies.

**Caffeine** runs W-TinyLFU with a window that resizes itself, merges concurrent loads of the same key,
and separates `expireAfterWrite` from `refreshAfterWrite` so popular keys refresh in the background.

**Uber's CacheFront** puts the cache inside the query engine of Docstore, Uber's own database, so
services get read-through caching without writing cache code. It started with TTLs (5 minutes by
default) and then added invalidation from **change data capture** (CDC): reading the database's own
record of changes to learn what changed. A service called Flux reads the MySQL **binlog**, the log in
which MySQL records every committed change, and invalidates the affected rows within seconds. To measure
the result, Uber sent a sample of reads to both the cache and the database and compared the answers;
the cache agreed 99.99% of the time
([Uber, 2024](https://www.uber.com/blog/how-uber-serves-over-40-million-reads-per-second-using-an-integrated-cache/)).
The 2025 follow-up describes the versioned markers and the Lua script covered above. Services can turn
caching off per table or per request when they need every read to be exact.

**Meta** runs TAO, the cache in front of the data that links people, posts and pages, at more than 10
trillion cache fills a day. It reports raising its consistency, by one measure, from 99.9999% to
99.99999999%
([Meta, 2022](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/06/08/core-infra/cache-made-consistent/)). In plain
numbers: 99.9999% consistent means 0.0001% inconsistent, which is 1 in 1,000,000; 99.99999999% means
0.00000001%, which is 1 in 10,000,000,000. That is ten thousand times fewer stale results
(10,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 10,000). The lesson in that post is about measurement: Meta first built
Polaris, a service that checks cache copies against the database after each invalidation, and tuned it
never to raise a false alarm, so that engineers would trust it. Their CacheLib engine, used by over 70
services and able to keep a cache in memory and on flash storage (SSD) together, is described in its own
paper ([OSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/berg)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A full Redis refuses writes by default]
With `maxmemory` set and the default `noeviction`, a full cache rejects every fill with an
out-of-memory error while reads keep working. Applications that treat a failed `SET` as fatal start
failing requests; ones that ignore it quietly stop caching. Without `maxmemory` at all, Redis grows
until the operating system runs out of memory and kills it. Set both, and alert on evictions and
rejected writes.
:::

:::caution[Not caching "not found" lets one bad key hit the database every time]
A read for a key that does not exist misses, queries the database, finds nothing and caches nothing. A
client that keeps asking for that key, or a bot walking through IDs, sends every request to the
database. **Negative caching** stores "not found" with a short TTL. The opposite mistake is caching an
error (a timeout, a 500) as if it were a value: keep errors out of the cache, or give them a TTL of
seconds.
:::

:::caution[The average hit ratio hides the endpoint that matters]
A 97% overall hit ratio can be 99.5% on product pages and 60% on checkout, because the product pages
have far more traffic and dominate the average. The database load and the p99 of checkout come from
checkout's own miss ratio, 40%. Record hits and misses per key prefix or per caller, not only per
cache.
:::

:::caution[Every deploy empties every in-process cache]
A **rolling deploy** replaces the pods a few at a time, so 40 pods means 40 empty caches over the
rollout. Each new pod sends all its misses to the remote cache or the database until it warms up, and
with fast rollouts several are cold at once. Size the backend for it, keep in-process caches small with
the remote cache behind them, or warm a pod before it takes traffic.
:::

:::caution[One huge value can stall everyone]
A 5 MB cached object costs a network transfer and a deserialisation on every hit. Redis runs commands
on a single thread, one at a time, so a large `GET` also delays every other client on that server.
Cache the pieces readers need, compress large values, and watch for keys that are both big and hot; the
hot keys page (coming soon) covers the second half.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

A cache adds a second copy of the data, and with it a second answer to "what is the price?". Every
strategy on this page narrows the time the two answers differ; none removes it, except by not caching
that read. It also adds a component whose failure moves load: a cache that absorbs 98% of reads means a
database sized for 2%, and a capacity plan that must say what happens when the cache is empty.

Do not cache data that must be exactly current for the decision being made: an account balance before
a payment, stock at the moment of reservation, a permission check after access was taken away. Read
those from the source, or from a cache with versioned invalidation you have measured. Do not cache data
that is rarely read twice: the cache pays for every insert and earns only on hits. And do not reach for
a cache to hide a slow query that an index would fix; the cache hides it until the first cold start.

## Interview corner

Caching appears in almost every system design interview, usually as "add a cache here". The strong
answer turns that box into the three decisions.

- **"How would you cache this read?"** Cache-aside with a remote cache, a TTL derived from how stale the
  data may be, an eviction policy chosen on purpose, and what happens on a write. Mention the near cache
  if latency matters, and the copies problem that comes with it.
- **"What happens on a write?"** Delete or update, the two races, and versions as the fix. A strong
  answer says why delete is preferred (order does not matter) and why it is not enough.
- **"Your hit ratio dropped from 99% to 95%."** The miss ratio went from 1% to 5%, and 5 ÷ 1 = 5, so the
  database load went up five times. Look for a new access pattern (a batch job, a crawler, a new feature
  reading cold data), a TTL change, an eviction policy that does not resist scans, or a cache that
  shrank.
- **"Design a cache for a feed or a product catalogue."** Size it from the popularity curve, pick a
  frequency-aware policy, plan the cold start, and say which data is never cached.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A service does 20,000 reads a second at a 95% hit ratio. A change raises it to 97.5%. What happens to database reads, and to the p99?</summary>

The miss ratio goes from 5% to 2.5%. Database reads go from 20,000 × 0.05 = 1,000 a second to
20,000 × 0.025 = 500 a second: halved. The p99 is still a database read in both cases, because more
than 1 read in 100 misses (2.5 in 100). To move the p99 into cache territory, the miss ratio must drop
below 1%.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. You switched from "update the cache on write" to "delete on write". Stale values still appear on hot keys. How?</summary>

A reader missed (the key had been deleted or had expired), read the old row, and was still in flight
when a writer committed and deleted the already empty key. The reader then filled the old value.
Deleting makes misses more frequent, so on a key with many reads and writes this happens often.
Versioned fills with a marker instead of a delete stop it: the old fill is refused because the cache
knows a newer version.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does plain LFU do badly after popularity shifts, and how do Redis LFU and W-TinyLFU avoid it?</summary>

Old keys keep the high counts they earned, so new hot keys with low counts are evicted first and never
build up enough hits. Redis lowers every counter by one per minute the key goes unread (by default);
W-TinyLFU's count-min sketch halves all counters after a fixed number of reads. Either way old
popularity fades.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A write-behind cache counts likes at 2,000 increments a second per hot post and flushes every 5 seconds. The cache server crashes. What is lost, and is that acceptable?</summary>

Up to 5 seconds of increments: 5 × 2,000 = 10,000 acknowledged likes per hot post. For a like counter
that shows "12.4K", usually yes. For anything a user paid for, never: use write-through or a direct
database write instead.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. After every deploy, p99 latency spikes for ten minutes. The service has 40 pods, each with a 1 GB in-process cache, and Redis behind them. Why, and what are two fixes?</summary>

Each restarted pod starts with an empty in-process cache, so its reads all go to Redis (and its misses
to the database) until it warms up. With a rolling deploy, several pods are cold at once. Fixes: shrink
the in-process tier so it warms in seconds and let Redis carry the hit ratio; warm each pod (replay the
hottest keys) before it takes traffic; or slow the rollout so few pods are cold at the same time.

</details>

## Recap

- Database load is reads × miss ratio. 99% to 98% hits doubles it; once more than 1% of reads miss,
  the p99 is a database read.
- Three decisions: where the cache sits (in-process is fast but multiplies copies), how reads and
  writes reach it, and what it keeps.
- Cache-aside with delete removes the two-writer race but not the reader-writer race, which delete
  makes more frequent on hot keys. Versions plus a marker instead of a delete stop it.
- Read-through merges misses and enables refresh-ahead; write-through pays two writes for fresh reads;
  write-behind batches writes and can lose acknowledged ones.
- LRU is flushed by one-time keys, plain LFU is stuck on old ones. W-TinyLFU admits by estimated
  frequency that fades; S3-FIFO drops one-hit wonders from a small queue and moves nothing on a hit.
- TTLs limit staleness, not memory. Set an eviction policy on purpose: Redis's default refuses writes.
