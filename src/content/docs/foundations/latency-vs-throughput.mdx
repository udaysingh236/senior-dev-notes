## The question

Your server needs 5 milliseconds of work to answer one request. At 100 requests a second, users wait
about 10 ms for an answer. Traffic grows to 180 requests a second. The code has not changed and the
server still has 10% of its time free, yet users now wait about 50 ms: five times longer. Why does
a server get slow long before it is full?

The short answer: requests do not arrive evenly. Sometimes several arrive close together, and all
but one of them must wait in line. The busier the server, the more often that happens and the longer
the line gets, and the waiting grows much faster than the traffic. The two words in this page's title
are the two sides of that line: **latency** is how long one request takes, **throughput** is how many
requests the server finishes per second.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one server through the morning. It runs a small API, and every request needs exactly the same
5 ms of work on average. Call that the **work** per request.

1. **What the server can carry.** One request takes 5 ms, and a second has 1,000 ms, so the server can
   finish at most 1,000 ÷ 5 = 200 requests a second. That is its **capacity**.
2. **A quiet hour: 100 requests a second.** The server is asked for 100 × 5 ms = 500 ms of work in
   every second, so it is busy half the time and idle half the time.
3. **If requests arrived on a timetable**, one every 10 ms, each would find the server free, get its
   5 ms of work, and leave. Every user would wait exactly 5 ms.
4. **But requests arrive at random.** Users do not coordinate. In some stretches three requests
   arrive within 2 ms of each other; in others nothing arrives for 30 ms. Take three requests arriving
   at 0, 1 and 2 ms. The first is served from 0 to 5 ms. The second must wait until 5 ms, so it waits
   4 ms and finishes at 10. The third waits until 10, so it waits 8 ms and finishes at 15. The server
   then sits idle until the next request arrives.
5. **Averaged over many requests at 100 a second**, the waiting turns out to be about as long as the
   work itself: 5 ms of waiting plus 5 ms of work, 10 ms in total. That matches the 10 ms in the
   opening question.
6. **A busy hour: 180 requests a second.** Now the server is busy 180 × 5 ms = 900 ms of every second:
   90% of the time. Bunches still arrive, but there is very little idle time between them to work off
   the line, so a line that forms now takes much longer to clear. On average a request waits 45 ms
   before its 5 ms of work: 50 ms in total.
7. **The pattern.** A handy rule for this kind of random traffic: the total time a request takes is
   about the work divided by the fraction of time the server is free. Free half the time: 5 ÷ 0.5 =
   10 ms. Free a tenth of the time: 5 ÷ 0.1 = 50 ms. Free a twentieth: 5 ÷ 0.05 = 100 ms. As the server
   approaches full, the time per request has no upper limit.

<Figure caption="One server and the line in front of it. Latency is the waiting plus the work; throughput is how many finish each second; utilisation is how busy the server is. At 180 requests a second the server is 90% busy and the waiting is nine times the work.">
  <MentalModel />
</Figure>

So the two measures pull against each other through the line. Pushing throughput towards the
server's capacity (from 100 towards 200 a second) makes the line longer, and latency climbs: slowly
at first, then steeply. A server that is "only 90% busy" is not 10% away from trouble; it is already
making most requests wait.

An everyday picture: a railway ticket counter with one clerk who takes a minute per passenger. If one
passenger arrives every two minutes on average, the clerk is busy half the time, but passengers do not
arrive every two minutes exactly: sometimes three walk up together and two of them wait. Make the
average one passenger every 66 seconds and the clerk is 90% busy; a bunch at 10 AM is still being
worked off at 10:20. The picture stops working in one place: a passenger can see the line and leave,
while a request cannot see anything and waits its turn, holding memory and a connection the whole time.

## The building blocks

Five ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### Latency

**What it is.** **Latency** is how long one request takes, from the moment it is sent to the moment
the answer arrives.

**Why it exists as a measure.** Users feel latency: a page that takes two seconds feels broken even if
the server is handling thousands of other people perfectly well.

**Example.** A request that spends 2 ms crossing the network, waits 45 ms in line and then gets 5 ms of
work has a latency of 2 + 45 + 5 = 52 ms.

**The precise version.** Latency has three parts: time on the network, **waiting time** (in a line for
a free worker, a free thread, a free database connection), and **service time**, the time a worker
spends actually doing the work. On this page "work" and "service time" mean the same thing, written S.
Most of what makes latency surprising is the waiting part.

### Throughput and capacity

**What it is.** **Throughput** is how many requests are finished per unit of time: 180 requests a
second, 2 GB an hour.

**Why it exists as a measure.** The business feels throughput: it is how many customers, orders or
messages the system actually serves.

**Example.** Our server finishes 180 requests a second in the busy hour. Its **capacity**, the most it
could ever finish, is 200 a second, because each takes 5 ms.

**The precise version.** Throughput is what got done; capacity is the ceiling. A neighbour word gets
mixed in: **bandwidth** is the capacity of a network link (1 Gbit/s), while throughput is what actually
went through it. When more arrives than the capacity, throughput stays at the capacity and the extra
waits, so the line grows for as long as the overload lasts.

### Utilisation

**What it is.** **Utilisation** is the fraction of time a worker is busy.

**Why it exists as a measure.** It is the number that predicts waiting. CPU graphs show it, and
autoscaling rules use it.

**Example.** At 180 requests a second, each needing 5 ms, the server is asked for 180 × 0.005 s = 0.9
seconds of work every second: 90% utilisation.

**The precise version.** Utilisation u = arrival rate × service time, divided by the number of workers
if there are several. It must stay below 1 (100%): at 1 or above, work arrives faster than it can be
done, and the line grows without end.

### A queue

**What it is.** A **queue** is the line of requests waiting for a worker, served in the order they
arrived.

**Why it exists.** A worker can do one thing at a time. When a request arrives while the worker is
busy, the system has two choices: reject it, or keep it until the worker is free. Keeping it is the
queue.

**Example.** At 10:00:00.000 the server is busy with request A. Requests B and C arrive 1 ms apart and
join the queue: B is first in line, C second. When A finishes, B starts.

**The precise version.** Queues hide everywhere, not only in message brokers: the accept queue of a
socket, a thread pool's task queue, requests waiting for a database connection, a lock that other
transactions wait on. Each one turns "too busy right now" into waiting time, which shows up as
latency. The order "first come, first served" is called **FIFO** (first in, first out).

### Requests in flight

**What it is.** A request is **in flight** from the moment it arrives until it is finished: while it
waits in the queue and while it is being worked on.

**Why it exists as a measure.** Every request in flight holds something: a thread, memory for its data,
an open connection, maybe a database connection. Servers run out of those, so the number in flight is
what you size pools and limits for.

**Example.** At one instant our busy server is working on one request and has four waiting: five
requests in flight.

**The precise version.** The count goes up and down all the time; what matters is the average over
time, written L. Part 2 shows a law that links it to throughput and latency, so you can work out any
one of the three from the other two.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- **Latency** is how long one request takes: waiting in line plus the work. **Throughput** is how many
  requests finish per second.
- Requests arrive at random, so they bunch up, and the ones in a bunch wait in a **queue**.
- The busier the server (its **utilisation**), the less idle time it has to clear the line, so waiting
  grows much faster than traffic: about work ÷ (fraction of time free). At 90% busy, the waiting is
  nine times the work.
- So "the server is only 80 or 90% busy" already means slow answers. To keep latency low, keep
  anything users wait on well below full.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 used one server, one queue and a rule of thumb. Real systems raise harder questions. Why does
an autoscaling rule of "add servers at 80% CPU", which sounds conservative, leave users with answers
five times slower than they could be? Why does a load test that sends ever more traffic report flat
latency right up to the point where production falls over? And how can batching, which makes each
request wait on purpose, triple throughput and make latency *drop*?

The rest of this page builds the tools to answer those: the curve behind the rule of thumb, a law that
links throughput, latency and requests in flight, what makes waiting worse besides utilisation, why
shared queues and pipelines behave as they do, when batching helps, and how to measure all of it
honestly.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

The usual rules: add servers when CPU passes 80%; watch average latency; check with a load test using
50 virtual users. A **virtual user** is a loop in a load-testing tool that sends a request, waits for the
answer, and then sends the next one, imitating one person.

Each rule quietly assumes latency stays close to the service time until the server is full, and each
breaks because of the waiting from Part 1. At 80% utilisation, the rule from Part 1 says a request takes
S ÷ 0.2 = 5 times its service time: four parts waiting, one part work. The CPU graph says "20% headroom";
users see a service five times slower than it could be.

The 50-virtual-user test never sees this, because each virtual user waits for its answer before
sending the next request. When the server slows down, the test automatically sends fewer requests, at
the exact moment real users, who do not wait for each other, would keep arriving. "Measuring them
honestly" below comes back to this.

## How it works, piece by piece

### The curve: latency is work divided by the time left free

Part 1's rule of thumb is a real formula. For one server, with requests arriving independently at
random and service times that also vary randomly around their average, the average latency is:

**latency = S ÷ (1 − u)**

Here S is the average service time and u is the utilisation as a fraction (0.9 for 90%), so 1 − u is
the fraction of time the server is free. Plotting it shows its shape:

<Figure caption="Mean latency for one server with random arrivals, in multiples of the work a request needs. The green band is the work itself; the amber area is waiting. Latency doubles at 50% utilisation, is five times the work at 80% and ten times at 90%, and has no limit as utilisation approaches 100%.">
  <HockeyStick />
</Figure>

Read it left to right. Up to about 50% busy, the waiting is at most as long as the work. Between 50%
and 80%, it grows to four times the work. Past 80% the curve turns almost straight up: going from 90%
to 95% busy doubles latency again, from 10 to 20 times the work. Engineers call this a "hockey stick"
for its shape. It is why "the server is only 80% busy" and "the service is five times slower than it
should be" are both true at the same time.

### Little's law: the one equation that links them

**What it says.** **Little's law**: the average number of requests in flight equals throughput times
average latency, L = throughput × latency.

**Why it matters.** It ties together the three numbers from Part 1, so knowing any two gives you the
third, and it holds for almost any system that is not piling up work, whatever the order of service,
the number of servers or the shape of the traffic.

**Example.** Picture a toll plaza. If 20 cars arrive a minute and each spends 3 minutes in the queue
and at the booth, then at any moment about 20 × 3 = 60 cars are somewhere in the plaza. Nothing about
the booths' speed or the order of service changes that arithmetic. The analogy breaks in one place: a
toll plaza's queue is visible, while a server's "cars" are threads, connections and memory, invisible
until they run out.

<Figure caption="Little's law: what is inside the box equals what goes through per second times how long each stays. Read one way, it tells you how many threads a load needs; read the other way, the most a fixed pool can do.">
  <LittlesLaw />
</Figure>

**The precise version.** The law needs the system to be in a **steady state**: on average, requests
leave as fast as they arrive. Three uses, each one line of arithmetic:

- **Sizing.** 1,000 requests a second at 200 ms each means 1,000 × 0.2 = 200 requests in flight, so 200
  threads, 200 coroutines (lightweight threads managed by the program) or 200 database connections busy on average, and more at peaks.
- **Ceilings.** A pool of 50 database connections, with queries taking 20 ms each, can finish at most
  50 ÷ 0.020 = 2,500 queries a second (throughput = L ÷ latency). Past that, requests wait for a
  connection, and the waiting shows up as latency, not as an error.
- **Cost.** Requests in flight hold memory and connections, so latency *is* capacity: double the
  latency at the same traffic and you need twice the threads. Marc Brooker uses this to show that in
  typical services the slowest 1% of requests can account for about half of the average latency, and
  so for about half of the threads and connections you pay for
  ([Brooker, 2026](https://brooker.co.za/blog/2026/07/29/lorenz-and-little.html)).

Little's law is also a sanity check for dashboards: if your metrics say 1,000 requests a second at
50 ms but 400 requests in flight, one of the three numbers is wrong (1,000 × 0.05 = 50, not 400).

### Queues, measured

Part 1 showed bunches making requests wait. There is a second half to the story that makes high
utilisation so costly: the idle time between bunches is lost for good. A server cannot do work ahead
of time for requests that have not arrived yet, so a quiet 10 ms gap does not help the bunch that
arrives after it. Bunches create waiting; gaps waste capacity; random arrivals produce both. The busier
the server, the fewer gaps it has to catch up in.

To check the formula, the example code simulates 200,000 requests with 10 ms of average work at
different utilisations and measures the average latency:

        90%     99.9 ms         100.0 ms          55.1 ms

### Variability: same load, different waits

The last column of that table is the same server with **fixed** service times: every request takes
exactly 10 ms instead of 10 ms on average. At every utilisation it waits about half as long. So waiting
depends on utilisation *and* on how unpredictable the arrivals and the service times are.

**What variability is.** How much individual values spread around their average. Three requests taking
10, 10 and 10 ms have no spread; three taking 1, 2 and 27 ms have the same 10 ms average and a large
spread.

**Why it matters.** A long request in front of the line makes everyone behind it wait; a predictable
stream lets the server keep up with fewer surprises.

**Example.** A queue serving 5 ms reads and one 5-second export: the export takes the worker for 5
seconds, and every read that arrives meanwhile waits behind it, even though the average service time
barely moved.

**The precise version.** Variability is measured as the **coefficient of variation**: the standard
deviation (the typical distance of a value from the average) divided by the average. Fully random
arrivals or service times score 1; perfectly regular ones score 0. Kingman's formula, a common
approximation, combines them:

```text
waiting ≈ (variability of arrivals² + variability of service²) / 2  ×  u / (1 − u)  ×  S
```

With random arrivals (1) and fixed service (0), the first factor is (1 + 0) / 2 = 0.5: half the waiting
of the fully random case, which is the last column of the table. The formula says two things the curve
alone does not. First, at high utilisation, halving variability is worth about as much as a large
capacity increase. Second, whatever makes service times erratic in real systems (garbage-collection
pauses, when the language runtime stops briefly to free unused memory; cache misses, when data is not
in fast memory and must be fetched from slower storage; a few huge requests among small ones; a slow
disk) costs latency for *everyone*
behind them, not only for the slow request. Splitting big and small work into separate queues, so a
5-second export never sits in front of a 5 ms read, removes variability without adding a single server.

### One shared queue beats many

Four servers can be fed in two ways. With **a queue per server**, a load balancer (a component that
spreads incoming requests over several servers) picks a server for each request, and the request waits
in that server's queue. With **one shared queue**, every request waits in a single line and goes to
whichever server frees up first. Same servers, same load:

<Figure caption="Four servers at 80% each. With a queue per server, one server can sit idle while another has five waiting. With one shared queue, no server is idle while work waits anywhere, and mean latency falls from 49.6 ms to 17.4 ms.">
  <SharedQueue />
</Figure>

The shared queue never lets a server sit idle while work waits elsewhere, so the gaps of one server
absorb the bunches of another. It is the single snaking line at an airport check-in counter instead
of one line per desk. It is also why a load balancer that sends each request to the least busy server
(the [proxy and load balancer page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/) covers the algorithms) beats **round
robin**, which hands requests to servers in turn regardless of how busy they are: least-busy behaves
almost like one shared queue. For the same reason one big pool usually beats several small ones, and
splitting a service's capacity across too many isolated partitions raises latency even at the same
total utilisation.

### Pipelines: throughput is the slowest stage

A request rarely meets one server. It passes through **stages**, each with its own workers and its own
queue: the load balancer, the application, a cache, the database. The two measures combine differently
along that path:

<Figure caption="Throughput of a pipeline is the throughput of its slowest stage. Latency is the sum of the stages, plus the queue that builds in front of the slowest one. Speeding up any other stage changes neither.">
  <Bottleneck />
</Figure>

The slowest stage is called the **bottleneck**, after the narrow neck that limits how fast a bottle
pours. Optimising any other stage is the most common performance mistake: making parsing twice as fast
in the figure saves 0.25 ms of latency and adds nothing to throughput. Find the stage with the highest
utilisation first; that is where the queue is.

Two lessons follow. **Adding workers** raises throughput only for the stage they are added to, and only
until that stage stops being the slowest; past that point, extra workers only add more requests waiting
at the next bottleneck, often the database. And **parallelism does not lower the latency of one
request** unless that request is itself split into parts that run at once, in which case its latency
becomes that of its slowest part (the percentiles page, coming soon, covers why that makes the slowest
requests matter so much).

### Why more servers stop helping

Doubling the servers doubles throughput only if the servers never wait for each other. Real systems
share things: a lock (a flag that lets only one worker touch something at a time), a database row, a cache that must stay consistent, a leader that every write goes
through. The **Universal Scalability Law** (USL) names two costs that grow as you add servers:

- **Contention** (written α): the fraction of the work that must happen one server at a time, such as
  holding a shared lock. Like the slowest stage of a pipeline, it caps the speed-up.
- **Coherence** (written β): the cost of keeping servers in agreement, such as telling each other to
  drop stale cache entries. With N servers there are N × (N − 1) ÷ 2 pairs that may need to talk, so
  this cost grows roughly with N²: past some point, adding a server makes the whole system *slower*.

Throughput relative to one server is N ÷ (1 + α(N − 1) + βN(N − 1)). With a modest 5% contention
(α = 0.05) and 0.1% coherence cost (β = 0.001):

| Servers | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Throughput (× one server) | 1.0 | 1.9 | 3.4 | 5.7 | 8.0 | 9.0 | 7.8 |

Work out one column to see where the numbers come from. For 8 servers: contention adds
0.05 × (8 − 1) = 0.35, coherence adds 0.001 × 8 × 7 = 0.056, so the bottom of the fraction is
1 + 0.35 + 0.056 = 1.406, and 8 ÷ 1.406 ≈ 5.7. Eight servers do the work of 5.7.

Thirty-two servers give nine times one server, and sixty-four give less than thirty-two. The peak sits
at √((1 − α) ÷ β): here (1 − 0.05) ÷ 0.001 = 950, and the square root of 950 is about 31 servers. You find α and β by measuring throughput at a few cluster
sizes and fitting the curve. The practical lesson: remove shared state from the busy path (split it,
cache it locally, make it read-only) before buying more servers to fight it. Aravindh Sampathkumar's
SREcon25 talk walks through fitting it to production data
([USENIX, 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/srecon25emea/presentation/sampathkumar)).

### Batching: paying latency to buy throughput

**What batching is.** Collecting several items and handling them in one operation instead of one at a
time.

**Why it exists.** Many operations have a large **fixed cost** per call and a small cost per item. Three
common ones: `fsync`, which forces data from memory onto the disk so it survives a crash; a network round
trip; and a database **commit**, which makes a transaction permanent.

**Example.** If one disk flush costs 2 ms whatever it carries, then flushing after every write caps you
at 1,000 ÷ 2 = 500 writes a second. One flush for 100 writes costs about the same 2 ms, so the same disk
could take tens of thousands of writes a second.

**The precise version.** A batcher waits a short **window** to collect items, or until a size limit, and
sends them together. The price is latency, but only some of the time. At low load, a write that arrives
alone waits for company that may not come, so it is slower than it needed to be. Near capacity, the
unbatched writer is queueing anyway, and the batch takes everything waiting in one call, so the queue
disappears and latency *falls*. Above the unbatched writer's capacity there is no contest: it cannot
keep up at all. All three cases on the same stream of writes:

<BatchSim />

Real systems expose this as a setting. Kafka, a widely used message broker, has its producers wait up to
`linger.ms` to fill a batch, and Kafka 4.0 (2025) changed the default from 0 ms to 5 ms, trading a few
milliseconds per message for fewer, fuller requests ([KIP-1030](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-1030%3A+Change+constraints+and+default+values+for+various+configurations)).
The cost showed up exactly where the sim predicts: a follow-up proposal, KIP-1224, reports latency
regressions for low-traffic internal writes after the upgrade and makes the wait adaptive.

PostgreSQL's `commit_delay` does the same for commits: it waits up to that many microseconds so several
commits can share one flush of the write-ahead log (the file every change is written to before it
counts as committed), and only when at least `commit_siblings` other transactions are active, so a lone
commit does not wait for nobody ([PostgreSQL docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-wal.html),
as of October 2026). That condition is the "wait only if others are around" rule the sim's batcher
uses. TCP's Nagle algorithm is the same trade inside the operating system, with the same cost at low
load (the [TCP page](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/) measures it).

### On the network: latency-bound or bandwidth-bound?

The same two words apply to moving data, and which one limits you depends on the size. Two terms first:
the **round-trip time** (RTT) is how long a message takes to reach the other side and an answer to come
back, set mostly by distance; **bandwidth** (B) is how many bytes per second the link can carry.
Sending S bytes takes roughly **a few RTTs + S ÷ B**: a few round trips to set up the connection and to
let TCP's **slow start** (TCP sends a little at first and speeds up as acknowledgements come back) open
up, plus the time to push the bytes through. The
[page on what happens when you type a URL](/foundations/how-the-web-works/) counts those round trips.

| Transfer, 100 ms RTT, 1 Gbit/s link | Round trips (about 3 × 100 ms) | Pushing bytes (S ÷ B) | Limited by |
|---|---|---|---|
| A 10 KB API response | 300 ms | 0.08 ms | latency |
| A 10 MB image | 300 ms | 80 ms | latency, mostly |
| A 10 GB backup | 300 ms | 80 s | bandwidth |

For small responses, a 10 Gbit/s upgrade changes nothing users can feel; moving the server closer,
reusing connections or removing round trips does. For bulk transfers it is the opposite. Most API
traffic is in the first row, which is why latency work pays off for it and bandwidth upgrades do not.

### Measuring them honestly

Most bad conclusions about latency come from how it was measured. Three traps.

**Closed load tests.** The virtual users from "The obvious fix" make a **closed** test: each waits for
its answer before sending the next request, so the load depends on how fast the server answers. When
the server slows down, the tool sends less: it backs off at the exact moment it should be applying
pressure, and the latency it reports stays deceptively low. An **open** test sends requests on a
schedule (say, 500 a second), whatever happened to earlier ones, as real users do.

<Figure caption="When the server slows down, a closed test (each virtual user waits for its answer) sends fewer requests and reports gentle latency. An open test keeps arriving on schedule, so the queue and the real latency show up.">
  <OpenClosed />
</Figure>

The k6 load-testing documentation describes the closed model's slowdown and names the effect
**coordinated omission**: the slow moments are left out because the test coordinated with the server
instead of ignoring it. Its "arrival-rate" executors give an open model
([k6 docs](https://grafana.com/docs/k6/latest/using-k6/scenarios/concepts/open-vs-closed/), as of
October 2026).

**Averages.** The average hides the shape. A service with a 20 ms average can have one request in a
hundred taking two seconds, and those are the ones users remember. Report **percentiles** instead: the
p50 is the latency half of all requests beat, and the p99 is the latency 99 in 100 requests beat, so it
shows the slowest 1%. Percentiles have their own page (coming soon).

**Averaged utilisation.** "60% CPU over the last minute" can be 100% for 30 seconds and 20% for the
other 30. The queue built up in the 100% half; the one-minute average hides it. Look at utilisation over
the same short window as your latency target.

## Choosing between them

| You want | Lever | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Lower latency at the same load | Remove work (cache, better query, fewer round trips) | Engineering time; caches need invalidating |
| Lower latency at the same capacity | Lower utilisation, lower variability, one shared queue | Idle capacity; separating big and small work |
| More throughput | More workers or servers at the bottleneck stage | Money; moves the bottleneck to the next stage |
| More throughput per server | Batching | Latency at low load; a window to tune |
| Predictable latency under overload | Limit what waits: bounded queues, load shedding | Rejected requests instead of slow ones |

As a working rule: keep the utilisation of anything users wait on below about 70–80% at peak, and treat
a latency target as a statement about utilisation and variability, not only about how fast the code is.

## Build it yourself

Two pieces. First, the queue simulation behind the table above. Requests arrive at random (the gap
between arrivals is drawn from an exponential distribution, the standard model for independent
arrivals), each waits for the first free server, and every arrival and departure is recorded, so
Little's law can be checked against a count taken over time instead of assumed.

def simulate(utilisation: float, servers: int = 1, fixed_service: bool = False, seed: int = 42) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Returns (mean latency in ms, time-averaged number in the system) for one shared FIFO queue."""
    rng = Rng(seed)
    arrival_gap = S / (utilisation * servers)
    free_at = [0.0] * servers  # when each server becomes free
    t = 0.0
    total_latency = 0.0
    events = []  # (time, +1 for an arrival or -1 for a departure)
    for _ in range(N):
        t += rng.exp(arrival_gap)
        k = min(range(servers), key=free_at.__getitem__)  # the server that frees up first
        start = max(t, free_at[k])
        service = S if fixed_service else rng.exp(S)
        free_at[k] = start + service
        total_latency += free_at[k] - t
        events += [(t, 1), (free_at[k], -1)]
    # Count how many requests are in the system at every moment, and average it over time.
    events.sort()
    area, count, last = 0.0, 0, 0.0
    for when, change in events:
        area += count * (when - last)
        count, last = count + change, when
    return total_latency / N, area / last

function simulate(utilisation, servers = 1, fixedService = false, seed = 42) {
  const r = rng(seed);
  const arrivalGap = S / (utilisation * servers);
  const freeAt = new Array(servers).fill(0); // when each server becomes free
  let t = 0;
  let totalLatency = 0;
  const events = []; // [time, +1 for an arrival or -1 for a departure]
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    t += r.exp(arrivalGap);
    let k = 0; // the server that frees up first
    for (let j = 1; j < servers; j++) if (freeAt[j] < freeAt[k]) k = j;
    const start = Math.max(t, freeAt[k]);
    const service = fixedService ? S : r.exp(S);
    freeAt[k] = start + service;
    totalLatency += freeAt[k] - t;
    events.push([t, 1], [freeAt[k], -1]);
  }
  // Count how many requests are in the system at every moment, and average it over time.
  events.sort((a, b) => a[0] - b[0] || a[1] - b[1]);
  let area = 0;
  let count = 0;
  let last = 0;
  for (const [when, change] of events) {
    area += count * (when - last);
    count += change;
    last = when;
  }
  return [totalLatency / N, area / last];
}

class Batcher:
    def __init__(self, backend: Backend, window_s: float = 0.002, max_batch: int = 100) -> None:
        self.backend, self.window_s, self.max_batch = backend, window_s, max_batch
        self.pending: list[tuple[int, asyncio.Future]] = []
        self.wakeup = asyncio.Event()
        self.task = asyncio.create_task(self.run())

    async def write(self, item: int) -> None:
        done = asyncio.get_running_loop().create_future()
        self.pending.append((item, done))
        self.wakeup.set()
        await done  # resolves when the batch containing this write is stored

    async def run(self) -> None:
        while True:
            await self.wakeup.wait()
            self.wakeup.clear()
            if len(self.pending) < 3:
                await asyncio.sleep(self.window_s)  # a lone write waits a little for company
            batch, self.pending = self.pending[: self.max_batch], self.pending[self.max_batch:]
            if self.pending:
                self.wakeup.set()  # more are waiting: go round again straight after this call
            await self.backend.write([item for item, _ in batch])
            for _, done in batch:
                done.set_result(None)

class Batcher {
  pending = []; // [item, resolve]
  #wake = null;

  constructor(backend, windowMs = 2, maxBatch = 100) {
    Object.assign(this, { backend, windowMs, maxBatch, stopped: false });
    this.loop = this.run();
  }

  write(item) {
    return new Promise((resolve) => {
      this.pending.push([item, resolve]);
      this.#wake?.(); // resolves when the batch containing this write is stored
    });
  }

  async run() {
    while (!this.stopped) {
      if (!this.pending.length) await new Promise((r) => (this.#wake = r));
      this.#wake = null;
      if (this.stopped) return;
      if (this.pending.length < 3) await sleep(this.windowMs); // a lone write waits a little for company
      const batch = this.pending.splice(0, this.maxBatch);
      await this.backend.write(batch.map(([item]) => item));
      for (const [, resolve] of batch) resolve();
    }
  }

## How real systems do it

- **Kafka producers** batch by size (`batch.size`, 16 KB by default) and by time (`linger.ms`, 5 ms by
  default since Kafka 4.0), sending when either fills; throughput-focused deployments raise both,
  latency-focused ones lower them ([KIP-1030](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-1030%3A+Change+constraints+and+default+values+for+various+configurations),
  as of October 2026).
- **PostgreSQL** group-commits: transactions committing at the same moment share one write-ahead-log
  flush, and `commit_delay` lets it wait for more when enough other transactions are active
  ([PostgreSQL docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-wal.html)).
- **Load-testing tools** such as k6 offer both closed (virtual users) and open (arrival rate) models;
  only the open one measures the latency real users would see when the server slows down
  ([k6 docs](https://grafana.com/docs/k6/latest/using-k6/scenarios/concepts/open-vs-closed/)).
- **Capacity planning with queues**: SRE (site reliability engineering) teams model services as queues
  to predict where latency turns up, as in Jeff Poole's SREcon24 talk on applying queueing theory to
  production metrics ([USENIX, 2024](https://www.usenix.org/conference/srecon24americas/presentation/poole))
  and Aravindh Sampathkumar's SREcon25 talk on the utilisation curve and the Universal Scalability Law
  ([USENIX, 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/srecon25emea/presentation/sampathkumar)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::caution[Autoscaling on 80% CPU]
**Autoscaling** adds or removes servers automatically based on a metric. A target of 80% average CPU
sounds conservative, but for a service with random arrivals and one queue per server it means latency
around five times the service time at the target, and a cliff right above it. Scale on latency or queue
length, or set CPU targets near 50–70% for anything users wait on.
:::

- **Retries near saturation.** When a service slows down, clients give up waiting and retry, which
  raises the arrival rate exactly when utilisation is highest, which slows it further. The
  [proxy and gateway page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/) shows the multiplication across hops.
- **A thread pool sized for throughput, not for the bottleneck.** Raising a web server's worker count
  from 50 to 500 does not help if the database can serve 100 queries at once; it moves 400 requests
  from a visible queue to an invisible one inside the database, where they also slow down the 100
  being served. Little's law with the database's real capacity sizes the pool.
- **Unbounded queues.** A queue with no limit turns overload into latency without limit: requests wait
  minutes for answers nobody is still waiting for. Limit the queue and reject early (load shedding has
  its own page, coming soon).
- **Batching at low traffic.** A batch window chosen for peak traffic adds its full delay to every lone
  request at night. Batch only when others are waiting, as PostgreSQL's `commit_siblings` does, or make
  the window adaptive, as Kafka's KIP-1224 proposes.

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

The queueing formulas on this page are models with assumptions: independent random arrivals, a steady
state, first come first served. Real traffic has daily cycles, bursts and feedback (retries,
autoscaling), and real servers slow down as they get busier (lock contention, caches thrashing), which
bends the curve further up. Use the models for shape and rough size, and confirm with an open load test
against the real service.

The levers also have costs that are not latency. Low utilisation is idle capacity you pay for. One
shared queue becomes a point of contention itself at very high rates, which is why large systems use
several queues and let idle workers take work from busy ones' queues (**work stealing**). Batching adds
code for partial failure: one bad item in a batch of 100 must not fail the other 99.

## Interview corner

**"What's the difference between latency and throughput?"** Latency is time per request, throughput is
requests finished per unit time. A strong answer links them with Little's law and mentions that latency
rises sharply with utilisation. The follow-up: *"How many threads do I need for 2,000 requests a second
at 50 ms?"* 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 on average, with headroom for peaks and for the slowest requests.

**"Why does latency spike before CPU hits 100%?"** Random arrivals bunch up; waiting grows as
u ÷ (1 − u) times the service time, so at 80% a request waits four times its service time on average.
Mention variability, and that averaged utilisation hides bursts.

**"How would you increase the throughput of a write-heavy service?"** Find the bottleneck stage, then
batch where the cost per call is fixed (group commit, bulk inserts, producer batching), add parallelism
at the bottleneck, and keep queues limited. Strong answers say batching costs latency at low load and
how to avoid that.

**"Your load test shows flat latency up to 5,000 requests a second, but production slows at 3,000.
Why?"** Probably a closed test (coordinated omission), averages instead of percentiles, or a test that
did not reproduce production's variability (mixed request sizes, cache misses).

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A service averages 40 ms per request and handles 500 requests a second. How many requests are in flight on average, and what happens if latency doubles at the same traffic?</summary>

500 × 0.040 = 20 in flight. If latency doubles to 80 ms at the same 500 a second, 40 are in flight:
twice the threads, connections and memory, for the same throughput. That is why a slow dependency
exhausts pools even when traffic is flat.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. One server with random arrivals runs at 60% utilisation and 10 ms service time. You can either make the code 25% faster or add a second server behind a shared queue. Which lowers average latency more?</summary>

Faster code: service 7.5 ms, utilisation 45%, latency 7.5 ÷ 0.55 ≈ 13.6 ms, down from 10 ÷ 0.4 = 25 ms.
A second server at 30% each with a shared queue keeps service at 10 ms and almost removes waiting:
latency a little above 10 ms. The second server wins on latency; the faster code is cheaper and also
raises the ceiling. At higher utilisation the difference grows.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your pipeline is load balancer → app (1,500 req/s capacity) → cache (20,000) → database (600). You double the app servers. What happens to throughput and to latency?</summary>

Throughput stays at about 600 a second, the database's capacity. Latency at peak can get worse: the
extra app capacity lets more requests reach the database at once, building a longer queue there. The
fix is at the database (more capacity, fewer queries per request, better caching) or a limit on
concurrent database work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A batch window of 20 ms tripled peak throughput, but overnight median latency went from 3 ms to 23 ms. Why, and what is the fix?</summary>

At night most writes arrive alone and wait the full 20 ms for company that never comes. Batch only when
other writes are already waiting (send a lone write at once), or scale the window with the arrival rate.

</details>

## Recap

- **Latency** is time per request: network + waiting + service. **Throughput** is requests finished per
  second. **Utilisation** is arrival rate × service time.
- **Little's law**: in flight = throughput × latency. It sizes pools and shows that latency is capacity.
- With random arrivals, latency ≈ S ÷ (1 − u): 2× the work at 50%, 5× at 80%, 10× at 90%.
- **Variability** adds waiting for everyone; separating big and small work removes it.
- **One shared queue** beats one queue per server; a pipeline's throughput is its **slowest stage**.
- **Batching** trades latency for throughput at low load and buys both near capacity; batch only when
  others are waiting.
- Measure with **open** load tests and percentiles, over short windows.
