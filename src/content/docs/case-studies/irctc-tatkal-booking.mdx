## The question

At 10:00 in the morning, Tatkal booking opens for AC classes on tomorrow's trains, and on a popular
train thousands of people press "Book" in the same second for a few hundred berths. Yet the same
berth is never given to two people. Stranger still, one berth can carry three different passengers
on one trip: New Delhi to Agra, then Agra to Bhopal, then Bhopal to Nagpur. How does the system keep
track of that, and who gets the berth when two people ask for it at exactly the same moment?

The short answer: the system does not store a berth as one seat that is either sold or free. It
splits the route into the stretches between stops and remembers, for every berth, which stretches
are sold. To book, a server asks the database: "mark these stretches of this berth as sold, but only
if they are still free". The database handles requests for one berth one at a time, so when two
people want the same stretches, the first gets them and the second is told to try another berth.
The waitlist, the payments and the 10 AM rush all build on those two ideas.

## How it works, in plain words

Follow one passenger, Ravi. He wants a Tatkal berth in the 3A class (three-tier AC) on tomorrow's
train, from Agra Cantt to Bhopal. To keep the numbers small, we give the route eight stops; real
long-distance trains have more. With the railways' station codes, the stops are New Delhi (NDLS),
Mathura (MTJ), Agra Cantt (AGC), Gwalior (GWL), Jhansi (VGLJ), Bhopal (BPL), Itarsi (ET) and
Nagpur (NGP).

1. **Tatkal is a quota.** A **quota** is a share of a train's berths set aside for one kind of
   booking. The Tatkal quota is sold only from the day before the journey: from 10:00 for AC
   classes and 11:00 for the others. Say 16 berths in each 3A coach are Tatkal quota (an
   illustrative number; each train has its own). Ravi can only get one of those.
2. **The route is cut into stretches.** Eight stops have seven stretches between them:
   NDLS→MTJ, MTJ→AGC, AGC→GWL, GWL→VGLJ, VGLJ→BPL, BPL→ET and ET→NGP. Ravi's journey covers three
   of them: AGC→GWL, GWL→VGLJ and VGLJ→BPL.
3. **Each berth remembers its stretches.** Berth 23 in coach B2 is a Tatkal berth that is already
   sold twice: to someone travelling NDLS→AGC (the first two stretches) and to someone travelling
   BPL→NGP (the last two). So the database stores berth 23 as seven marks in a row: sold, sold,
   free, free, free, sold, sold.
4. **Ravi presses "Book" at 10:00:00.3.** A booking server asks the database for a 3A Tatkal berth
   whose three stretches from AGC to BPL are all free. Berth 23 qualifies: its three middle marks
   are free. (A passenger going MTJ→GWL could not have berth 23, because the stretch MTJ→AGC is
   sold.)
5. **Meera presses "Book" in the same millisecond**, for the same journey, AGC to BPL. Her request
   reaches a different booking server, which looks at the same berth and sees the same three free
   stretches. If both servers now wrote "sold", berth 23 would carry two passengers from AGC to BPL.
   This is the danger at the centre of the whole system.
6. **So the check and the take are one step.** Neither server checks in one step and writes in
   another. Each sends the database one instruction: "mark AGC→BPL as sold on berth 23, but only if
   those three stretches are still free". The database never applies two changes to the same row at
   the same time. It applies Ravi's first: the stretches were free, so they become sold. Then it
   applies Meera's: the stretches are now sold, so it changes nothing and reports "0 rows changed".
7. **Meera's server tries again.** It picks the next berth that fits, berth 31, sends the same kind
   of instruction for berth 31, and this time gets "1 row changed". Both of them have a berth, and
   the whole exchange took a few milliseconds.
8. **The berth is held, not yet sold.** Berth 23 is set aside for Ravi for 15 minutes
   (an illustrative limit) while he pays. If the payment arrives in time, the hold becomes a
   ticket. If not, a cleanup job marks the three stretches free again for the next person.
9. **When no berth fits**, the booking still goes through, as RAC (half a berth, shared) or on
   the waitlist. When someone cancels, the stretches they free go to waitlisted passengers whose
   journeys fit inside those stretches, which is why waitlist number 4 sometimes confirms before
   number 1.

That answers both halves of the question. One berth can carry several passengers because what is
sold is a stretch, not a berth. And two people never get the same stretch because "is it free?" and
"take it" happen as one step inside the database, for one request at a time on each berth.

Why is 10:00 hard, then? Not because of the total number of bookings. IRCTC, the Indian
Railways' online ticketing company, had its busiest minute so far on 16 August 2025, when 37,410
tickets were booked. A minute has 60 seconds, so that is 37,410 ÷ 60 = about 620 tickets a
second, across every train in India, and one database server can write that many rows. The hard
part is that at 10:00:00 almost all of the requests want the same few hundred berths on a handful
of popular trains, so they all queue up for the same rows. Part 2 shows how a system can survive
that: let people in at the rate the database can serve them, and keep the time each request spends
on a berth's row very short.

An everyday picture: a booking clerk with a paper chart, one line per berth, and a pencil mark for
each sold stretch. Two clerks working from two copies of the chart would sell the same berth to two
people; one chart that only one clerk can write on at a time cannot. The picture stops working in
one place: the database is not one clerk for the whole train. It is one clerk per berth row, all
working at once, so thousands of bookings for different berths go through in parallel.

## The building blocks

Six ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A segment

**What it is.** A **segment** is the stretch of the route between two stops that come one after
the other.

**Why it exists.** A berth is sold per segment, not per train. Whether a berth is free depends on
which segments your journey covers, so the system has to count in segments.

**Example.** Our route has 8 stops, so it has 7 segments. Programmers usually number things from
0, so the stops are 0 (NDLS) to 7 (NGP) and the segments are 0 (NDLS→MTJ) to 6 (ET→NGP). Ravi
travels from stop 2 (AGC) to stop 5 (BPL). He uses segments 2, 3 and 4: that is 5 − 2 = 3
segments, ending one before his last stop's number.

**The technical version.** A route with N stops has N − 1 segments. A journey from stop *a* to stop
*b* occupies segments *a* up to *b* − 1. Two journeys on the same berth clash exactly when they
share at least one segment.

### Bits and a bitmask

**What it is.** A **bit** is a single 0 or 1. A **bitmask** is a row of bits used as on/off
switches, one switch per thing you track.

**Why it exists.** For every berth we need seven sold/free marks, and we need to compare a journey
with a berth very quickly, millions of times a minute. A row of bits is the smallest way to store
the marks, and a computer can compare two rows of bits in a single step.

**Example.** Write 1 for sold and 0 for free, segment 0 first. Berth 23 is `1100011`. Ravi's
journey needs segments 2, 3 and 4, so it is `0011100`. Line them up and look for a position where
both rows have a 1:

```text
berth 23   1 1 0 0 0 1 1
Ravi       0 0 1 1 1 0 0
both 1?    0 0 0 0 0 0 0   no position is 1 in both: the berth fits
```

After Ravi's booking, berth 23 becomes `1111111`: sold on every segment.

**The technical version.** A computer stores a whole number as a row of bits, so a bitmask is kept
as an ordinary integer. "Is there a position where both are 1?" is the **bitwise AND** operation,
written `&`. Part 2 shows how the integer is built and the other operations we need.

### A transaction

**What it is.** A **transaction** is a group of database changes that either all happen or none
happen.

**Why it exists.** A booking makes more than one change: it marks the berth's segments as sold, and
it adds a row for the passenger. If the server crashed between the two, the berth would be marked
sold with no passenger on it, and nobody could ever use those segments.

**Example.** Ravi's booking runs as:

```sql
BEGIN;
UPDATE berth SET ... WHERE coach = 'B2' AND berth_no = 23;   -- mark segments 2-4 sold
INSERT INTO booking (...) VALUES (...);                        -- Ravi's passenger row
COMMIT;
```

If the server dies after the `UPDATE`, the database never sees `COMMIT` and throws the half-done
change away. That is called a **rollback**.

**The technical version.** This all-or-nothing property is the "A" (atomicity) in **ACID**, the
four guarantees transactional databases give. A transaction does not, by itself, stop another
transaction from reading and changing the same row at the same time. The next two blocks are about
that.

### A race, and a lost update

**What it is.** A **race** is a bug where the result depends on the exact timing of two requests
running at the same time.

**Why it matters here.** At 10:00:00, requests for the same berth arrive within the same
millisecond. A bug that needs two requests at once never shows up in testing and shows up every
Tatkal morning.

**Example.** Suppose each booking server did "read, then decide, then write" in separate steps:

| Time | Ravi's server | Meera's server |
|---|---|---|
| 10:00:00.301 | reads berth 23: AGC→BPL free | |
| 10:00:00.302 | | reads berth 23: AGC→BPL free |
| 10:00:00.315 | writes "sold", adds Ravi | |
| 10:00:00.318 | | writes "sold", adds Meera |

Both writes succeed. Berth 23 now has two passengers from AGC to BPL, and no error appeared anywhere.

**The technical version.** This pattern is a **lost update**: two transactions each read a value,
compute a change from what they read, and write it back, so the second write is based on a value
that was already out of date. The database sees two valid writes and has no way to know anything
went wrong.

### A row lock

**What it is.** A **row lock** is a mark the database puts on a row for one transaction, so that
any other transaction that wants to change that row waits until the first one finishes.

**Why it exists.** It turns "at the same time" into "one after the other" for each row, which is
what stops a lost update.

**Example.** Ravi's instruction "mark AGC→BPL sold on berth 23 if still free" locks berth 23's row
at 10:00:00.315. Meera's identical instruction arrives at .316 and waits. Ravi's transaction commits
at .317 and the lock is released. Meera's instruction now runs, reads the row as it is after Ravi's
change, finds the segments sold, and changes 0 rows. Meera waited one millisecond.

**The technical version.** An `UPDATE` takes the lock automatically on every row it changes.
`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` takes the same lock while reading. A lock is held until the transaction
commits or rolls back. A plain `SELECT` takes no lock and waits for nobody: in PostgreSQL and MySQL,
reading never waits for writing, and writing never waits for reading.

### A hold

**What it is.** A **hold** is a booking saved with the status "held" and an expiry time.

**Why it exists.** Paying takes minutes. If the booking kept berth 23's row locked while Ravi
typed his UPI PIN (UPI is India's instant bank-to-bank payment system), every other request that
touched that row would wait for minutes. A hold sets the segments aside as ordinary data, so the
lock can be released in milliseconds.

**Example.** At 10:00:00.317 Ravi's transaction commits: berth 23's segments are marked sold, and
his booking row says `HELD`, expires at 10:15:00. He pays at 10:03, and the row becomes
`CONFIRMED`. If he had closed the app instead, a cleanup job would have seen the expired hold
after 10:15 and marked segments 2 to 4 free again, in a new transaction.

**The technical version.** Locks last milliseconds and belong to the database; holds last minutes
and belong to your application, as a status column and an `expires_at` column. Part 2 shows what
happens when a payment arrives after its hold expired.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- A berth is sold per **segment**, the stretch between two stops, so one berth can carry several
  passengers on one trip.
- Each berth stores one sold/free bit per segment. A journey fits a berth when none of its segments
  is already sold on it.
- Two requests at the same moment can both see a berth as free. The fix is to make "check it is
  free" and "mark it sold" one instruction, which the database runs for one request at a time on
  each row; the loser gets "0 rows changed" and tries another berth.
- The berth is then **held** for a few minutes while the passenger pays, as data with an expiry, not
  as a lock.
- At 10 AM the problem is not the total number of bookings but thousands of them wanting the same
  few hundred rows at once.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 used one berth and two requests. Here is the real version of the question. Berth 23 is sold
NDLS→AGC and BPL→NGP. At 10:00:00.3 three requests arrive in the same millisecond: AGC→BPL, MTJ→GWL
and NDLS→NGP. How does the database decide which of them fits, for 1,300 berths, in a few
microseconds? When two requests both decide berth 23 is free, what exactly is locked, for how long,
and what happens to the loser? And the trick that removes waiting on locks turns out to waste the
most berths. Why?

IRCTC and CRIS (the railways' IT organisation that builds the reservation system) do not publish its
internals. What follows is how we would build a system like it, using the railways' published
rules (quotas, Tatkal timings, RAC, charting) wherever they shape the design.

## What the system has to do, in numbers

Two words in the table need a line each. A **PNR** (passenger name record) is one booking: one
ticket number that can carry several passengers. A **lakh** is the Indian word for 100,000, so 1.5
lakh is 150,000.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Peak tickets booked in one minute | 37,410, on 16 August 2025 ([IANS, June 2026](https://ianslive.in/irctc-delivers-58-crore-meals-a-year-handles-18-lakh-daily-tickets--20260603170101)) |
| That peak per second, across all trains | 37,410 ÷ 60 seconds = about 620 |
| Tatkal opens | 10:00 for AC classes, 11:00 for non-AC, one day before the journey ([All India Radio, June 2025](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/railways-announces-new-rules-for-tatkal-tickets-aadhaar-based-otp-authentication-must-from-july-1)) |
| Passengers per Tatkal PNR | at most 4 |
| Berths on one long-distance train | about 1,300 (illustrative: 18 sleeping coaches × about 72 berths = 1,296) |
| Stops on one route | 10 to 60, so 9 to 59 segments (one fewer than the stops) |
| Requests per ticket at the peak | 10 to 20 (illustrative: search, availability, passenger details, payment, retries) |
| Requests per second at the peak | 620 tickets × 10 to 20 requests = about 6,200 to 12,400 (illustrative) |

About 620 bookings a second is small for a database. The write rate is not the problem. Two other
things are:

1. **The writes are not spread out.** In the first seconds after 10:00 almost every write goes to
   a handful of popular trains, and within each train to one class and one quota. Many requests
   wanting the same rows at the same moment is called **contention**, and here it is concentrated
   on a few hundred rows, not spread across the database.
2. **A berth is not one unit of stock.** It is sold per segment, so every question about
   availability, locking, choosing a berth and the waitlist has to be asked per segment.

The hard requirements, in priority order: no segment of a berth is sold twice; every payment ends
in a ticket or a refund; and the booking rate stays at its maximum during the worst minute, instead
of collapsing.

## The big picture

Before the figure, one idea it depends on. When data is too big or too busy for one server, it is
split into **shards**: slices of the data, each living on its own server. The field that decides
which shard a row belongs to is the **partition key**. For example, if the partition key is "train
+ date", every row for train 12627 on 24 October lives on one shard, and every row for train 12628
on that date may live on another. A booking only ever touches one train on one date, so with this
key every row a booking needs is on one server, and no booking ever has to lock rows on two
servers at once.

Reading the figure box by box:

- **Web and app users, and counters.** Station booking counters and phones compete for the same
  berths. There is no separate web quota to hide behind.
- **Edge: bot checks, waiting room.** The **edge** is the layer of servers that every request hits
  first, before any of our own code. It filters out automated booking scripts (bots) and runs a
  **waiting room**, which lets users in at the rate the system behind it can serve.
- **Booking servers.** Many identical servers that run the booking logic. They are **stateless**:
  they keep no data of their own between requests, so any server can handle any request, and
  adding servers adds capacity.
- **Read model.** A **cache** of availability: a copy of data kept where it can be served
  quickly, without asking the source every time. Searches read "43 left" from it, and it is a few
  seconds old. If the
  count is two seconds stale, a booking fails and the user sees the new count. Nothing is oversold,
  because only the shard decides.
- **Inventory shard.** The one place that holds, for its train-dates, the berth bitmasks, the quotas
  and the waitlist, and the only place that decides who gets a berth.
- **Payment gateway.** The outside service that talks to banks, UPI and card networks.
- **Charting job.** A job that freezes the shard into the final passenger list hours before
  departure.

## The hard parts

### One berth, many journeys: the inventory model

Part 1 wrote a berth as `1100011`. This section shows how that becomes a number in a database row,
and how one instruction answers "does this journey fit?".

**How an integer holds bits.** A computer stores a whole number as a row of bits, and each position
is worth a power of two: bit 0 is worth 1, bit 1 is worth 2, bit 2 is worth 4, bit 3 is worth 8, and
so on, each double the one before. The number is the sum of the positions that are 1. Berth 23 has
segments 0, 1, 5 and 6 sold, so it is stored as 1 + 2 + 32 + 64 = 99.

On this page masks are written with segment 0 on the left, so that they line up with the route on a
map. That is the reverse of how binary numbers are usually printed (largest position on the left);
only the writing is reversed, not the number.

**Three operations on masks.** Each one works on every position of two masks at once, in a single
CPU instruction, and SQL databases support them on integers too:

| Operation | Rule at each position | What we use it for | Example (segment 0 first) |
|---|---|---|---|
| AND, `&` | 1 only where both are 1 | Do two sets of segments overlap? Zero means no | `1100011 & 0011100 = 0000000` |
| OR, `\|` | 1 where either is 1 | Mark a journey's segments as sold | `1100011 \| 0011100 = 1111111` |
| AND NOT, `& ~` | Clear the positions that are 1 on the right | Free a cancelled journey's segments | `1111111 & ~0011100 = 1100011` |

One more operation builds masks: a **shift**, written `x << k`, slides every bit k positions toward
the higher segments. So `1 << 3` is a single 1 at segment 3.

**The table.** Store each berth's occupancy as one integer column. A `BIGINT` is a 64-bit integer,
so it has room for 64 segments. (A **byte** is 8 bits, so a 64-bit integer takes 64 ÷ 8 = 8
bytes; a KB, kilobyte, is about a thousand bytes, and a GB about a million KB.)

```sql
CREATE TABLE berth (
  train_date  TEXT,     -- partition key: '12627/2026-10-24'
  coach       TEXT,     -- 'B2'
  berth_no    INT,      -- 23
  kind        TEXT,     -- LB, MB, UB, SL, SU
  quota       TEXT,     -- GN, TQ, PQ, LD, ...
  mask        BIGINT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,   -- bit i = segment i sold
  PRIMARY KEY (train_date, coach, berth_no)
);
```

The `kind` codes are the berth positions: lower, middle, upper, side lower, side upper. The `quota`
codes are general (GN), Tatkal (TQ), pooled (PQ) and ladies (LD).

**Building a journey's mask.** Ravi travels from stop 2 (AGC) to stop 5 (BPL), which is 5 − 2 = 3
segments. We need three 1s, starting at segment 2. Three steps get there:

| Step | Value | Mask (segment 0 first) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1 << 3` | 8 | `0001000` | a single 1 at position 3 (3 = the number of segments) |
| `− 1` | 7 | `1110000` | 8 − 1 = 7 = 1 + 2 + 4: three 1s at the bottom |
| `<< 2` | 28 | `0011100` | slide them up to start at position 2 (AGC): segments 2, 3 and 4 |

As a formula, for a journey from stop *a* to stop *b*: `need = ((1 << (b − a)) − 1) << a`.

With that, the three questions every booking asks are three one-line operations:

- the berth fits when `mask & need = 0` (no segment both sold and wanted);
- booking it is `mask = mask | need`;
- cancelling it is `mask = mask & ~need`.

The third request, NDLS→NGP, needs all seven segments, `1111111`, and ANDs to `1100011` with berth
23: four clashes, so it needs a berth that is empty for the whole route.

**Why not keep a "seats left" counter per journey?** Because availability depends on the journey.
With 40 stops, the number of different journeys is the number of ways to pick a start and a later
end: 40 choices of start × 39 choices of end = 1,560, halved because the end must come after the
start, which gives 780. One booking from stop 3 to stop 9 changes the count for every journey that
overlaps segments 3 to 8, which is hundreds of counters updated in one transaction, all of them
contended. With bitmasks, nothing is stored but the masks, and availability for a journey is
computed when someone asks:

```sql
SELECT count(*) FROM berth
WHERE train_date = $1 AND coach LIKE 'B%' AND quota = 'TQ'
  AND mask & $need = 0;
```

That scans a few hundred integers (one per Tatkal 3A berth on the train). Comparing one integer
takes a few nanoseconds (billionths of a second), so the scan takes a few **microseconds**: a
microsecond (µs) is a millionth of a second, and 1,000 µs make 1 ms. This is what the read model
caches for popular journeys.

A 64-bit mask covers 64 segments, which is a route of 64 + 1 = 65 stops. Longer routes use two
integers, or a bit-string column type (PostgreSQL's `bit varying` supports `&` and `|`). Only stops
where the train actually halts get a segment number.

**Quotas are a property of the berth.** Each berth carries a quota tag, and the quota decides which
requests may look at it: a Tatkal request scans only `TQ` berths. Some quotas are kept for
passengers who board or leave at particular stations along the route. In this model such a berth is
still one mask; its quota rule only filters which journeys may use it.

### What exactly gets locked

Every correct booking does three things: find a berth whose mask fits, set the bits, insert the
passenger rows. Part 1 showed the race this invites and the row lock that puts the two requests in order. Here are
the precise rules, then four ways to write the transaction.

**Isolation levels.** An **isolation level** is a database setting that decides how much one
transaction sees of other transactions' changes while it runs. It exists because seeing less is
safer but makes transactions wait or fail more often, so databases let you choose. PostgreSQL's
default is **read committed**: every statement sees the data that was committed at the moment that
statement started. So between your `SELECT` at 10:00:00.301 and your `UPDATE` at .315, another
transaction can commit a change to berth 23, and your `UPDATE` will not notice unless the
`UPDATE` itself checks. MySQL's default is **repeatable read**, where a plain `SELECT` keeps seeing
the data as it was at the transaction's first read, even after others commit. For this race it
behaves the same way, because the naive write never re-checks anything.

**Two bets: pessimistic and optimistic.** Built from row locks, there are two ways to stop the lost
update, and each is a bet about how often collisions happen:

- **Pessimistic** locking bets a collision is likely, so it locks the row *before* reading the value
  it will decide on (`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`). The second transaction waits behind the first and,
  when its turn comes, reads the new value. Nobody decides on stale data; the price is waiting.
- **Optimistic** locking bets a collision is rare, so it reads without a lock and checks at write
  time that nothing it depended on has changed. The classic check is a **version column**, a
  number that goes up by one on every change. For example,
  `UPDATE berth SET mask = ..., version = 8 WHERE id = 23 AND version = 7` changes one row if the
  berth is still at version 7, and zero rows if someone else committed version 8 in between. Zero
  rows means "you lost; read again and retry". Nobody waits; the price is retries.

Here are the same two transactions, Ravi's and Meera's, with no locking, with the pessimistic bet
and with the optimistic one:

|---|---|---|
| Lock taken | Before reading, until commit | Only during the final `UPDATE`, until commit |
| The loser | Waits, then reads the new value | Gets 0 rows, re-reads and retries |
| Cheap when | Collisions are frequent and the work inside is short | Collisions are rare |
| Hurts when | Work inside the lock is slow: the line of waiters grows; locking several rows can leave two transactions waiting for each other (a deadlock, below) | Many requests want the same row: retries pile up and repeat work |

The booking problem sits in the awkward middle: at most times of day collisions are rare, and at
10:00:00 thousands of transactions want the same few hundred rows.

**Wrong: read, work, write.** Read a fitting berth, compute the fare and build the passenger rows,
then write `mask = mask | need`. This is the race from Part 1 in SQL. Under the defaults of both
databases, two transactions read the same old mask, both decide berth 23 fits, and both write. The
second `OR` does not fail: it sets the same bits again. The only symptom is two passenger rows on
one segment.

There are four correct ways to write the transaction. Here they are racing on the same burst of
requests for one coach:

<LockRaceSim />

**1. Lock the coach (pessimistic).** `SELECT ... FROM berth WHERE coach = 'B2' FOR UPDATE` locks
every row the query reads, so the next transaction waits until the first commits. It never
oversells, and it is the first thing most people write. Its cost is that the coach becomes a
single-file line. With 15 ms of work inside each transaction, the 12th request has 11 requests
ahead of it, so it waits 11 × 15 ms = 165 ms before it even starts. At Tatkal volume, requests join
the line faster than it empties. The lock covers the whole coach for a decision that touches one
row.

**2. Conditional update (optimistic).** Do the slow work first, then make the check and the take
one statement. This is the instruction from Part 1, step 6:

```sql
UPDATE berth SET mask = mask | $need
WHERE train_date = $1 AND coach = $2 AND berth_no = $3
  AND mask & $need = 0;          -- 1 row: it is yours. 0 rows: someone beat you; pick again.
```

This is optimistic locking with a better check than a version column. A version column fails the
write whenever *anything* on the berth changed, including a booking of MTJ→AGC, which does not touch
Ravi's segments at all. The condition `mask & $need = 0` fails only if one of *Ravi's* segments was
taken in between, so unrelated bookings on the same berth never cause a retry.

Why is it safe at read committed, when the naive version was not? In PostgreSQL, when an `UPDATE`
finds that another transaction has changed its row, or is in the middle of changing it, it waits for
that transaction to finish, then re-checks the `WHERE` clause against the new version of the row
before updating. In MySQL, an `UPDATE` always reads the latest committed version of the row and locks it. Either way,
Meera's `UPDATE` sees Ravi's bits, the condition is false, and it updates zero rows.

At PostgreSQL's repeatable read the same race ends differently: instead of re-checking, the database
refuses with a **serialization failure**. SQL databases report errors as a five-character code
called a SQLSTATE, and this one is `40001`. Your code must catch it and retry the transaction.

The row lock is held only from the `UPDATE` to `COMMIT`, which is microseconds if nothing slow
happens in between. The cost shows in the sim: everyone's first choice is the same "best" berth, so
the losers retry, and retries grow with the number of requests for the same journey at once.

**3. `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` (pessimistic, without the line).** Ask for a free berth and lock it,
but skip rows that other transactions have locked instead of waiting for them:

```sql
SELECT coach, berth_no FROM berth
WHERE train_date = $1 AND quota = 'TQ' AND mask & $need = 0
ORDER BY coach, berth_no
LIMIT 1
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;
```

Twelve transactions at once get twelve different berths, with no waiting and no retries; job queues
use this pattern to hand out work. There are two catches. When every fitting row is locked by
someone else, the query returns nothing, so a request can be told "sold out" while a berth it could
have had is held by a transaction that is about to roll back. And the berth you get is "the first
unlocked one", not the best one, which matters a lot in the next section.

**4. A single writer per train-date (no locks at all).** Locks exist because several threads may
change the same rows at once. A **thread** is one sequence of instructions running inside a
program; a server runs many threads at once so it can serve many requests together. Remove the
"several" and the locks go with it: send every write for one train and date to one thread that owns
that shard's masks in memory, and let it process requests one at a time from a **queue**, a line of waiting requests served in
arrival order. No two
decisions can overlap, so there is nothing to lock.

Memory is lost in a crash, so before replying "booked", the thread appends the decision to a
**durable log**: a file of decisions in order, from which the masks can be rebuilt by replaying
it. "Durable" has a precise meaning. Writing to a file only puts the bytes in the operating
system's memory; they reach the disk later. The **system call** (a request from a program to the operating system) **`fsync`** forces them onto the disk
and waits until the disk confirms, which takes far longer than a mask scan.

So the thread's speed is set by the disk, not by the deciding. Deciding one booking is a scan of a
few hundred integers, about 1 to 2 µs. A second has 1,000,000 µs, so even at the slower 2 µs one
thread could decide 1,000,000 ÷ 2 = 500,000 bookings a second, while the whole train has only 1,300
berths.

Now add the disk. Say one fsync takes 0.5 ms (an illustrative figure; it depends on the disk). With
one fsync per booking, a second of 1,000 ms allows at most 1,000 ÷ 0.5 = 2,000 bookings. That is
500,000 ÷ 2,000 = 250 times fewer than the deciding alone allows.

The fix is **group commit**: collect every decision made in the last 2 ms, write them together,
call fsync once, then reply to all of them. If 500 bookings arrive in those 2 ms, they cost one
disk flush instead of 500, and each waits at most 2 ms longer. For **failover** (another server
taking over when this one dies), a standby server reads the same log and replays it, so it holds the
same masks and can take over. This is the design we would choose for the shard in the architecture
diagram: the contention is naturally per train, and this removes it instead of managing it. The
same idea, one thread processing an ordered input log, was described by the LMAX trading exchange
in 2011.

**What about `SERIALIZABLE`?** **Serializable** is the strictest isolation level: the database
promises that running transactions at the same time gives the same result as running them one after
another in some order. PostgreSQL keeps that promise by letting transactions run without waiting,
recording which rows each one read and wrote, and at commit aborting any transaction whose reads and
writes cannot fit into a one-at-a-time order. Ravi's and Meera's naive transactions cannot be put in
any order (whichever went second would have seen the berth sold), so one of them is aborted with a
serialization failure. It is correct, and it lets you write the naive code. The cost appears under
exactly this workload: every transaction that read the same coach may conflict, so at 10:00 most of
them abort and retry, and each retry repeats the slow work. Serializable is a good default for
business logic where collisions are rare; for a known hot spot, a conditional update says what you
mean and fails cheaply.

**Make the booking request itself idempotent.** An operation is **idempotent** when doing it twice
has the same effect as doing it once. It matters because a user on a slow network taps "Book"
twice, or the app retries after a timeout, and without protection that is two holds on two berths
for one person; at Tatkal time the second hold takes a berth from someone else. The fix: the app
generates one request ID per booking attempt, say `r-7f3a`, and sends it with every retry. The
bookings table has a unique constraint on the request ID, so a second insert with `r-7f3a` fails,
and the server returns the first booking instead of choosing a berth again.

:::caution[Group bookings and deadlocks]
A Tatkal PNR can carry four passengers: four berths in one transaction. A **deadlock** is two
transactions each waiting for a lock the other holds, so neither can ever continue. If request A
locks berth 21 and then wants 22, while request B locked 22 and wants 21, that is a deadlock. The
database detects it and kills one of them (PostgreSQL checks after `deadlock_timeout`, 1 s by
default). Always lock rows in one fixed order (coach, then berth number), or take all four with one
statement and check that it updated four rows, rolling back otherwise.
:::

**Never hold a lock across a payment.** This is the hold from Part 1, and now the reason is
concrete: a row lock held for the one to five minutes a payment takes would make every conditional
update on that berth wait for minutes. The booking transaction commits the hold (bits set, status
`HELD`, an `expires_at`), which releases the lock. If payment does not arrive in time, an expiry job
clears the bits in a new short transaction.

### Choosing which berth

The lock strategies above decide *whether* two requests collide. Allocation decides *which* berth
each one gets, and it has three goals that conflict.

**Goal 1: keep long free runs whole.** Put each journey where it leaves the smallest free gap
around it. This is the **best-fit** rule. Memory allocators, the part of a program that hands out
pieces of memory, use it: given free gaps of 8 KB, 3 KB and 20 KB, a 3 KB request goes into the 3 KB
gap, so the 20 KB gap stays whole for a large request later. Here the gaps are runs of free segments
on each berth. A MTJ→AGC passenger should go on a berth that is already sold NDLS→MTJ and AGC
onwards, not on an empty berth, which it would split into two shorter pieces nobody may want.

Here is the cost of ignoring it, on the same journeys. The top grid uses best-fit; the bottom one
puts each journey on any berth that fits, at random:

With ten short journeys first and eight end-to-end requests after, packing turns away 4 of the 8
end-to-end passengers. Spreading the same journeys over any fitting berth turns away 6 of the 8, and
leaves almost twice as many cells empty: 28 against 15. To check this was not one lucky run, we ran
the same setup 500 times with different random journeys: on average, packing still had room for
3.9 of the 8 end-to-end passengers, and random spreading for 1.4.

Spreading is exactly what `SKIP LOCKED` and "pick a random free berth to avoid contention" do.
**The trick that removes waiting on locks is the one that wastes the most berths.** This is the
strongest argument for the single-writer shard: one thread can run best-fit over the whole train
with no contention at all.

**Goal 2: respect the passenger.** Preferences (lower, side, window) are soft: the system tries but
may not manage. Some are rules: the reservation system automatically considers senior citizens and
women aged 45 and over for lower berths even if they did not ask, subject to availability, and each
coach keeps a small number of lower berths for them, about 6–7 in Sleeper, 4–5 in 3A and 3–4 in
2A (two-tier AC) ([Lok Sabha answer, 2025](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/3013644/1/AU3199_LYz0sV.pdf)).
A group of four wants the same **bay** (the block of berths facing each other) of one coach, not four
coaches.

**Goal 3: be fast and give the same answer every time.** The allocation runs while the row is
being decided, so it must be a short scan with a score, not a search. A workable score for each
candidate berth, lowest wins:

1. the free run left around the journey (tightest fit first);
2. a penalty if the passenger needs a lower berth and this is not one;
3. a penalty if this berth is not in the same bay as the rest of the group;
4. the berth number, to break ties the same way every time.

For a group, allocate the passengers together: try each bay, score the bay as a whole, and take
the best bay that fits all four; only if no bay fits, fall back to the best individual berths.

Seat choice changes the contention picture. The next-generation reservation system that the
railways began moving to in August 2026 is reported to let passengers choose their seat as airlines
do, and to target over 1.5 lakh (150,000) bookings a minute
([Tribune, 2026](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/india/just-like-airlines-now-you-can-choose-your-seat-when-travelling-by-train),
[PIB, August 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/aug/doc202686945401.pdf)).
How that system allocates is not public. In our model, letting thousands of people click the same
lower berths at 10:00 turns those rows into hot spots, rows that many requests hit at once: conditional updates fail far more often, and
the interface needs a fast fallback ("that berth is gone; we gave you B2-31, also lower").

### RAC, waitlist and charting

When no berth fits, the booking can still succeed, as **RAC** (reservation against cancellation)
or on the **waitlist** (WL). Both are queues with segment ranges, and both have races that a naive
design gets wrong.

**RAC is half a berth.** An RAC passenger shares a side lower berth (the berth along the corridor,
which is a seat by day) with another RAC passenger
([Deccan Herald, November 2025](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/indian-railways-reshuffles-rules-for-booking-lower-berth-seats-all-you-need-to-know-3782790)).
Model each side lower berth set aside for RAC as two **slots**, each with its own mask. An RAC
booking is then the same allocation problem on slots instead of berths. Promotion has an order: a
freed berth goes to an RAC passenger first, and the slot that passenger leaves goes to the first
waitlisted passenger who fits.

**The waitlist is not first in, first out.** A cancellation frees some segments of one berth. Only
waitlisted journeys that fit entirely inside those segments can use them. In the figure, the
AGC→BPL passenger cancels, freeing segments 2 to 4:

<Figure caption="The AGC–BPL passenger cancels. WL 1 needs the whole route and WL 3 starts one stop early, so neither fits. WL 2 and WL 4 do, and confirm ahead of them. Waitlist numbers are an order of consideration, not a promise of order of confirmation.">
  <WaitlistSegments />
</Figure>

So the promotion step is: walk the waitlist in order, give each entry the best-fitting freed range
it fits in, and stop when the freed bits are used up. This is why a WL 12 ticket can confirm while
WL 3 does not, which passengers often take as a bug.

**The race: a fresh booking against the waitlist.** When a passenger cancels at 10:00:05, both the
promotion job and a brand-new Tatkal request want the freed bits. If the cancellation clears the
bits in one transaction and the promotion runs in a later one, the new booking can slip in between,
and the people who have been waiting lose a berth they were owed. Two fixes: do the cancellation
and the promotions **in the same transaction**, so the bits are never visibly free; or, with the
single-writer shard, process the cancellation and its promotions as one event before taking the
next request.

**Caps.** The railways cap the waitlist at 60% of the berths in AC classes and 30% in non-AC
classes, and officials have said only about 20–25% of waitlisted tickets usually confirm
([PTI via The Week, June 2025](https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/national/2025/06/29/del48-rail-enhances-waiting-list.html)).
For a 3A class with 100 berths on a train, 60% of 100 = 60 waitlist places. The cap is a limit on
the length of the queue per train, class and quota, so it must be checked in the same transaction
that adds to the queue; checked anywhere else, two requests at once both see 59 and both add one.

**Charting is a batch job (a job that runs once over all the data) that turns live state into a
fixed list.** The first chart is prepared at
least 8 hours before departure
([All India Radio, 2025](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/railway-board-to-prepare-reservation-charts-8-hours-prior-to-train-departure));
a second chart is prepared up to 30 minutes before departure
([Deccan Herald, 2020](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/railways-to-restore-system-of-issuing-second-reservation-charts-30-minutes-before-departure-of-trains-898230.html)).
At charting, online tickets that are still fully waitlisted are cancelled and refunded
([IANS, 2023](https://ianslive.in/navigating-railway-ticket-cancellations--20231130124042)). In our
model, charting is: stop accepting waitlist entries, run the final promotions, take a snapshot (a
frozen copy) of the masks, generate the chart from the snapshot, and switch the shard into a "current booking" mode
that sells only from the leftover bits. Taking the snapshot inside one transaction, or as one event
on the single writer, is what makes the chart agree with the last booking before it.

### Routing every write to one owner

The single-writer design and the read model both depend on two things: knowing which shard owns a
train-date, and keeping the read side close to the truth.

**How many owners?** Bookings open 60 days ahead
([All India Radio, 2024](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/railways-reduces-time-limit-for-advance-ticket-booking-from-120-to-60-days)).
Say 3,000 reserved train services run each day (an illustrative number). Then 3,000 trains × 60
days = 180,000 train-dates are open for booking at any time. Each needs its masks (1,300 berths × 8
bytes = 10,400 bytes, about 10 KB) plus its bookings and waitlist, say 300 KB in all (illustrative).
In total that is 180,000 × 300 KB = 54,000,000 KB. A GB is about a million KB, so that is
54,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = about 54 GB, which fits in the memory of a few
ordinary servers.

**Which shard owns a train-date?** The usual tool is a **hash function**: a recipe that turns any
input into a number that looks random but is always the same for the same input. It exists so that
keys spread evenly over shards without a lookup table. For example, the text `12627/2026-10-24`
might hash to 9,071. The obvious mapping is `hash mod N`: the remainder after dividing by the number
of shards. With 4 shards, 9,071 mod 4 = 3 (9,071 = 4 × 2,267 + 3), so shard 3.

That mapping has a flaw. Add a fifth shard and 9,071 mod 5 = 1 (9,071 = 5 × 1,814 + 1): the
train-date moves to shard 1. Most train-dates move like this. Look at the hash values 0 to 19: only
0, 1, 2 and 3 give the same remainder for 4 and for 5, so 4 out of every 20 values stay, which is 1
in 5. The other 16 out of 20, which is 4 in 5, change shards at once.

**Consistent hashing** avoids that. Picture all possible hash values arranged on a circle. Each
shard is placed at a few points on the circle, and each train-date belongs to the next shard point
clockwise from its own hash. Adding a fifth shard only takes over the train-dates that fall right
before its new points, about one in five, and the rest stay where they were. The booking servers
keep the result in a cached routing table.

**Hot shards are expected, not a bug.** At 10:00 the train-dates for tomorrow's popular trains are
hot, and everything else is quiet. Because each train-date has one owner, a hot one cannot be split
across machines. It does not need to be: one train has about 1,300 berths to choose from, and the
single writer decides among them in memory, in microseconds per booking. What must not happen is two hot train-dates
landing on one weak shard. Assign tomorrow's known popular trains to separate shards the evening
before, which the railway can do because it knows its own timetable.

**Keeping the read model fresh.** The shard publishes every committed change (berth, bits, quota) to
a **change stream**: an ordered feed of changes that other systems can read, such as a Kafka topic
(Kafka is a widely used system for such feeds) or the database's own change log. Here the durable
log can be the change stream. The read model applies the changes and recomputes availability only
for the journeys people actually search. Computing all 780 journeys per class per train on every
change is wasted work; computing "AGC→BPL, 3A, TQ" when someone searches it, then caching it for a
second or two, costs one scan. Under the Tatkal spike, that cache answers thousands of identical
searches per popular journey.

### Surviving 10:00:00

At 10:00:00 a popular train's shard gets most of its day's booking requests in a few seconds, and
the site gets many times its normal searches. The railways' own controls work on the demand. AC and
non-AC Tatkal open an hour apart. Booking agents are barred for the first 30 minutes. Since July
2025, Tatkal users must verify themselves with Aadhaar, India's national identity number, and an
**OTP** (a one-time password sent to the phone)
([All India Radio, June 2025](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/railways-announces-new-rules-for-tatkal-tickets-aadhaar-based-otp-authentication-must-from-july-1)).
The Ministry has said bots made up to half the login attempts in the first five minutes of Tatkal
before its anti-bot work ([IANS, June 2025](https://ianslive.in/indian-railways-ai-system-cracks-down-on-25-crore-suspicious-user-ids--20250604161204)).

On the system side, four mechanisms matter, in order:

1. **Admission control.** **Admission control** means deciding at the door how many requests to let
   in, so the system behind the door only gets work it can finish. Here, the waiting room at the
   edge gives each user a **signed** place in line (a token the edge can check it issued, so nobody
   can forge an earlier place) and lets users through at the rate the shards can commit. A user
   turned away by the waiting room costs the edge a few bytes; a user turned away by the database
   cost it a transaction.
2. **Fail fast in memory.** Once the Tatkal quota of 3A on a train is gone for most journeys, the
   shard keeps a "sold out" summary per class and quota and rejects requests without touching a row.
   Take a train with 96 Tatkal 3A berths and 20,000 interested users (illustrative). At most 96 of
   the 20,000 can get a berth; 96 ÷ 20,000 = 0.0048, which is 0.48%. The other 100% − 0.48% = 99.52% of
requests end at the summary, without touching a row.
3. **Reads off the write path.** Availability comes from the read model. A stale count costs one
   failed booking attempt; reading the shard for every search would cost the shard.
4. **No retries from the client.** A booking that loses a race is retried inside the shard (pick
   the next berth), never by asking the user to resubmit the whole flow, which would multiply the
   requests.

### Payments: when money leaves but no ticket arrives

The berth is in the railway's database; the money is in the passenger's bank. No transaction can
cover both, because they are different systems run by different organisations.

A **saga** is the pattern for a business action that spans such systems: a sequence of steps, each
committed on its own, where every step that can be undone has a **compensating step** that undoes
it if a later step fails. It exists because the all-or-nothing of a transaction is not available
across systems, so you build it from steps and undo-steps. The booking is a small saga: hold the
berth (a local commit), take the payment (at the bank), then confirm the booking (a local commit).
If the payment succeeded but the confirmation cannot happen, the compensation is a refund.

The bank tells us about a payment through a **callback**: the payment gateway calls a URL on our
server to report "payment `P-88213` succeeded". The trouble is timing. The hold and the payment run
on two separate clocks:

<Figure caption="The hold and the payment run on two clocks. If the bank's confirmation lands after the hold expired and the bits were given to someone else, money has moved and the berth has not, until reconciliation settles it.">
  <PaymentGap />
</Figure>

In the figure, the hold runs from 10:00 to 10:15, the money leaves at 10:02, and the bank's
confirmation arrives at 10:16, one minute after the berth went back. There are four failures to
design for:

- **Late success.** The callback arrives after the hold expired. Try the allocation again for the
  same journey; if a berth fits, confirm; otherwise refund at once and record why.
- **Duplicate callback.** The gateway sends the same callback twice. The handler looks up the
  payment ID first, and a repeat gets the same answer and does nothing. This is an
  [idempotency key](/messaging/idempotency-keys/): the payment ID is the primary key of an outcomes
  table.
- **Lost callback.** The callback never arrives. **Reconciliation** is a job that compares our
  records with the gateway's: every few minutes it asks the gateway for successful payments and
  settles any it has no outcome for, through the same handler.
- **Ambiguous timeout.** The app does not know whether the payment went through. It asks the server
  for the status by payment ID, and never retries the payment itself.

The bank side has its own deadline: the Reserve Bank of India's rule for failed transactions, issued
in 2019 and still the rule in force, requires failed debits to be reversed within one to five days
depending on the payment method, with compensation of ₹100 a day after that
([RBI, 2019](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=11693)).

:::danger[Every path to "paid" goes through one gate]
During an incident, the tempting fix for a stuck booking is to mark it paid by hand. When the
delayed callback or the reconciliation job then arrives, the same payment produces a second ticket,
or a refund of money that bought a berth. Fix stuck payments only by replaying them through the
idempotent handler.
:::

## When things go wrong

| Failure | What users see | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Expiry job runs 10 minutes late | Berths held by abandoned sessions show "sold out" | Check `expires_at` when reading too: an expired hold counts as free even before the job runs |
| Read model 30 s out of date | Users see availability, then the booking fails | Accept it; show the fresh count from the failed attempt |
| Shard's main server dies at 10:00:02 | Bookings for that train stall | The standby replays the log and takes over; requests being processed carry request IDs, so retries do not double-book |
| Flood of callbacks after a bank outage | Thousands of late successes | The late-success path rebooks or refunds; reconciliation catches the rest |
| Cancellation and promotion in two transactions | A new booking takes a waitlisted passenger's berth | One transaction, or one event on the single writer |

## Build a piece of it

The inventory model and the conditional update, against SQLite (a small database that runs inside
the program): eight berths, seven segments, 40 requests at once for overlapping journeys, each
doing 20 ms of work between choosing a berth and writing it. The naive version reads, waits, then
ORs the bits in; the correct one makes the check part of the `UPDATE` and chooses again when it gets
zero rows.

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="berth_allocator.py"
# runnable version: examples/irctc-tatkal-booking/python/berth_allocator.py
def need(a: int, b: int) -> int:
    """Bits a..b-1 set: the segments a journey from station a to station b occupies."""
    return ((1 << (b - a)) - 1) << a


def waste(mask: int, a: int, b: int) -> int:
    """How much free run is left around the journey on this berth. Smaller = tighter fit."""
    lo, hi = a, b
    while lo > 0 and not mask >> (lo - 1) & 1:
        lo -= 1
    while hi < SEGMENTS and not mask >> hi & 1:
        hi += 1
    return (a - lo) + (hi - b)


def best_berth(db: sqlite3.Connection, a: int, b: int, senior: bool) -> int | None:
    rows = db.execute("SELECT id, kind, mask FROM berth WHERE mask & ? = 0", (need(a, b),)).fetchall()
    # Tightest fit first; seniors get lower berths first; then by berth number.
    rows.sort(key=lambda r: (waste(r[2], a, b), senior and r[1] != "LB", r[0]))
    return rows[0][0] if rows else None


async def book(db: sqlite3.Connection, a: int, b: int, senior: bool, stats: dict) -> int | None:
    n = need(a, b)
    berth_id = best_berth(db, a, b, senior)  # what the user was shown
    await asyncio.sleep(0.02)  # the same slow work; the choice above is now stale
    while berth_id is not None:
        with db:  # one short transaction: the check and the take are one statement
            taken = db.execute(
                "UPDATE berth SET mask = mask | ? WHERE id = ? AND mask & ? = 0", (n, berth_id, n)
            ).rowcount
            if taken:
```

```js title="berth-allocator.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/irctc-tatkal-booking/js/berth-allocator.mjs
const need = (a, b) => ((1 << (b - a)) - 1) << a;

/** How much free run is left around the journey on this berth. Smaller = tighter fit. */
function waste(mask, a, b) {
  let lo = a;
  let hi = b;
  while (lo > 0 && !((mask >> (lo - 1)) & 1)) lo--;
  while (hi < SEGMENTS && !((mask >> hi) & 1)) hi++;
  return a - lo + (hi - b);
}

function bestBerth(db, a, b, senior) {
  const rows = db.prepare('SELECT id, kind, mask FROM berth WHERE mask & ? = 0').all(need(a, b));
  // Tightest fit first; seniors get lower berths first; then by berth number.
  const key = (r) => [waste(r.mask, a, b), senior && r.kind !== 'LB' ? 1 : 0, r.id];
  rows.sort((x, y) => key(x).reduce((d, v, i) => d || v - key(y)[i], 0));
  return rows.length ? rows[0].id : null;
}

async function book(db, a, b, senior, stats) {
  const n = need(a, b);
  let berthId = bestBerth(db, a, b, senior); // what the user was shown
  await sleep(20); // the same slow work; the choice above is now stale
  while (berthId !== null) {
    db.exec('BEGIN'); // one short transaction: the check and the take are one statement
    const taken = db.prepare('UPDATE berth SET mask = mask | ? WHERE id = ? AND mask & ? = 0').run(n, berthId, n).changes;
    if (taken) {
      db.prepare('INSERT INTO booking (berth_id, from_idx, to_idx) VALUES (?, ?, ?)').run(berthId, a, b);
      db.exec('COMMIT');
      return berthId;
    }
    db.exec('COMMIT');
    stats.retries++; // an overlapping journey got this berth first: re-read, choose again
    berthId = bestBerth(db, a, b, senior);
  }
  return null;
}
```

Read it like this. `need` builds the journey's mask, as in the three-step table. `waste` walks left
and right from the journey along the berth's free segments and counts how many free segments would
be left over around it: that is the best-fit score. `best_berth` fetches every berth that fits and
sorts by that score. `book` chooses a berth, does 20 ms of other work (by which time the choice may
be stale), then runs the conditional `UPDATE`. If it changed one row, the berth is taken and the
passenger row is added in the same transaction; if it changed zero rows, someone else got there
first, so it counts a retry and chooses again.

```text title="python berth_allocator.py (node berth-allocator.mjs prints the same)"
8 berths x 7 segments (NDLS..NGP); 40 concurrent requests, 20 ms of work each

naive    40 of 40 booked, 276 overlapping pairs,  0 retries
correct  29 of 40 booked,   0 overlapping pairs, 34 retries

bitmask check: berth booked NDLS-AGC 1100000, journey AGC-BPL needs 0011100, AND = 0 -> it fits
```

The naive version "booked" all 40 by stacking journeys on top of each other: 276 pairs of
passengers share a segment of a berth. The correct version booked the 29 that fit and turned the
other 40 − 29 = 11 away, with 34 cheap retries caused by stale choices. The check that counted the
overlapping pairs is worth keeping as a production check that should always find nothing: two
bookings `x` and `y` on one berth overlap when `x.from_idx < y.to_idx AND y.from_idx < x.to_idx`
(each starts before the other ends).

The payment side (holds that expire, an idempotent callback, reconciliation) is in `seat_hold.py`
and `seat-hold.mjs` in the same folder. Full code and run instructions:
[`examples/irctc-tatkal-booking/`](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/irctc-tatkal-booking).

## Trade-offs, and what we'd change

- **Single writer per train-date vs row locks in a shared database.** The single writer removes
  contention and makes best-fit free, at the cost of building failover, log replay and routing
  yourself. Row locks reuse the database's machinery and are simpler to operate, at the cost of
  contention on popular trains and pressure to spread allocations. Below a few thousand bookings at
  once per train, the conditional update in PostgreSQL is the simpler choice.
- **Bitmask per berth vs one row per berth-segment.** A row per segment makes availability a
  `GROUP BY` and a booking a multi-row update with more locks. The bitmask keeps a booking to one
  row and one statement, and needs care only for routes past 64 segments.
- **Best-fit vs fast.** Best-fit costs a scan over candidate berths while the decision is being
  made. On 1,300 berths that is microseconds; on a 50,000-seat stadium it would need an index of
  free runs.
- **Cancellation with promotion in one transaction.** Correct and simple, but a cancellation now
  does work that grows with the length of the waitlist. The waitlist cap keeps that bounded.
- **At 10× the scale**, the shard per train-date still holds; what breaks first is the read side and
  the edge. **At a tenth of the scale**, one PostgreSQL server with conditional updates does all of
  this.

## Interview corner

"Design a train ticket booking system" is a favourite because it looks like a seat-booking problem
and is not. A strong 45 minutes:

1. Requirements and numbers (5 min): partial journeys, quotas, waitlist, the 10 AM spike.
2. Data model (10 min): berth with a segment bitmask, why counters fail, availability as a query.
3. Concurrency (15 min): the lost update, then conditional update vs `SKIP LOCKED` vs single writer,
   with their costs.
4. Allocation and waitlist (10 min): best-fit, group bookings, segment-aware promotion.
5. Spike and payments (5 min): admission control, holds, idempotent callbacks.

The follow-ups interviewers usually ask:

- **"Why not lock the row while the user is paying?"** Because a lock is held by a database
  connection for minutes, makes every writer of that row wait, and vanishes if the connection dies.
  A hold is data: bits set, a status and an expiry, released by a job.
- **"How do you handle two passengers booking four berths each in opposite order?"** Lock in a
  fixed order by (coach, berth), or one multi-row conditional `UPDATE` with a row-count check.
- **"Where is your bottleneck at 10:00?"** Not the write rate. Contention on one train's rows, then
  reads and retries; the answer is partitioning by train-date and admission control.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A berth's mask on a 7-segment route is 1000110 (segment 0 first). Which of these fit: NDLS→MTJ (segment 0), MTJ→GWL (segments 1–2), GWL→ET (segments 3–5)?</summary>

Only MTJ→GWL. Read left to right, 1000110 has segments 0, 4 and 5 sold. NDLS→MTJ needs segment 0:
clash. MTJ→GWL needs segments 1 and 2 (0110000): no position is 1 in both, so it fits. GWL→ET needs
segments 3 to 5 (0001110): clash on 4 and 5. Reading masks by eye is error-prone, which is why the
check is one `&` in code.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. You switch from a coach-level FOR UPDATE to FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED with ORDER BY berth_no. Latency drops. A week later, the business reports more end-to-end passengers turned away on long routes. Why?</summary>

SKIP LOCKED gives each request the first unlocked berth that fits, not the tightest fit. Short
journeys get spread over many berths, cutting free runs into short pieces, so fewer berths stay free
for the whole route. Fix it with best-fit ordering inside a single writer, or score candidates by
fit and use the conditional update.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your booking transaction runs at PostgreSQL REPEATABLE READ and uses the conditional UPDATE. Under load you see errors with SQLSTATE 40001. Is your data wrong?</summary>

No. At repeatable read, PostgreSQL refuses to update a row that changed after your transaction first
read the database, and raises a serialization failure instead of re-checking the condition. The data is
safe; the transaction must be retried. At read committed the same `UPDATE` would re-check the
`WHERE` and return zero rows instead.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A passenger cancels NDLS→BPL at 10:00:05. At 10:00:05.001 a new Tatkal request for AGC→GWL books that berth. WL 1 was AGC→GWL. What went wrong?</summary>

The cancellation and the waitlist promotion ran in separate transactions, so the freed bits were
visible to new bookings in between. The berth belonged to WL 1. Clear the bits and promote in one
transaction, or process cancellation and promotion as one event on the train's single writer.

</details>

## Recap

- Rail inventory is per **segment**: a berth is a bitmask, a journey is a mask, `mask & need = 0`
  means it fits. Counters per journey do not work.
- **Read, work, write** loses updates under default isolation, and the database will not warn you.
- Correct options: lock the coach (simple, one at a time), a conditional `UPDATE` (short locks,
  retries), `SKIP LOCKED` (no waiting, worse allocation), or a **single writer per train-date** (no
  locks, best allocation, you build failover).
- **Allocation decides sellable capacity.** Best-fit keeps long runs free; spreading to avoid
  contention fragments the train.
- **Waitlists promote by fit, not by number,** and promotion must be atomic with the cancellation.
- Holds are data with an expiry, payments are a saga with idempotent callbacks and reconciliation,
  and the spike is handled before the database by admission control and fail-fast summaries.
