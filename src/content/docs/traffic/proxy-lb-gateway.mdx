## The question

When your phone asks an online shop for your order, the request almost never goes straight to the
program that knows about orders. It first reaches a box in front of the shop's servers. Engineers
call that box a reverse proxy, a load balancer or an API gateway, sometimes all three in the same
sentence. Are these three different machines? What does each one actually do?

The short answer: they are three **jobs** for the box in front of your servers, not three kinds of
machine. A **reverse proxy** receives requests on behalf of your servers and passes them on. A
**load balancer** picks which of several identical copies of a server gets each request. An
**API gateway** checks who is calling and whether they are allowed, then sends the request to the
right service. One program, such as nginx, can do all three jobs at once. The rest of Part 1 walks
through why each job appears; Part 2 shows where they really differ.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one online shop as it grows, and one request from one customer, Asha. Her phone asks for
order 42: `GET /orders/42` on `api.shop.example`. The program that answers order requests is the
**orders service**. One copy of it, on one server, can answer at most 100 requests a second.

1. **No box in front.** On day one, the orders service runs on one server, and Asha's phone
   connects straight to it. That one program must do everything: decrypt HTTPS, wait for slow
   phones to finish sending, and answer. Its address is public, and if it crashes, the shop is down.
2. **Add a box in front: a reverse proxy.** Now the shop's address points at a separate program,
   for example nginx. Asha's phone connects to nginx. nginx reads her request, opens its own
   connection to the orders service, passes the request along, and passes the answer back. Asha's
   phone never learns where the orders service runs. nginx takes over the chores: it decrypts
   HTTPS and waits for slow phones, so the orders service only sees complete requests.
3. **Traffic grows: a load balancer.** At the evening peak the shop gets 180 requests a second. One
   copy handles at most 100, so the shop runs 3 identical copies of the orders service. nginx now
   picks a copy for each request: the first to copy 1, the next to copy 2, the next to copy 3, then
   back to copy 1. Each copy gets 180 ÷ 3 = 60 requests a second, well under its 100.
4. **A copy crashes.** nginx notices that copy 3 stopped answering and stops sending it requests.
   The other two now get 180 ÷ 2 = 90 requests a second each. That is still under 100, so Asha
   notices nothing. This is why you run one more copy than you need.
5. **More services, outside callers: an API gateway.** The shop adds a users service and a payments
   service, and lets partner apps call its API. Every call must now carry an **API key**, a secret
   string that says who is calling. nginx checks the key on every request before any service sees
   it: no key, answer `401` at once; more calls than the caller's plan allows, answer `429`. Then it
   reads the path and sends `/orders/...` to the orders copies and `/users/...` to the users service.
6. **One box, three jobs.** Asha's `GET /orders/42` now passes through all three jobs inside the same
   nginx: it is taken in (reverse proxy), her key is checked and the orders service is chosen
   (API gateway), and one healthy copy of orders is picked (load balancer).

</Figure>

So the three names describe what the box does with a request, not what the box is. A company
may run three separate products for the three jobs, or one nginx for all of them. Where the jobs
really differ is in what the box must be able to read. Picking a copy needs very little. Checking
an API key needs the box to read inside the request.

An everyday picture: the reception desk of a large hotel. Guests never walk into the kitchen or the
laundry; they talk to reception, which passes their requests on (reverse proxy). When a guest asks
for room service, reception sends it to whichever of the three cooks is free (load balancer). When
someone wants the gym, reception first checks their room key (API gateway). The picture stops
working in one place: a receptionist always hears the whole request, but Part 2 shows a kind of
load balancer that cannot read the request at all, and that turns out to matter a lot.

## The building blocks

Five ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A proxy, and why "reverse"

**What it is.** A **proxy** is a program that receives a request meant for someone else, sends it
on, and passes the answer back.

**Why it exists.** Putting something in the middle lets you add work to every request (checking,
counting, encrypting) without changing either end.

**Example.** An office network may force every employee's browser through one proxy that blocks
some websites. That proxy works for the people *sending* requests. nginx in front of the shop works
for the *servers*: Asha's phone thinks nginx is the shop.

**The precise version.** A proxy chosen by the client and working for it is a **forward proxy**. A
proxy chosen by the server's owner, which clients cannot tell apart from the real server, is a
**reverse proxy**. Everything on this page is the reverse kind.

### Backends and a pool of copies

**What it is.** A **backend** is a server that sits behind the box and does the real work. A
**pool** is a set of identical copies of the same backend.

**Why it exists.** One copy can serve only so many requests a second, and it can crash. Several
copies share the work and cover for each other.

**Example.** The shop's orders pool has 3 copies, each good for 100 requests a second, so the pool
can serve 3 × 100 = 300 requests a second. At the peak of 180, it is using 180 ÷ 300 = 0.6 of that,
or 60%.

**The precise version.** Proxy documentation also calls a backend an **upstream** (nginx's word) or an
**origin**. "Identical" matters: a load balancer assumes any copy can answer any request.
Copies that keep a user's data in their own memory break that assumption, which comes back under
"Common confusions".

### A connection and a request

**What it is.** A **connection** is an open line between two programs over the network. A
**request** is one question sent on that line, such as `GET /orders/42`.

**Why it exists.** Opening a connection costs time, because the two machines must exchange
messages to set it up before any request can travel. So programs open a connection once and send
many requests on it, one after another. Keeping the line open between requests is called
**keep-alive**.

**Example.** Asha's phone opens one connection to the shop and sends 20 requests on it over a minute:
her orders, her profile, the menu, and so on. That is 1 connection and 20 requests.

**The precise version.** On the internet the connection is a **TCP connection**. TCP is the protocol
that delivers bytes between two programs in order and resends lost ones. Setting it up takes one
**round trip**: a message there and an answer back. HTTPS then adds a second round trip to agree on
encryption. A phone 50 ms away from the server therefore waits 2 × 50 = 100 ms before its first
request can even leave. Keep-alive pays those 100 ms once instead of on every request.

### Layer 4 and layer 7: what a box can read

**What it is.** A box in front of servers can look at traffic at one of two depths. At **layer 4** (L4)
it sees only which machine and which program the bytes come from and go to. At **layer 7** (L7) it opens
the request and reads the HTTP inside: method, path, headers, body.

**Why it exists.** Reading the request costs work, and for HTTPS it needs the key to decrypt it.
A layer 4 box skips that work, so it is cheaper and faster, but it is blind to what the request
asks for.

**Example.** Asha's request arrives over HTTPS. A layer 4 box sees "a connection from address
203.0.113.7, port 51234, to the shop's address, port 443, carrying 2 KB of encrypted bytes". A
**port** is a number that picks one program on a machine; 443 is the usual port for HTTPS. A layer 7
box sees `GET /orders/42` with the header `X-Api-Key: key-asha`.

**The precise version.** The numbers come from the **OSI model**, a numbered list of the jobs that
networking software does, from cables (layer 1) to applications (layer 7). Layer 4 is TCP and UDP,
where ports live. Layer 7 is the application's own protocol, here HTTP. A layer 4 box decides once
per **connection**; a layer 7 box can decide once per **request**. Part 2 shows why that difference
decides how well the load is spread.

### Knowing the caller: API keys and limits

**What it is.** An **API key** is a secret string a caller sends with every request, so the server
knows who is calling. A **rate limit** caps how many calls one caller may make in a period.

**Why it exists.** An API used by outside apps needs to know who sent each call: to refuse
strangers, to stop one caller from using up the capacity everyone shares, and to bill by usage.

**Example.** Asha's app sends `X-Api-Key: key-asha`. The gateway looks the key up and finds the
caller `asha` on the free plan, which allows 100 calls a minute. Her 101st call within the same
minute gets `429 Too Many Requests` and never reaches the orders service.

**The precise version.** Checking who the caller is is **authentication**; deciding what that caller
may do is **authorization**. Keys are one way to authenticate. Another is a **signed token**: a short message from a login
server saying who the caller is, with a signature the receiver can check, so nobody can alter it. The counters
behind a limit, and why the simple ones let bursts through, are on the
[rate limiting page](/resilience/rate-limiting/).

:::tip[If you stop here]
- A **reverse proxy** receives requests on behalf of your servers and passes them on, taking over
  chores like decrypting HTTPS and waiting for slow clients.
- A **load balancer** spreads requests over several identical copies of a server and skips copies
  that have failed.
- An **API gateway** checks who is calling (keys, limits) before any service sees the call, and
  sends each call to the right service.
- They are jobs, not machines: one program such as nginx can do all three.
- The real dividing line is what the box can read: a layer 4 box sees only connections; a layer 7
  box reads each request.
:::

## Going deeper

Here is a puzzle Part 1 cannot answer. Put a layer 4 load balancer in front of six servers that
speak gRPC, a protocol services use to call each other, and send traffic from four clients. Two
servers sit idle and four run hot. Add six more servers and nothing changes. Swap in a layer 7
proxy and the load spreads evenly, but now that box must decrypt and read every request. Then make
one server three times slower: round robin, the default almost everywhere, keeps feeding it its full
share, and its line of waiting requests grows by seconds.

None of that shows up in a feature list. It follows from two questions about each box: **what can
it read, and does it decide once per connection or once per request?** The rest of the page answers
those for each role, then looks at what every extra box in the path costs.

## What they have in common

All three are **intermediaries** that work for the server side: the client connects to them, they
open their own connection to a backend, and the client cannot tell how many servers are behind.
HTTP's own specification calls this kind of intermediary a **gateway**
([RFC 9110, section 3.7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#section-3.7)); most engineers call
it a reverse proxy. So "gateway" meant "reverse proxy" long before anyone sold an API gateway, which
is half of the naming confusion.

The other half is that these are roles, as Part 1 showed, and products are named after the role
they were first sold for. nginx, HAProxy and Envoy can each play all three roles. Kong is an API
gateway built on nginx. AWS's Application Load Balancer is called a load balancer, but it routes by
path and can check logins, which is gateway work.

Part 1 used layer 4 and layer 7. Here is what sits between them on the wire. Asha's `GET /orders/42`
over HTTPS travels wrapped three times. The outer wrapper is **layer 3**, IP: the packet's from and
to addresses, which get it to the right machine. Inside that is layer 4, TCP: ports and the ordered
connection. Inside that is **TLS**, the encryption that HTTPS adds, which turns the HTTP request into
**TLS records**, chunks of bytes that only the holder of the key can read. Layers 5 and 6 are rarely
named in practice, and TLS sits between 4 and 7. So a box that reads only the outer two wrappers knows
"203.0.113.7 is talking to port 443" and nothing about orders.

**Terminating** a connection is how a box gets past those wrappers.

**What it is.** A box terminates a connection when it is the far end of it: the client's connection
ends at the box, not at your server.

**Why it matters.** Only the end of a TLS connection holds the key to decrypt it. A box that wants
to read the request has to be that end.

**Example.** Asha's phone completes its TCP and TLS setup with nginx. nginx decrypts the bytes, reads
`GET /orders/42`, then sends the request to orders copy 2 over a second, separate connection, often
one it opened earlier and keeps open for reuse.

**The precise version.** A layer 4 box does not terminate TLS: the TLS session runs from the client
all the way to the backend, and the box forwards encrypted bytes it cannot read. Some layer 4
balancers (Google's Maglev, Linux IPVS) forward the client's packets so that even the TCP connection
runs end to end. Others, such as HAProxy in TCP mode, accept the TCP connection and open their own,
but still never see inside the TLS.

This figure puts the two side by side. Watch where TLS ends.

So one request through a layer 7 proxy rides two connections, each with its own setup and its own
**timeout**: how long one side waits for the other before giving up. Every box you add is another
such hop, and another component that, if it fails, fails every request at once.

## Each one on its own

### Reverse proxy

**Its job:** stand in front of an application and handle the parts of HTTP the application should
not have to. It is layer 7: it terminates the client's connection and sees the whole request and
response.

**TLS termination.** The proxy holds the site's certificate and does the decryption, so the
application does not. The [TLS page](/networking/tls/#where-tls-ends-inside-your-system) covers
whether to encrypt again between the proxy and the application.

**Buffering slow clients.** This one needs a number. Many Python web apps run under Gunicorn with
**sync workers**: each worker is a process that serves one request at a time, from the request's
first byte to its last.

- Say there are 4 workers, and each request needs 20 ms of real work.
- 100 users on poor mobile links each take 2 s to finish sending their request.
- **Without a proxy**, a worker accepts a connection and then waits for the bytes. Each request ties
  up a worker for 2 s of waiting plus 0.02 s of work, about 2 s. One worker finishes about 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5
  requests a second, so 4 workers finish 4 × 0.5 = 2 requests a second.
- **With nginx in front**, nginx collects each slow upload (it is built to hold thousands of idle
  connections cheaply) and hands the worker a complete request in one go. Now each request ties up a
  worker for only its 20 ms of work. One worker finishes 1,000 ms ÷ 20 ms = 50 requests a second,
  so 4 workers finish 4 × 50 = 200 requests a second.

The same 4 workers serve a hundred times more: 200 ÷ 2 = 100. The same mechanism defends against
**slowloris**, an attack that ties up workers on purpose by sending each request's bytes very slowly.

**Caching, compression and static files.** The proxy can keep copies of answers that do not change
for every user and send them without asking the app, shrink responses before sending, and serve
images and scripts from disk.

**Header rewriting.** Behind a proxy, every connection the app sees comes from the proxy, so the app
loses the client's address. The proxy adds it back in a header, `X-Forwarded-For`. It also redirects
plain HTTP to HTTPS and strips internal headers from responses.

<Figure caption="A reverse proxy in front of one app. It reads the whole request, which is what lets it end TLS, buffer, cache and rewrite headers. With a single backend, nothing is being balanced.">
  <Role kind="proxy" />
</Figure>

**Typical tools:** nginx, Caddy, HAProxy, Envoy, Traefik. A **CDN** (content delivery network) is
reverse proxies run as a service in many cities near users. **Reach for it** in front of any app
server that faces the internet, from day one; it is the cheapest of the three to run.

**Its job:** spread work over a pool of identical copies, and stop sending work to copies that are

#### Per connection: why HTTP/2 and gRPC break it

A layer 4 balancer picks a backend when a connection opens and sends every packet of that
connection there. Whether that spreads *requests* depends on how many requests share one
connection, and that depends on the HTTP version.

**HTTP/1.1** carries one request at a time on a connection: the next request waits until the
previous answer has fully arrived. So a browser that wants six files at once opens up to six
connections to the same site. Many short connections give a layer 4 balancer many decisions to
make, and the load evens out.

**HTTP/2** works differently, and it needs the four steps.

- **What it is.** HTTP/2 lets one connection carry many requests at the same time.
- **Why it exists.** Waiting for each answer before sending the next request wastes time, and
  opening six connections wastes setup round trips.
- **Example.** Asha's app needs her orders, her profile and the menu. Over HTTP/2 it sends all three
  requests at once on its one connection, and the three answers come back on that same connection,
  in whatever order they are ready.
- **The precise version.** HTTP/2 **multiplexes**: it cuts every request and answer into small
  pieces called frames, labels each frame with a **stream** number (one stream per request), and
  mixes frames of many streams on one connection. Servers commonly allow 100 or more streams at once
  ([RFC 9113, section 6.5.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113#section-6.5.2) recommends no
  fewer than 100), so a client has no reason to open a second connection.

**gRPC** is a common way for one service to call a function on another over the network, and it
sends every call as an HTTP/2 request. A gRPC client usually keeps one connection per server address
for as long as its process runs
([gRPC load balancing design](https://github.com/grpc/grpc/blob/master/doc/load-balancing.md)).

Now put those together. Per-connection balancing works when connections are short and numerous,
as with HTTP/1.1 browsers. It breaks when they are long-lived and carry many requests. A gRPC
client that sends 60 requests a second for an hour sends 60 × 3,600 s = 216,000 requests on one
connection, and the layer 4 balancer made one decision for all 216,000. The whole client lands on
one backend, and the balancer cannot move individual requests, because it cannot see them.

This is why gRPC behind a plain Kubernetes Service is famously uneven. **Kubernetes** is a system
that runs many copies of your program across machines; each running copy is a **pod**. Pods come
and go and change addresses, so Kubernetes gives a set of pods one fixed address, called a
**Service**. A component on every machine, kube-proxy, writes rules that pick one pod for each
*new connection* to that address
([Kubernetes: virtual IPs and service proxies](https://kubernetes.io/docs/reference/networking/virtual-ips/)).
That is a layer 4 mechanism, so one gRPC client sticks to one pod for life.

A **layer 7 balancer** fixes this. It is a reverse proxy with a pool behind it: it terminates the
client's connections, keeps its own connections open to every backend, and picks a backend for each
request. That costs decryption and reading on every request, and buys the right unit of balancing.

#### Per request: which copy gets it

Once each request is a separate decision, the rule for choosing matters. Two terms carry the rest
of this section; both are covered in depth on the [latency vs throughput page](/foundations/latency-vs-throughput/).

A request is **in flight** (also called **outstanding**) from the moment the balancer sends it to a
backend until the answer comes back. Meanwhile it is either being worked on or waiting in that
backend's **queue**, the line of requests waiting their turn. If a backend has 1 request being
worked on and 3 waiting, it has 4 in flight.

**Utilisation** is how busy a backend is: the rate requests arrive divided by the rate it can finish
them. A backend that finishes 100 requests a second and receives 60 is at 60 ÷ 100 = 60%, and
requests rarely wait. What happens past 100% needs the working:

- It receives 120 a second and finishes 100, so 120 − 100 = 20 more arrive each second than leave.
- After 10 s, 20 × 10 = 200 requests are waiting. A new request waits for all 200 to finish first:
  200 ÷ 100 per second = 2 s.
- After a minute, 20 × 60 = 1,200 are waiting, and a new request waits 1,200 ÷ 100 = 12 s.

The queue never settles at a new level; it grows for as long as arrivals exceed what the backend can
finish. Even below 100%, requests arrive in random clumps, so short queues form, and waits rise
steeply as utilisation nears 100%.

The common rules for picking a backend:

- **Round robin**: each backend in turn, as in Part 1. nginx's default. Fair only if every backend is
  equally fast.
- **Least connections** or **least outstanding requests**: the backend with the fewest in flight. A
  slow backend collects in-flight requests, because it finishes them slowly, so it gets fewer new
  ones without anyone telling the balancer it is slow.
- **Power of two choices**: pick two backends at random and send to the one with fewer in flight.
  Envoy's least-request rule works this way
  ([Envoy docs](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/load_balancers)).
- **Hashing** the client's address or a key, when the same client must always reach the same backend.
  A **hash function** turns any input into a number in a fixed way, so the same input always gives
  the same number, and therefore the same backend.

Why are two random picks enough? Finding the least loaded of *all* backends means reading every
backend's count on every request. Worse, when many balancer copies see the same "least loaded"
backend at the same moment, they all send to it together and overload it. Two random picks avoid
both problems and still dodge bad backends almost every time. With 10 backends of which 3 are
overloaded:

- With one random pick, the chance of landing on an overloaded one is 3 out of 10: 30%.
- With two picks, you lose only if both are overloaded. The first is overloaded 3 times out of 10.
  The second is picked from the 9 left, of which 2 are overloaded: 2 out of 9.
- Both happen 3/10 × 2/9 = 6/90 of the time, which is about 0.067, or about 7%.

So the second pick cuts the bad outcome from 30% to about 7%.

The sim below reports **p95 latency**, which needs its own four steps.

- **What it is.** p95 is the response time that 95 out of every 100 requests match or beat.
- **Why it exists.** An average hides the few users who waited a long time; p95 shows them.
- **Example.** Of 100 requests, 94 take 20 ms and 6 take 2,000 ms. The average is
  (94 × 20 + 6 × 2,000) ÷ 100 = (1,880 + 12,000) ÷ 100 = 13,880 ÷ 100, about 140 ms, which matches
  nobody's experience. Sorted from fastest to slowest, the 95th request is one of the six slow ones,
  so p95 is 2,000 ms.
- **The precise version.** Sort the response times in a recent window and read the value at the
  95% mark. p50, the middle value, is the **median**: here 20 ms. The slow end of the list is called
  the **tail**.

The tail matters more than it looks. A page that makes 20 backend calls waits for the slowest one.
If each call has a 5% chance of being slow, the chance that one call is fast is 95%, or 0.95. The
chance that all 20 are fast is 0.95 multiplied by itself 20 times, which is about 0.36. So about
1 − 0.36 = 0.64 of page loads, or 64%, hit at least one slow call.

Now all of it at once, on the same traffic: four long-lived connections, each sending 60 requests a
second (4 × 60 = 240 a second in total), and six backends that can each finish 100 a second (600 a
second of capacity). Three lanes run side by side: layer 4, layer 7 with round robin, and layer 7
with least outstanding requests.

<BalancerSim />

Three effects to take away.

- **Per-connection balancing leaves backends idle.** Four connections spread over six backends
  leave two backends with no connection at all, and nothing the balancer can do moves requests
  onto them.
- **Round robin is blind to a slow backend.** At a third of normal speed, backend 1 finishes
  100 ÷ 3, about 33 requests a second. Round robin still sends it its equal share: 240 ÷ 6 = 40 a
  second. So its queue gains about 40 − 33 = 7 requests every second, and the requests stuck there
  become the tail.
- **Least outstanding requests reacts with no health check at all.** The slow backend's growing
  in-flight count is the signal, so it gets fewer new requests.

One trap in the other direction. A freshly started backend has nothing in flight, so least
outstanding requests (and least connections) send it a burst of traffic at the moment it is
slowest. Its **caches**, copies of recent data kept in memory, are empty. Its connection pools to
the database are not open yet. And on runtimes such as Java's, the code runs slowly for its first
minute because its busiest parts have not been compiled into fast machine code yet. The fix is
**slow start**: raise a new backend's share gradually over 30 to 60 seconds. Envoy has a slow start
mode for its round robin and least-request rules; check that whatever you run has one before you
let new copies start automatically.

#### Health checks: noticing a dead copy

In Part 1, nginx noticed that copy 3 had crashed. That is a **health check**: the balancer finding
out whether each backend can serve, and removing the ones that cannot.

**Active** checks call each backend on a timer, for example `GET /healthz` every few seconds, and
mark it down only after several failures in a row, so one lost packet does not throw out a healthy
server. That makes them slow by design. With a check every 5 s and 3 failures needed, a crashed
backend stays in the pool for up to 5 × 3 = 15 s. At 100 requests a second, that is
15 × 100 = 1,500 requests sent to a dead server.

**Passive** checks watch real traffic instead and react to the failures themselves. In open-source
nginx, one failed attempt takes a backend out for 10 s by default (`max_fails=1`, `fail_timeout=10s`
in the [upstream module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html), as of
November 2026). **Draining** is the polite version for deploys: stop sending new work to a backend,
let its in-flight requests finish, then stop it.

#### Where layer 4 still wins

Layer 4 still earns its place, because not reading is cheap. Google's 2016 Maglev paper reported one
ordinary Linux machine filling a 10 Gbit/s network link with small packets, kept here as history
([Maglev, 2016](https://research.google/pubs/maglev-a-fast-and-reliable-software-network-load-balancer/)).
Cloudflare's [Unimog (2020)](https://blog.cloudflare.com/unimog-cloudflares-edge-load-balancer/)
spreads connections across every server in each of its data centres using **XDP**, a Linux hook that
runs the balancer's code on each packet as the network card's driver receives it, before the rest of
the operating system touches the packet.

**Reach for layer 4** for traffic that is not HTTP (databases, UDP game protocols, MQTT, a messaging protocol for small devices),
for fixed IP addresses that partners can add to their firewall allow-lists, for passing TLS through
to the backend unread, and in front of your layer 7 tier to spread load over its copies. **Reach for
layer 7** whenever connections are long-lived, routing depends on the request, or backends differ in
speed.

#### Who balances the load balancer?

One layer 4 machine is a **single point of failure**: if it dies, everything behind it is
unreachable. So large sites run several and spread traffic over them one layer lower, in the
**routers**, the network devices that forward packets towards their destination address.

A router learns where to send packets from **route announcements**: a machine tells it "send packets
for 203.0.113.10 to me". On the internet this is done with **BGP**, the protocol networks use to tell
each other which addresses they can reach. If four balancers all announce 203.0.113.10, the router has
four equally good next steps. **ECMP** (equal-cost multi-path routing) then picks one for each packet
by hashing the packet's addresses, ports and protocol. Every packet of one connection carries the
same values, so it hashes the same way: a connection from 198.51.100.7, port 51234, keeps reaching,
say, balancer 3.

Adding or removing a balancer changes the set that hash picks from, so some connections start
arriving at a balancer that never saw them. Maglev answers with two mechanisms.

- **Consistent hashing.** Every Maglev machine picks a backend from the same lookup table, so
  whichever machine a packet reaches, it goes to the same backend. "Consistent" means that when a
  backend joins or leaves, most connections keep their backend. A plain `hash mod N`, the remainder
  after dividing the hash by the number of backends N, would not. Take hash values 0 to 11 and go
  from 4 backends to 3. A value keeps its backend only if its remainder after dividing by 4 equals its
  remainder after dividing by 3, which is true only for 0, 1 and 2. That is 3 values out of 12 staying
  put, so 9 out of 12, three quarters, move.
- **Connection tracking.** Each machine keeps a table of the connections it already forwards, so
  those stay on their backend even while the backend set changes.

Across regions, the same announcement trick runs on the whole internet. With **anycast**, every data
centre announces the same address, and each network on the way delivers packets to the announcement
it sees as nearest. A user in Mumbai connecting to Cloudflare's DNS resolver at 1.1.1.1 reaches a data
centre near Mumbai; a user in Frankfurt reaches one in Frankfurt, at the same address. "Nearest" is in
routing terms (fewest networks to cross, adjusted by each network's own policies), not in kilometres.
DNS-based balancing (the [DNS page](/networking/dns/)) instead hands out different addresses per
region. It is load balancing all the way down; only the layer, and what each layer can read, changes.

**Typical tools:** layer 4: AWS Network Load Balancer, Google Cloud network load balancers, HAProxy
in TCP mode, Linux IPVS. Layer 7: AWS Application Load Balancer, nginx, HAProxy, Envoy.

### API gateway

**Its job:** be the single front door for many services that outside clients call as an API, and
decide about the **caller** before any service sees the call. It sees everything a reverse proxy
sees, plus who is calling and on which plan.

- **Authentication**: reject missing, unknown or expired keys and tokens with `401` at the front
  door, and pass the checked identity inward, ideally as a signed token rather than a plain header.
- **Per-caller rate limits and quotas**: for example 100 calls a minute on the free plan, as in Part 1,
  2,000 a minute on the paid one, and a million a month in total.
- **Routing** by path, version or header to different services.
- **Transformation** between protocols, for example accepting JSON over HTTP from phones and calling
  a gRPC service behind.
- **Aggregation**: answering one client call by calling several services and combining the answers.
- **The API product**: a developer portal, self-service keys, usage reports and billing.

Why put this in one place? With twelve services, each checking keys and counting calls its own way,
revoking a leaked key means twelve services must notice, and changing a plan's limit means twelve
teams make the change. With a gateway, each is one change.

Aggregation saves real time. Say a phone is 150 ms of round trip away from the shop and needs answers
from three services.

- Calling them one after another, the phone pays 3 × 150 = 450 ms.
- With a gateway in the same data centre as the services, the phone makes one call: 150 ms. The
  gateway makes the three calls nearby, about 1 ms each, so 3 × 1 = 3 ms. Total: about 153 ms.

One small gateway per kind of client (one for the phone app, one for the website) is the **backends
for frontends** pattern
([Azure Architecture Center: Backends for Frontends](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/architecture/patterns/backends-for-frontends)).

</Figure>

**Typical tools:** Kong, Apache APISIX, Tyk, Envoy Gateway, Spring Cloud Gateway, and managed
services: Amazon API Gateway, Azure API Management, Google Apigee. **Reach for it** when callers you
do not control (partners, outside developers, mobile apps you cannot update quickly) reach several
services as one API. With one service and your own website as the only caller, a gateway mostly adds
a hop.

Read it from the outside in. Anything that terminates HTTP is a reverse proxy. A reverse proxy with
a pool behind it is a layer 7 load balancer. A reverse proxy that also knows the caller is an API
gateway. The layer 4 balancer is the only different kind of thing, and it shares exactly one job
with the rest.

When you look at a product, ignore its name and ask three questions. Does it terminate HTTP, or only
forward bytes? Does it know who the caller is, or only what the request is? Does it decide per
connection or per request? A "load balancer" that routes by path and checks logins is doing gateway
work. A Kubernetes "Gateway" (the [Gateway API](https://gateway-api.sigs.k8s.io/)'s `Gateway` and
`HTTPRoute` objects) mostly describes routing and balancing, which is the reverse-proxy role.

## How to choose

Start from your situation and add one role at a time, usually to a box you already have.

<Figure caption="Each yes adds one role, usually to a box you already have. Most systems stop after the first or second question, and many small ones never need a separate gateway at all.">
  <Choose />
</Figure>

1. **One app on one server**: a reverse proxy (nginx, Caddy) for TLS and slow clients, or nothing if
   your hosting platform already runs one.
2. **More than one copy**: load balancing, usually as configuration on the proxy you have, or your
   cloud's managed layer 7 balancer. Use least outstanding requests if copies can differ in speed.
3. **Many services or outside callers**: an API gateway, behind the balancer, when you need
   per-caller work. Routing by path alone does not need a gateway product.
4. **Non-HTTP traffic, fixed IP addresses, or very high connection rates**: a layer 4 balancer in front
   of everything, which is also how the layer 7 tier itself grows past one machine.

Managed gateways come with limits you design around. Amazon API Gateway's REST APIs, for example,
give up on a backend after 29 seconds by default and cap request and answer sizes at 10 MB
([AWS quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/apigateway/latest/developerguide/limits.html), as of
November 2026; since June 2024 the timeout can be raised for Regional and private REST APIs). A
report export that takes 40 seconds fails every time behind it, and that is the right outcome. Long
work belongs in a background job: the API answers at once with `202 Accepted` and a URL the client
can check for the result, instead of holding an HTTP request open through several hops.

## See it in action

One process that plays each role in turn, so you can watch the roles stack:

- as a **reverse proxy** it forwards to one backend and sets `X-Forwarded-For`;
- as a **load balancer** it round-robins over two copies and marks a failed one down for 10 s;
- as an **API gateway** it checks an API key, limits each key to 3 calls in 10 s, and routes by path.

# runnable version: examples/proxy-lb-gateway/python/front_door.py
def do_GET(self) -> None:
    # Reverse proxy: forward to a backend, and say who the real client was. We overwrite any
    # X-Forwarded-For the client sent: this box is the trust boundary.
    headers = {"X-Forwarded-For": self.client_address[0]}
    pool = ROUTES["/orders"]

    if self.role == "gateway":
        user = API_KEYS.get(self.headers.get("X-Api-Key", ""))
        if user is None:
            return self.reply(401, {"error": "missing or unknown API key"})
        if not self.allow(user):
            return self.reply(429, {"error": f"limit is {LIMIT} per {WINDOW_S:.0f} s"})
        headers["X-User"] = user  # services trust this header, not the key
        prefix = next((p for p in ROUTES if self.path.startswith(p)), None)
        if prefix is None:
            return self.reply(404, {"error": f"no route for {self.path}"})
        pool = ROUTES[prefix]

    if self.role == "proxy":
        pool = pool[:1]  # a plain reverse proxy has one upstream

    # Load balancer: try each backend once, in round-robin order, skipping ones marked down.
    for _ in range(len(pool)):
        with self.lock:
            backend = pool[type(self).next_index % len(pool)]
            type(self).next_index += 1
        if self.down_until.get(backend, 0) > time.monotonic():
            continue
        try:
            return self.forward(backend, headers)
        except OSError:
            self.down_until[backend] = time.monotonic() + DOWN_FOR_S
            self.log(f"{backend} failed, marked down for {DOWN_FOR_S:.0f} s")
    self.reply(502, {"error": "no healthy backend"})

    const user = API_KEYS[req.headers['x-api-key'] ?? ''];
    if (!user) return reply(res, 401, { error: 'missing or unknown API key' });
    if (!allow(windows, user)) return reply(res, 429, { error: `limit is ${LIMIT} per 10 s` });

  for (let i = 0; i < pool.length; i++) {

    try {
      return await forward(port, req.url, headers, res);
    } catch {
      downUntil.set(port, Date.now() + DOWN_FOR_MS);
      console.log(`    [${role}] 127.0.0.1:${port} failed, marked down for 10 s`);
    }
  }
  reply(res, 502, { error: 'no healthy backend' });
});

Read it like this. Every request starts with the reverse-proxy defaults: one header naming the real
client, and the orders pool. If the process plays the gateway, it first looks up the key (`401` if
unknown), counts the call (`429` if over the limit), adds the caller's name as `X-User`, and swaps in
the pool for the request's path (`404` if none matches). A plain proxy keeps only the first backend.
Then every role runs the same loop: take the next backend in turn, skip it if it is marked down,
forward the request, and if forwarding fails, mark that backend down for 10 s and try the next one.

Three things to notice. In part 1, the client claimed to be `6.6.6.6`, and the backend saw
`127.0.0.1` anyway: the proxy wrote the header itself instead of passing on what the client sent.
In part 2, the request that landed on the dead `orders-2` still succeeded, because the balancer
retried it on `orders-1` within the same request; the client never knew. In part 3, the services
received `x_user`, never the API key itself. They trust the gateway, not the caller.

The demo is deliberately small. It has no TLS, no active health checks and no timeouts on the
client side, and its rate limiter is a fixed window that the [rate limiting
page](/resilience/rate-limiting/) explains how to improve. Do not put it in production; put nginx
there. Here are the same three roles in one nginx configuration, trimmed to the parts that matter.
The `upstream` block is the pool, `map` turns a key into a caller name, and `limit_req_zone` sets
each caller's limit: `18r/m` is 18 requests a minute, which is 18 ÷ 6 = 3 every 10 seconds, the
demo's limit.

```nginx title="nginx.conf (trimmed)"
upstream orders {                          # load balancer: a pool, round robin by default
    server 127.0.0.1:9001 max_fails=1 fail_timeout=10s;
    server 127.0.0.1:9002 max_fails=1 fail_timeout=10s;
}
map $http_x_api_key $api_user {            # gateway: API key -> caller
    default "";  "key-asha" asha;  "key-ravi" ravi;
}
limit_req_zone $api_user zone=per_user:1m rate=18r/m;

server {
    listen 127.0.0.1:8080;
    proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-For $remote_addr;   # reverse proxy: overwrite, don't append
    proxy_set_header X-User $api_user;

    location /orders/ {
        if ($api_user = "") { return 401; }
        limit_req zone=per_user burst=2 nodelay;
        proxy_pass http://orders;
    }
}
```

## How real stacks combine them

Large systems run the roles as separate tiers, each doing what it is best at. Each arrow in this
figure is a separate connection.

<Figure caption="A common production stack. The edge ends TLS and absorbs attacks, the layer 4 balancer gives fixed addresses and spreads connections over the gateways, and the gateways check callers and balance over each service's copies. Every arrow is a separate connection.">
  <RealStack />
</Figure>

The edge, a CDN, ends TLS near users and absorbs attacks. The layer 4 balancer gives stable addresses
and spreads connections over several gateway copies. The gateways decide about callers and balance
requests over each service's copies. Traffic from outside, called **north-south** traffic, takes this
path. Traffic between your own services, **east-west** traffic, usually skips the gateway and goes
through internal layer 7 balancing or a **service mesh**: a small proxy such as Envoy running next to
every service and handling its calls to other services.

Published systems follow the same pattern. Cloudflare layers Unimog (layer 4) under Pingora, its HTTP
proxy written in Rust, which replaced nginx in 2022 and serves over a trillion requests a day with
about a third of the CPU and memory nginx needed
([Cloudflare, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-we-built-pingora-the-proxy-that-connects-cloudflare-to-the-internet/)).
In Kubernetes, the community `ingress-nginx` controller, for years the usual front door of a cluster,
was retired in March 2026, and the project points to the Gateway API instead
([Kubernetes blog, 2025](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2025/11/11/ingress-nginx-retirement/), as of
November 2026).

### What every extra hop costs

**Timeouts must shrink as you go inward, and only one hop should retry.** Every hop has a timeout for
the hop behind it. When a caller gives up, the work behind it does not stop: the service keeps
running a request whose answer nobody will read. A **retry** is the caller sending the same request
again after a failure or a timeout.

Picture a service that needs 1.2 s. The gateway in front of it gives up after 1 s and retries. The
client in front of the gateway gives up after 1.5 s and also retries. Most gateway attempts time out
before the service can answer, and one click turns into several runs of the same expensive request,
most of them for nobody.

The count multiplies rather than adds.

- A hop that makes one attempt plus two retries sends up to 3 attempts to the hop behind it.
- If the client makes 3 attempts, and each one reaches a gateway that makes 3 of its own, the service
  can run the request 3 × 3 = 9 times.
- Add a third hop that retries the same way and it is 3 × 3 × 3 = 27.

Every retry at an outer hop restarts the whole chain inside it. The sim shows one click at a time on
a timeline:

<TimeoutSim />

With the sim's defaults, a click causes about six runs of the request on the service on average,
over 90% of the service's work is for callers that already gave up, and the user still sees an error
about half the time. Under real overload it is worse than the sim shows: the extra runs make the
service slower still, which causes more timeouts, which cause more retries. That loop is called a
**retry storm**, and it is a classic way for one slow service to take down the services that call it
([Brooker, 2022](https://brooker.co.za/blog/2022/02/28/retries.html)).

Three rules follow. The client waits longest, and each hop inward waits a little less. Retry at one
layer only, usually the outermost one that knows whether the request is safe to repeat, and give it a
budget. And pass a **deadline** inward, the time by which the answer is no longer wanted, so the
service can stop work nobody will read.

Safe to repeat means **idempotent**: running the request twice leaves the same result as running it
once. Reading an order, or setting its status to "shipped", is idempotent. A `POST /payments` that
charges ₹500 is not: two runs charge ₹500 + ₹500 = ₹1,000, unless the request carries an
**idempotency key**, a unique ID the service remembers so it can recognise a repeat.

A **retry budget** caps retries as a share of normal traffic, for example at most 10% extra. With
1,000 requests a second, that allows at most 1,000 × 0.10 = 100 retries a second, enough to cover the
odd failure but not enough to multiply load during an outage. gRPC builds this in as retry throttling
([gRPC retry guide](https://grpc.io/docs/guides/retry/)).

**Idle connections must be closed from the outside first.** Part 1 showed why connections are kept
open between requests: keep-alive saves the setup round trips. A proxy keeps a pool of such
connections to each backend. But an open connection holds memory and a **file descriptor**, one of
a limited number of slots the operating system gives each program for open files and connections.
So each side also has an **idle timeout**: close the connection if nothing has been sent on it for
that long. The two sides set their idle timeouts independently, and a close is a message that takes
time to arrive.

Here is the race that follows.

1. The backend's idle timeout is shorter, so the backend closes an idle connection.
2. Its close message is on its way to the balancer.
3. In that same instant, the balancer picks that connection for a new request and sends it.
4. The request reaches a connection that is already closed. The client gets a `502 Bad Gateway`, and
   the app's logs show nothing, because the app never received the request.

AWS's Application Load Balancer keeps idle connections for 60 s by default, while Node.js's HTTP
server closes them after 5 s and Gunicorn after 2 s (as of November 2026). With those defaults, Node
closes every pooled connection that sits idle for 5 s, and each close is a chance for that race. AWS's
guidance is to set the application's keep-alive timeout longer than the load balancer's idle timeout
([ALB docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/edit-load-balancer-attributes.html)),
so the balancer always closes first and never sends on a connection the other side closed.

**Health checks must mean "I can do my job".** The layer 4 balancer checks the gateways; the gateways
check the services. A gateway health check that returns 200 while the gateway cannot reach any
service keeps traffic flowing into a dead end.

## Common confusions

**"They are three products, so we need one of each."** They are roles. List the jobs (end TLS,
spread load, decide about callers), then pick the fewest boxes that cover them. Every extra box is
a hop, a timeout and a failure point.

**"X-Forwarded-For is the client's address."** Any client can send its own `X-Forwarded-For`, and a
proxy that adds to it passes the lie along. Only entries added by proxies you control can be trusted.
Either overwrite the header at your outermost proxy, as the demo does, or read the list from the right
and take the first address that is not one of your proxies
([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/HTTP/Headers/X-Forwarded-For)). The standard form is the
`Forwarded` header ([RFC 7239](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7239)), with the same trust rule.
Behind a layer 4 balancer there is no header to add, because it never reads HTTP. The client's address
arrives only if the balancer keeps the original addresses on the packets, or uses the
[PROXY protocol](https://www.haproxy.org/download/3.1/doc/proxy-protocol.txt), a short line it sends
at the start of each connection that names the real client.

:::danger[A header anyone can fake, used for security]
Rate limits, allow-lists and fraud checks keyed on a client-supplied `X-Forwarded-For` can be
bypassed by anyone who sets the header. Limits keyed on the connection's address behind a proxy lump
every user into one client, the proxy. Decide where your trust boundary is, and read the address only
from there.
:::

**"The gateway checks auth, so services can trust every request."** Only requests that came through
the gateway. If a service can be reached any other way (from another service, a debug port, a loose
network rule), anyone can send `X-User: admin`. Lock services so only the gateway can reach them, with
network rules or [mutual TLS](/networking/tls/), or pass a signed token the service checks itself.

**"The gateway is a good place for business logic."** It sees every request, so it is tempting. But
then a shared box, usually owned by a platform team, holds rules owned by product teams, and every
change is a change to the company's front door. Microsoft's architecture guidance says plainly that
business logic should never be moved into the gateway; keep it to work every service shares, such as
authentication and rate limits
([Azure Architecture Center: Gateway Offloading](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/architecture/patterns/gateway-offloading)).

:::caution[Every feature in the front door runs on every request]
On 2 July 2019, kept here as history, a new firewall rule went out to Cloudflare's edge proxies
worldwide. It contained a regular expression that, on some inputs, tried an enormous number of
matching paths. It used all the CPU on the machines handling HTTP and caused `502` errors for about
27 minutes
([Cloudflare postmortem, 2019](https://blog.cloudflare.com/details-of-the-cloudflare-outage-on-july-2-2019/)).
Anything in a box every request passes through shares fate with every request: roll changes out
gradually, and keep its work cheap and bounded.
:::

**"Sticky sessions are a feature to turn on."** Sticky sessions send a client to the same backend
every time, which hides the problem of per-user data kept in one server's memory. They make load
uneven (the layer 4 problem, chosen on purpose) and still lose that data when the server dies. Keep
session data in a shared store such as Redis, and keep stickiness for cases that need it, such as
WebSocket connections that stay open for a long time.

## Interview corner

**"Reverse proxy vs load balancer vs API gateway?"** Strong answers start with roles and the layer 4
versus layer 7 line: what each box can read, and per-connection versus per-request decisions. Then:
one product can play all three. Follow-up: *"When would you run separate products?"* A layer 4 tier
for fixed addresses and to spread load over the layer 7 tier; a dedicated gateway when there are
outside developers, plans and quotas, or a separate team owning the front door.

**"Your gRPC service behind a Kubernetes Service has some pods at 90% CPU and some idle. Why?"**
kube-proxy balances connections, and each gRPC client holds one long-lived HTTP/2 connection. Fixes:
a layer 7 proxy or mesh that balances per request; balancing in the client over all pod addresses (a
"headless" Service hands the client every pod's address); or closing connections periodically so
clients reconnect and get a new pick. Follow-up: *"Which rule would you use?"* Least outstanding
requests or power of two choices, so slow pods get less.

**"Isn't the gateway a single point of failure and a bottleneck?"** Not if it runs as several copies
that keep no data of their own, behind a layer 4 balancer in more than one data centre, with shared data (rate-limit
counters) in a store like Redis, and a deliberate choice of what happens when that store is down:
let calls through, or refuse them. It becomes a bottleneck when it holds slow logic, so keep it to
cheap checks, with timeouts shorter than the client's.

<details>
<summary>2. Six identical backends behind round robin; one gets slowed down by another program on its machine and becomes 3× slower. p50 latency is fine but p99 is seconds. Why does round robin cause this, and what fixes it without a health check?</summary>

Round robin keeps sending the slow backend one sixth of the traffic, more than it can now finish, so
a queue builds there and every request that lands on it waits. Only one request in six goes there,
so the median stays fast while the slowest 1% (p99) is all queue. Least outstanding requests (or
power of two choices) sends new requests to backends with fewer in flight; the slow one collects
in-flight requests and so receives fewer, which needs no health check.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. The gateway times out a backend after 30 s and retries twice; the mobile app times out after 10 s and retries three times. The backend slows to 12 s. What happens?</summary>

Every app attempt times out at 10 s while the gateway is still waiting, so the app retries and starts
another gateway request, which may itself retry. The app makes up to 1 + 3 = 4 attempts, and each
starts gateway work that can make up to 1 + 2 = 3 backend calls, so one tap can become up to
4 × 3 = 12 backend calls. Nearly all of that work is for callers that already gave up, which slows
the backend further. Make timeouts shrink inward (the gateway's under the app's), retry at one layer
with a budget, and pass deadlines inward.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. After moving behind a cloud load balancer, your per-IP rate limiter blocks almost every user at the same time. Why, and what is the fix?</summary>

Every connection now comes from the balancer's addresses, so all users share a few "clients". Key
the limiter on the real address from `X-Forwarded-For` or `Forwarded`, taking the rightmost entry
that is not one of your own proxies, and only when the connection came from your balancer. Better,
limit at the edge or the gateway, which knows the caller's identity, not only their address.

</details>

## Recap

- What separates the roles is what a box can read and how often it decides: a layer 4 box decides
  once per connection and cannot read requests; a layer 7 box decides per request.
- Long-lived HTTP/2 and gRPC connections carry thousands of requests each, so they need layer 7
  balancing to spread evenly.
- Round robin is blind to a slow backend; least outstanding requests and power of two choices react
  to it without a health check. Give new backends a slow start.
- Every box is a hop: timeouts shrink inward, only one hop retries, with a budget, and the outer side
  closes idle connections first.
- Add roles to boxes you already have before adding boxes.
