## The question

Your shop's checkout service calls a payment service for every order. One day the payment service
starts ignoring some calls: it does not answer them with an error, it does not answer them at all.
95 of every 100 payments would still go through. Yet within a couple of seconds your checkout
rejects every new order. How does a problem with a few calls take down the whole service? And how
long should your code wait before it gives up on a call?

The short answer: your server can only handle a fixed number of requests at once, and a request
that is waiting for an answer still takes one of those places. Calls that never answer keep their
places for ever, so the places run out. A **timeout** is a limit on how long your code waits:
when it runs out, your code stops waiting, frees the place and reports an error. Choose the limit
well and a sick payment service costs you 5 failed payments in 100; choose it badly, or not at
all, and it costs you every payment.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one service through a bad day. The **Orders** service handles checkout. For every order it
calls the **Payments** service and waits for the answer. Payments is a **dependency** of Orders: a
service Orders needs before it can answer.

1. **The setup.** Orders can work on 20 requests at the same time and no more. Each of those 20
   places is called a **worker** (the building blocks below explain what a worker is inside a
   server). A request keeps its worker from the moment it starts until it is finished, including
   the time it spends waiting for Payments.
2. **A normal day.** 200 orders arrive every second. Most calls to Payments answer in about 40 ms,
   a few take a few hundred milliseconds, and on average a call takes 48 ms. So each second,
   Orders needs 200 × 48 ms = 9,600 ms of worker time. That is 9.6 seconds of waiting packed into
   every second, and since each worker can give one second of its time per second, about 9.6
   workers are busy at any moment. Call it 10 of the 20. Plenty of room.
3. **A bad day.** Payments gets into trouble, and 5 of every 100 calls never get an answer. At 200
   calls a second, that is 200 × 5 ÷ 100 = 10 calls every second that hang. The other
   200 − 10 = 190 calls answer as usual, and they keep 190 × 48 ms = 9,120 ms, about 9.1 workers,
   busy.
4. **With no limit on waiting.** A call that never answers holds its worker for ever. Every second,
   10 more workers get stuck. After one second 10 are stuck, and the healthy calls need about 9
   more: 10 + 9 = 19 of the 20. A fraction of a second later all 20 are taken. From then on every
   new order waits for a worker that never comes free, and Orders has to reject it, even though
   95 of every 100 of those orders would have gone through.
5. **With a 2-second limit.** Now each hanging call holds its worker for 2 seconds and then gives
   up. 10 new hanging calls arrive every second and each stays 2 seconds, so at any moment
   10 × 2 = 20 workers are stuck on them. Add the 9.1 workers the healthy calls need:
   20 + 9.1 = 29.1 workers wanted, and Orders has 20. Still down. A limit that is too long is
   almost as bad as none.
6. **With a 300 ms limit.** Each hanging call now holds a worker for 0.3 seconds. At any moment
   10 × 0.3 = 3 workers are stuck. Add the 9.1 for the healthy calls: 3 + 9.1 = 12.1 workers
   wanted, out of 20. Orders stays up. The 10 hanging payments a second fail after 300 ms with a
   clear error, and the other 190 a second succeed.
7. **Too short is wrong too.** Set the limit to 100 ms and Orders gives up on healthy calls that
   happen to be slow: about 1 normal call in 100 takes 160 ms or more, and every one of those now
   fails although Payments would have answered. So the limit has to sit a little above how long
   healthy calls take, and the next section's building blocks show how to measure that.

</Figure>

The picture shows the whole point: a waiting call costs a worker for as long as it waits. A limit
on waiting turns "this call might never finish" into "this call fails after 300 ms", and a failure
you can see and count is far cheaper than a worker you lose without noticing.

Two more facts complete the idea, and Part 2 opens each of them up.

8. **"Timeout" often does not mean what you think.** In most libraries, the timeout setting limits
   one silence, not the whole call: the clock starts again every time a piece of the answer
   arrives. A Payments service that sends its answer one character a second never trips a
   3-second timeout, and the call can last 20 seconds. To limit the whole call you need a
   **deadline**: one fixed moment after which nobody wants the answer, whatever is happening.
9. **Giving up does not stop the other side.** When Orders stops waiting, Payments is not told. It
   goes on working on an answer nobody will read, using up its own workers. So Orders sends along
   how much time it has left, and Payments checks that before each slow step and stops when the
   time is gone.

An everyday picture: you phone a shop, and they put you on hold. A timeout is "I hang up after 3
minutes of silence". If the hold music says "your call is important to us" every 2 minutes, the
silence never reaches 3 minutes and you wait for ever. A deadline is "I hang up at 10:15, whatever
I hear". The picture stops working in one place: when you hang up, the shop assistant hears the
line go dead and stops looking for your order. A server usually does not notice, and keeps looking.

## The building blocks

Five ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A worker and a worker pool

**What it is.** A **worker** is the part of a server that handles one request at a time. A
**worker pool** is the fixed set of workers the server has.

**Why it exists.** Every request in progress uses memory and other resources, so a server cannot
take on unlimited requests at once. It sets a fixed number and makes the rest wait or turns them
away.

**Example.** Orders has a pool of 20 workers. Request number 21 arrives while all 20 are busy. It
waits in a line, called a **queue**, until one of the 20 finishes. If the queue is full too, Orders
rejects it at once, usually with `503 Service Unavailable`.

**The precise version.** What counts as a worker depends on how the server is built: a **thread** (one line
of work inside a program; a program can run many threads side by side), a **process** (one running
copy of the program, as in a Python server that starts 20 of them), a connection taken from a pool of database
connections, or a slot under a limit on how many requests an **async** server handles at once (an async
server handles many requests on one thread, switching between them while they wait). In an async
server a waiting request costs less memory than a thread, but if the server limits how many
requests it handles at once, a waiting request still holds one of those slots. Either way the rule
is the same: a request holds its worker until it finishes, and waiting counts. The
[latency and throughput page](/foundations/latency-vs-throughput/) explains why the queue in front
of a busy pool grows so fast.

### How many workers a load keeps busy

**What it is.** The number of workers busy at any moment, on average, is the number of requests
per second times how many seconds each one holds its worker.

**Why it exists.** It tells you whether a pool is big enough, and it shows why one slow
dependency is so expensive: doubling how long each call holds a worker doubles the workers you
need, at the same traffic.

**Example.** 200 requests a second, each holding a worker for 48 ms. First turn the milliseconds
into seconds: 48 ms is 0.048 s. Then multiply: 200 × 0.048 = 9.6. On average 9.6 workers are busy.
If instead 10 requests a second each hold a worker for 2 s, that is 10 × 2 = 20 workers busy, a
whole pool of 20, from only 10 requests a second.

**The precise version.** This is **Little's law**: workers in use = arrival rate × time each is
held, true on average whenever requests leave as fast as they arrive. Read the other way round, it
gives the most a pool can serve: workers ÷ time held. For 20 workers and 48 ms per request, that is
20 ÷ 0.048 s = 416.7, about 417 requests a second. Engineers often write the product as **worker-seconds per
second**: 9.6 worker-seconds of work arriving every second needs 9.6 workers.

### A timeout

**What it is.** A **timeout** is a limit on how long one wait may last. When it runs out, the code
stops waiting and gets an error instead of an answer.

**Why it exists.** A call to another service can end three ways: an answer, an error, or never.
"Never" is the dangerous one, because it produces nothing to see: no error in the logs, no failed
request on a dashboard, only a worker that quietly stops being available. A timeout turns "never"
into an error that the code can handle and the dashboards can count.

**Example.** In Python, `requests.get(url, timeout=0.3)` raises an exception if Payments stays
silent for 0.3 seconds. Orders catches it and tells the customer
"payment is slow, please try again", and the worker is free for the next order.

**The precise version.** Libraries put timeouts on individual waits inside a call: waiting to
connect, waiting for the next piece of the answer, and so on. Most of them, as Part 2 shows, start
the clock again every time a piece of data arrives. And most libraries have no timeout at all
unless you set one.

### A deadline

**What it is.** A **deadline** is a fixed moment by which the whole job must be finished. After
it, nobody wants the answer.

**Why it exists.** A job often makes several waits in a row: connect, send, read, maybe a second
call. Separate timeouts on each wait do not add up to a limit on the job, and they say nothing to
the next service about how long the caller will wait. A deadline is one number that covers the
whole job and can be handed on.

**Example.** An order arrives at Orders at 10:00:00.000, and the mobile app that sent it will wait
2 seconds. So the deadline is 10:00:02.000. Orders spends 300 ms checking stock. Now it is
10:00:00.300 and the time left is 2,000 − 300 = 1,700 ms. So the call to Payments may wait at most
1,700 ms, even if Payments' own timeout setting is 5 seconds.

**The precise version.** A deadline is the start time plus a **budget** (the total time allowed).
Before every wait, the code works out the time left (deadline minus now) and waits no longer than
that. Part 2 shows why the clock used for this must be a special one, and how the time left travels
from service to service.

### Percentiles: p99 and p99.9

**What it is.** The **99th percentile**, written **p99**, is the time within which 99 of every 100
calls finish. The **p99.9** is the time within which 999 of every 1,000 finish.

**Why it exists.** An average hides the slow few. The timeout must sit above nearly all healthy
calls, slow ones included, or it will cut them off, so we need a number that describes the slow
end.

**Example.** Take 1,000 calls to Payments on a normal day and sort them from fastest to slowest.
The average is 48 ms. The 990th call in the list took 160 ms, so 990 of the 1,000 took 160 ms or
less: the p99 is 160 ms. The 999th call took 255 ms: the p99.9 is 255 ms. Only 1 call in 1,000 was
slower than that.

**The precise version.** The pN of a set of measurements is the value below which N percent of
them fall. It is measured over a window of time (the last hour, the last day), and it changes when
the dependency changes. A timeout a little above the dependency's p99.9 on a good day, here about
300 ms, cuts off almost no healthy calls.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- Every request holds one of a server's few workers until it finishes, and waiting for another
  service counts. A call that never answers holds its worker for ever.
- Workers busy = calls per second × seconds each call holds a worker. 10 hanging calls a second
  with no limit take every worker of a pool of 20 within about a second.
- A **timeout** limits how long a call may wait. Too long (or none) and a few hanging calls take the
  whole pool; too short and it cuts off healthy calls. Set it a little above how long healthy calls
  take at the slow end (their p99.9).
- Most timeout settings limit one silence, not the whole call. A **deadline** limits the whole job,
  and the time left should be passed to the next service, because giving up does not stop the work
  on the other side.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 used one service, one dependency and round numbers. Real calls raise sharper questions.
Your code calls a service with a 3-second timeout, and the call returns after 20 seconds, with the
full answer and no error. We measured exactly that with Python's `urlopen(url, timeout=3)` and with
Node's `http.get(url, { timeout: 3000 })`, and neither library has a bug. What does a timeout
setting actually promise? When your code does give up, what is the server, and the database
behind it, still doing? And why do so many libraries ship with a default of minutes, or none?

The rest of the page answers those: which wait each kind of timeout covers, how to pick the number
and check it against a pool, how a deadline travels between services, how far giving up reaches,
and the limits a server needs against its own slow callers.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

**"Set `timeout=5` on every call."** Better than nothing, and wrong in three ways.

First, in most clients that setting is not a limit on the call. Python's `requests` documentation
says its read timeout is the time the client will wait *between* pieces of data from the server,
and that neither of its timeouts is "wall clock" time, meaning the real time that passes from the
start of the call to its end. A server that trickles data a little at a time can keep such a call
alive for ever. That is the 20 seconds in the question above.

Second, the number 5 is arbitrary. For a dependency that answers in 40 ms, a 5 s timeout lets a
hung call hold a worker 5,000 ÷ 40 = 125 times longer than a normal call, and Part 1 showed what a
handful of those do to a pool. For a report that always takes 8 s, a 5 s timeout fails every
request.

Third, a timeout stops the caller from waiting; it does not stop the work. The server goes on
computing, the database goes on running the query, and nobody reads the answer.

**"Then set them long, to be safe."** A long timeout is safe for the dependency and dangerous for
you: Part 1's 2-second limit still took the whole pool.

Timeouts sit next to four ideas they are often confused with:

- A **retry** sends a failed call again. It decides what to do *after* a failure, a timeout
  included, and the two must be designed together, because every retry needs time from the same
  budget. The [retries and backoff page](/resilience/retries-and-backoff/) covers it.
- A **circuit breaker** stops calling a dependency that keeps failing, for a while, so you stop
  paying even the timeout on every call.
- **Load shedding** refuses work at the door when your own server is full, before it takes a
  worker.
- How timeouts must shrink from the outermost service inward, and how retries at each hop
  (each step from one service to the next) multiply, is on the [reverse proxy, load balancer and gateway page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/#what-every-extra-hop-costs).

This page is about the single call: what to limit, by how much, and what happens after.

## How it works, piece by piece

### Where a call waits

Before we can say which timeout limits what, we need to see where an HTTP call actually waits. Four
terms first, each in a sentence or two.

A **TCP connection** is the two-way channel two programs open before any HTTP data moves; TCP is
the protocol that makes sure the data arrives complete and in order. It sends data in small pieces
called **packets**, and resends any packet that gets lost on the way. Opening one takes a
**handshake**: three small messages, "can we talk?", "yes, can we?", "yes". It exists so both sides
know the other is there and listening. If the other machine never answers, nothing in TCP gives up
quickly: the operating system resends the first message, waiting longer each time, and the
[what happens when you type a URL page](/foundations/how-the-web-works/) measured 21 to 135 seconds
before it gave up. The [TCP vs UDP page](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/) has the details.

A **TLS handshake** comes next for `https://` addresses: a few more messages in which the two sides
agree on encryption keys, the secret numbers used to scramble and unscramble the data. It is a second wait before any HTTP data moves
([TLS page](/networking/tls/)).

A **socket** is the program's handle on one connection: the object it reads from and writes to.
Timeouts at the lowest level are set on a socket.

A **byte** is the smallest piece of data a program sends or receives: 8 bits, enough to hold one
character of plain English text, such as `a`. A 20-byte answer is an answer 20 characters long.
Answers arrive as a stream of bytes, often in several pieces called **chunks**, and the wait for the
next chunk is one of the waits below.

With those, here are the waits inside one call, and the timeout that covers each. Libraries name
them differently, but these exist almost everywhere:

- **Connect timeout**: how long to wait for the TCP handshake to finish. A machine that does not
  answer at all fails here. `requests` applies it to each address of the server in turn, so a name
  with an IPv4 and an IPv6 address (the old and new forms of internet address) can take twice as
  long.
- **TLS handshake timeout**: the same, for the encryption handshake. AWS's SDKs, the client
  libraries Amazon publishes for calling its cloud services, set it separately, to the same value as
  connect.
- **Read timeout**, also called socket timeout or idle timeout: how long to wait for the *next*
  bytes. It starts again every time data arrives. In 99.9% of calls, the `requests` documentation
  notes, that wait is the one before the first byte, which is why it is mistaken for a limit on the
  whole response.
- **Write timeout**: how long sending may block, which matters when uploading to a server that
  reads slowly.
- **Total timeout**, or **deadline**: a limit on the whole call, from the start to the last byte of
  the answer. Go's `http.Client.Timeout` is one; most other clients do not have one built in.

Why are read timeouts built this way? Because it is what the operating system offers. Python's
`socket.settimeout()` puts a limit on each socket operation that waits (a **blocking** operation),
one read at a time. A library
that wants a limit on the whole call has to subtract the time already spent before every read. Most
do not.

The figure shows what that means for the server in the question above, which answers at once and
then sends its 20-byte answer one byte a second.

Twenty bytes at one a second take 20 × 1 = 20 seconds. The read timeout only ever sees a 1-second
silence, which is under its 3 seconds, so it never fires. Any server that sends at least one byte
every 2.9 seconds keeps it quiet for ever. The examples on this page run that server and print:

```text
1. A server that sends 20 bytes, one every 1 s. Client timeout: 3 s
   [ 20.0s] urlopen(timeout=3): got 20 bytes, no error
   [  3.0s] fetch_with_deadline(3 s): gave up at the deadline (timed out)
```

The first line is Python's standard `urlopen` with `timeout=3`: 20 seconds, no error. The second is
our own function, shown in "Build it yourself", that keeps a deadline: it gave up at 3 seconds.

Node.js is the same. Its `http.get` with `{ timeout: 3000 }` also returned after 20.0 s. Node's
documentation is explicit about why: the `'timeout'` event "only notifies that the socket has been
idle. The request must be destroyed manually." In other words, the option tells your code that 3
seconds of silence passed, and leaves stopping the call to you. We made a server stay silent for
6 s, and a request whose `'timeout'` handler only logged a message, as many do, got the event at
3.0 s and its answer at 6.0 s.

`fetch()` in Node has no overall timeout either: its defaults wait up to 300 s for the headers and
then up to 300 s between chunks of the body. What does limit the whole call is an **abort signal**,
an object you pass to `fetch` that can tell it to stop. `fetch(url, { signal:
AbortSignal.timeout(3000) })` creates a signal that fires after 3,000 ms, and it stopped the call
at 3.0 s, in the middle of reading the body.

**Proxies behave like clients.** A **proxy** is a server that sits between a client and a service
and passes requests through; nginx and Envoy are two widely used ones, and Cloudflare runs a large
network of them in front of other companies' websites. Each proxy waits on the service behind it,
so each has its own timeouts. nginx's `proxy_read_timeout` applies between two reads, like a
client's read timeout. Envoy's route timeout covers the whole response, but starts only once the
full request has been received.

Here are the defaults, as of November 2026, from each project's documentation. Read the right-hand
column carefully: "timeout" alone never says which wait it covers.

| Client or proxy | Default | What it limits |
|---|---|---|
| Python `requests`, `urllib` | none | — |
| Python `httpx` | 5 s | each connect, each read, each write, and the wait for a free connection |
| Node.js `fetch` (built on a library called undici) | 300 s, 300 s | waiting for the headers; then each gap between body chunks |
| Node.js `http.request` | none | the `timeout` option only emits an event |
| Go `http.Client` | none | when set, the whole request including the body |
| gRPC, a framework for calls between services | no deadline | when set, the whole call |
| nginx `proxy_read_timeout` | 60 s | each gap between two reads |
| Envoy route timeout | 15 s | the whole response, from the end of the request |
| Cloudflare, waiting on your server | 125 s | waiting for the response (error 524) |
| AWS SDKs (Amazon's client libraries), connect | 3.1 s (1.1 s in-region) | the TCP and TLS handshakes |
| PostgreSQL `statement_timeout` | none | each SQL statement, on the database server |

Two things stand out. The defaults that exist are minutes, not milliseconds: 300 s is 5 minutes,
125 s is a little over 2. And the right-hand column is not one thing: some limit a whole call, some
limit one wait inside it.

:::caution[A slow trickle is not exotic]
A response trickles when the server sends its answer bit by bit as it computes it, when a proxy
collects the answer and passes it on in pieces, or when a congested network path loses packets and
TCP resends them one by one. In each case the read timeout keeps starting again, and only a
deadline puts a limit on the total.
:::

### Choosing the number

The bottom two bars hold the same healthy traffic. Only the timeout changed, and it decided

The sim shows both edges. Too long, and hung calls take the pool: at the defaults, the sim's
counters show about 70% of worker time going to calls that would never answer, and between a
quarter and half of all requests rejected, depending on the moment. Too short, and the timeout cuts
into healthy calls: at 100 ms, below the dependency's slow end, about 89 in 100 requests were
answered instead of 95 in 100. With no hung calls at all, any timeout looks fine, which is why the
number is so often wrong: it only matters on the bad day.

So the number comes from two measurements, not from a habit:

1. **How long the dependency takes on a good day**, at the slow end. Set the timeout a little above
   its p99.9, so healthy calls almost never reach it. For the dependency in the sim, the p99.9 is
   about 255 ms (measured from the sim's model of call times), so about 300 ms.
2. **What the caller can afford**: its own deadline minus everything else it must do. If that is
   less than the dependency's p99.9, no timeout fixes it, because healthy calls will not fit; the
   design needs a cache (a saved copy of a recent answer), a fallback answer, or a background job
   (work done later, outside the request, while the caller gets a quick reply) instead.

Then check the result against the pool with Little's law from Part 1. Work out the average time a
request holds a worker, counting the hanging share at the full timeout, and confirm that workers ÷
that time stays above your peak traffic. For Orders on the bad day: 95 in 100 calls (a share of 0.95)
hold a worker for 0.048 s, and 5 in 100 (a share of 0.05) hold it for 0.3 s. The average hold is 0.95 × 0.048 + 0.05 × 0.3 =
0.0456 + 0.015 = 0.0606 s. The pool can then serve 20 ÷ 0.0606 ≈ 330 requests a second, above the
200 that arrive. With the 2-second timeout the average hold would be 0.0456 + 0.05 × 2 = 0.1456 s,
and the pool could serve only 20 ÷ 0.1456 ≈ 137 a second, below the 200 that arrive.

Measure again after the dependency changes, because a timeout set from last year's latency is a
guess.

:::note[From the trenches]
The first timeout to look at in an incident is not the one that fired; it is the one that did not.
A pool full of requests stuck on one slow dependency usually traces back to a call with no limit,
or with the library's default of minutes, that nobody noticed while everything was fast.
:::

### Deadlines: passing the budget along

A request that enters your system with a 2-second budget may call three services,
each of which calls a database. If each hop sets its own fixed
timeout, they do not add up to anything: the innermost service may spend 1.5 s on work for a caller that gave up a second ago. The
fix is to pass the budget itself, as Part 1's step 9 described. Doing that well needs two more
ideas.

**Clocks disagree.** Every machine keeps its own clock, and a time-sync service (usually NTP, the
Network Time Protocol) nudges it towards the true time every so often. Two machines' clocks are
close, but never exactly equal, and the gap is sometimes far larger than anyone expected.

So a deadline can travel in two forms. An **absolute deadline** is a clock time: "finish by
13:00:02.000". It breaks when the two clocks disagree. Example: the caller's clock runs 500 ms
ahead of the receiver's. A 2-second deadline set by the caller at what its clock calls 13:00:00.000
reads "13:00:02.000". The receiver gets it at that same instant, when its own clock says
12:59:59.500, and works out 13:00:02.000 − 12:59:59.500 = 2.5 s left: half a second more than the
caller will actually wait. A **relative timeout** is the time
left: "1,450 ms from now". The receiver turns it into a deadline on its own clock the moment the
request arrives, and the two clocks never need to agree.

**gRPC** works exactly this way. gRPC is a framework, first built at Google, for one service to call
a function on another over the network. It converts a deadline into the time remaining before
sending it, in a header called `grpc-timeout`, and each server turns that back into a deadline on its
own clock ([gRPC docs](https://grpc.io/docs/guides/deadlines/)). For plain HTTP there is no
standard header. Envoy sends `x-envoy-expected-rq-timeout-ms` to the services behind it so they can
stop early, and many teams define their own; our examples use `x-request-timeout-ms`.

The second idea is which clock to measure "time left" on.

**What a monotonic clock is.** A **monotonic clock** is a clock that only ever counts forward, like
a stopwatch, and has nothing to do with the time of day.

**Why it exists.** The ordinary clock, the **wall clock**, shows the time of day, and time sync can
move it, even backwards. A deadline measured on it can fire early or late.

**Example.** A request starts at 10:00:00.000 on the wall clock, with a 2-second deadline at
10:00:02.000. At 10:00:00.500, time sync sets the clock back by 1 second, to 09:59:59.500. The code
now thinks 2.5 s are left (10:00:02.000 minus 09:59:59.500), although only 1.5 s really are. A
stopwatch started with the request reads 0.5 s whatever happens to the wall clock, so 2 − 0.5 =
1.5 s left, correctly.

**The precise version.** Use `time.monotonic()` in Python and `performance.now()` in JavaScript for
any duration; `time.time()` and `Date.now()` follow the wall clock. A monotonic value means nothing
on another machine, which is one more reason to send time remaining, not a timestamp.

Three details make it work. Subtract the time spent waiting in queues, not only the time spent
working: a request that waited 400 ms for a worker has 400 ms less to spend. Measure on the
monotonic clock. And keep a margin for the trip back, so the answer arrives before the caller's
deadline rather than at it.

gRPC passes the deadline of an incoming call on to the calls it makes automatically in Java and Go,
and needs it switched on in C++. Everywhere else, the deadline is a value your code carries from
function to function: a `context.Context` in Go (its standard object for carrying a deadline and a "stop"
signal), an `AbortSignal` in JavaScript, an object like our
`Deadline` in Python.

### A timeout stops the waiting, not the work

When a client gives up, it closes its connection or stops reading. The server is not told
anything. It finds out, at best, when it tries to send the answer and there is nobody left to
receive it.

We measured it with a handler (the server function that answers one kind of request) that needs
2 s of work, done in ten steps of 200 ms each, called by a client with a 0.5-second deadline. The
figure compares two versions of the handler.

Bottom: the client sends its remaining time in a header, and the handler checks it before each 200 ms step; at 0.4 s only about 0.1 s is left, less than a step, so it returns 504 and stops.">
  <Cancel />
</Figure>

The handler that ignored its caller ran all ten steps, 10 × 200 ms = 2 s, and then found nobody to
send the answer to: 2 − 0.5 = 1.5 s of work after the client had left. Under load, that is how a
slow service gets slower: it spends its workers on answers nobody will read. The handler that read
the caller's remaining time did two steps (2 × 200 ms = 0.4 s), saw that the roughly 0.1 s left was
less than the 0.2 s the next step needs, and stopped.

To **cancel** work means to stop it before it finishes, from outside. How far that reaches depends
on the layer:

- In Python's `asyncio` (its library for running many waiting tasks on one thread), cancelling a
  task raises `CancelledError` inside it at its next `await`, the next point where it waits.
- A Python thread cannot be stopped from outside at all; it has to check a flag or a deadline
  itself, as our handler does.
- In JavaScript, an `AbortSignal` passed to `fetch` and to your own functions carries the
  cancellation inward.
- In Go, every blocking call that takes a `context.Context` returns when that context is
  cancelled.
- gRPC cancels the call on the server when the deadline passes, but its documentation is clear that
  stopping the work the handler already started is the application's job.

**Databases are where this hurts most.** Two terms first. A **transaction** is a group of SQL
statements that succeed or fail together. A **lock** is a database's mark on a row that says "a
transaction is changing this; others must wait". Locks exist so two transactions cannot change the
same row at once and lose one change. For example, while transaction A updates order 42, transaction
B's `UPDATE` of order 42 waits until A finishes. So a query that keeps running holds its locks and
its CPU, and every transaction that needs those rows waits behind it.

We started an 8-second query on PostgreSQL 16, using `pg_sleep(8)`, a function that does nothing for
8 seconds and stands in for a slow query. Then we stopped the client in three different ways, and
checked whether the query was still running. `psql` is PostgreSQL's command-line client.

```text
== 1. The client process dies 1 s into an 8 s query
   at 2 s, the query is: active for 2.0 s
   at 9 s, the query is: gone
== 2. The same, with client_connection_check_interval = 500ms (PostgreSQL 14+)
   at 2 s, the query is: gone
== 3. The client sends a cancel request after 1 s (psql does this on Ctrl-C)
   psql: ERROR:  canceling statement due to user request
   0.5 s later, the query is: gone
```

Read it like this. In case 1, with default settings, the query ran to its end, 8 seconds, for a
client that no longer existed. In case 2, the setting `client_connection_check_interval`, off by
default, made the server check every 500 ms during the query whether the client was still there, so
the query was gone within a second. In case 3, the client sent a **cancel request**, a separate
message on a new connection that asks the server to stop one query, and the query stopped at once.

Whether your database driver sends a cancel request when its own timeout fires is worth checking in
its documentation, because many only close the connection, which is case 1. The surest limit is the
server's own: at the start of the transaction, `SET LOCAL statement_timeout` to the time left in
the budget. `SET LOCAL` changes a setting only until the current transaction ends, so the next
request on the same connection starts clean.

### Server-side timeouts: protecting the server from its callers

Everything so far protects a caller. A server needs limits of its own, because some callers are slow
on purpose or by accident. A client that sends its request headers one byte every few seconds holds
a connection, and the server's memory for reading it, for as long as the server lets it. Thousands of
such clients, an attack known as **Slowloris**, can exhaust a server with very little traffic. The
defence is a limit on how long receiving a request may take:

| Layer | Setting | Default (as of November 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Node.js server | `headersTimeout`, then `requestTimeout` | 60 s for headers; 300 s for the whole request |
| Go `http.Server` | `ReadHeaderTimeout`, `ReadTimeout`, `WriteTimeout` | none: set them |
| nginx | `client_header_timeout`, `client_body_timeout`, `send_timeout` | 60 s each, between reads or writes |
| PostgreSQL | `statement_timeout`, `lock_timeout` | none |
| PostgreSQL | `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout`, `transaction_timeout` (17+) | none |

The database rows deserve attention:

- `statement_timeout` limits how long one statement may run.
- `lock_timeout` limits how long a statement may wait for a lock, which is often the real problem
  when a query is "slow". In our run, a second transaction trying to update a locked row failed
  after exactly 0.5 s with `lock_timeout = 500ms`.
- `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout` ends sessions that opened a transaction and then stopped
  sending anything while holding its locks, the "idle in transaction" problem from the
  [connection pooling page](/databases/connection-pooling/).
- `transaction_timeout`, new in PostgreSQL 17, limits a whole transaction.

Set these per database user (a "role" in PostgreSQL) or per session rather than for the whole
server, since a schema migration (a script that changes the structure of the tables) and a web
request need very different limits.

One more server-side limit is the **keep-alive timeout**: how long an open connection may sit idle,
waiting for the next request, before the server closes it. Why the load balancer in front (the proxy that
spreads requests over several servers) must close first is covered on the [proxy page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/#what-every-extra-hop-costs).

## Choosing between them

| Limit | Limits | Catches | Misses | Typical value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Connect | the TCP handshake | a machine that does not answer | anything after connecting | 1–3 s; about 1 s inside one cloud region |
| Read (idle) | each gap between bytes | a server that stops sending | a slow trickle | above the dependency's p99.9 wait for the first byte |
| Total / deadline | the whole call | everything, slow trickle included | nothing on the client side | what the caller can afford |
| Passed-on deadline | the whole request, across services | work done for a caller that left | services that ignore it | the first service's budget, minus time spent |
| Server receive limits | reading a request from a client | slow or malicious clients | slow handlers | seconds for headers, more for bodies |
| Database limits | a statement, a lock wait, a transaction | runaway queries, lock queues | work outside the database | per role: short for web, long for jobs |

How to choose: every outgoing call gets a connect timeout and a total deadline, the deadline taken
from the request's remaining budget. Long reads, such as streaming or large downloads, get a read
timeout instead of a total, plus a separate limit on the whole stream. Every server sets receive
limits, and every database role used by request handlers gets `statement_timeout` and
`lock_timeout`.

## Build it yourself

The naive version is the question from "Going deeper": `urllib.request.urlopen(url, timeout=3)` or
`http.get(url, { timeout: 3000 })`, both of which limit silence, not the call. The correct version
limits the whole call with a deadline. In Python that means shrinking the socket's timeout to the
time remaining before every read. In JavaScript it means an `AbortSignal`, combined with the
caller's own signal so that either one can end the call.

```python title="timeouts.py (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/timeouts/python/timeouts.py
class Deadline:
    """A point in time, measured on the monotonic clock, after which nobody wants the answer."""

    def __init__(self, seconds: float) -> None:
        self.at = time.monotonic() + seconds

    def remaining(self) -> float:
        return self.at - time.monotonic()

    def check(self, needed: float = 0.0) -> None:
        if self.remaining() < needed:
            raise DeadlineExceeded(f"deadline: {max(self.remaining(), 0) * 1000:.0f} ms left, {needed * 1000:.0f} ms needed")


def fetch_with_deadline(url: str, deadline: Deadline, headers: dict[str, str] | None = None) -> bytes:
    """GET url, failing as soon as the deadline passes, however the server spreads out its bytes."""
    parts = urlsplit(url)
    deadline.check()
    conn = http.client.HTTPConnection(parts.hostname, parts.port, timeout=deadline.remaining())
    try:
        # Tell the server how long we will wait, so it can stop when we stop.
        sent = {"x-request-timeout-ms": str(int(deadline.remaining() * 1000)), **(headers or {})}
        conn.request("GET", parts.path or "/", headers=sent)
        sock = conn.sock
        sock.settimeout(max(deadline.remaining(), 0.001))
        resp = conn.getresponse()
        if resp.status == 504:
            raise DeadlineExceeded("the server stopped: it would not finish in time")
        body = b""
        while chunk := resp.read1(65536):  # each read waits at most for what is left of the deadline
            body += chunk
            deadline.check()
            sock.settimeout(max(deadline.remaining(), 0.001))
        return body
    except DeadlineExceeded:
        raise
    except OSError as err:  # includes the socket's own timeout
        raise DeadlineExceeded(f"gave up at the deadline ({err})") from None
    finally:
        conn.close()
```

```js title="timeouts.mjs (excerpt)"
// runnable version: examples/timeouts/js/timeouts.mjs
class DeadlineExceeded extends Error {}

/** fetch() that gives up when the deadline passes, or when the caller's own signal aborts. */
async function fetchWithDeadline(url, deadlineMs, { signal } = {}) {
  const remaining = Math.floor(deadlineMs - performance.now()); // AbortSignal.timeout wants whole ms
  if (remaining <= 0) throw new DeadlineExceeded('deadline already passed');
  // One signal for "my deadline" and "my caller gave up", whichever comes first.
  const combined = signal ? AbortSignal.any([signal, AbortSignal.timeout(remaining)]) : AbortSignal.timeout(remaining);
  try {
    const res = await fetch(url, {
      signal: combined,
      headers: { 'x-request-timeout-ms': String(remaining) }, // so the server can stop when we stop
    });
    if (res.status === 504) throw new DeadlineExceeded('the server stopped: it would not finish in time');
    return await res.text(); // the same signal also bounds reading the body
  } catch (err) {
    if (err.name === 'TimeoutError' || err.name === 'AbortError') {
      throw new DeadlineExceeded(`gave up at the deadline (${err.name})`);
    }
    throw err;
  }
}
```

Read the Python version like this. `Deadline` remembers one moment on the monotonic clock and can
say how much time is left. `fetch_with_deadline` sends the time left to the server in the
`x-request-timeout-ms` header, then reads the answer in a loop. Before every read it sets the
socket's timeout to the time left, so each read can wait only as long as the whole call still may.
When the time runs out, the socket raises a timeout error, which the function turns into
`DeadlineExceeded`.

The JavaScript version does the same job with less code, because `fetch` accepts an abort signal.
`AbortSignal.timeout(remaining)` fires when the time is up; `AbortSignal.any` combines it with the
caller's own signal, so the call stops at whichever comes first.

The production shape adds the server side: read the caller's remaining time, turn it into a
deadline on the server's own clock, and check it before each expensive step.

## How real systems do it

**gRPC** puts the deadline into the protocol, as the deadlines section showed. When the deadline
passes, the client fails the call with the status `DEADLINE_EXCEEDED`, and the server cancels it.
The gRPC documentation's own warning is about the default: no deadline at all, so a client can wait
"effectively forever".

**Envoy** separates the same limits this page separates
([Envoy docs](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/faq/configuration/timeouts)):

- the route `timeout` (15 s by default) covers the whole response, but starts only when the full
  request has arrived, so it does not apply to uploads that stream in slowly;
- `per_try_timeout` limits each retry within that route timeout;
- `per_try_idle_timeout` limits the gaps between bytes within one try;
- `stream_idle_timeout` (5 minutes by default) closes streams with no activity at all.

It also tells each service behind it how long it will wait, in `x-envoy-expected-rq-timeout-ms`.

**AWS's SDKs** choose connect timeouts by where you call from. With "smart configuration defaults",
the connect and TLS handshake timeouts are 3.1 s in `standard` mode, 1.1 s in `in-region` mode
(where your code runs in the same cloud region as the service, meaning the same group of Amazon
data centres, a millisecond away), and 30 s in `mobile` mode
([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sdkref/latest/guide/feature-smart-config-defaults.html),
values marked subject to change). Why such odd numbers? When a handshake message gets no answer,
Linux resends it after 1 s, and again 2 s later, at 3 s from the start. A timeout of 1.1 s or 3.1 s
sits a little past one of those resends, so a single lost message does not fail the connect. The
`requests` documentation gives the same advice: a connect timeout a little over a multiple of 3.

**Cloudflare** waits 125 s by default for your server to start answering before it returns error
524 to the visitor, and 30 s for sending to your server, which cannot be changed. Enterprise
customers can raise the read limit to 6,000 s, which is 6,000 ÷ 60 = 100 minutes
([Cloudflare docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/support/troubleshooting/http-status-codes/cloudflare-5xx-errors/error-524/)).
Any endpoint that can take longer than that behind Cloudflare needs to become a background job:
answer `202 Accepted` at once and let the client check back, as the
[REST API design page](/networking/rest-api-design/) describes.

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A hang does not raise an error]
On 12 November 2024, one JavaScript file that Canva's editor needed took 20 minutes to download
through its **CDN** (content delivery network: servers around the world that keep copies of a site's
files close to its users), because one network path was losing packets. More than 270,000 user
requests waited on that one download. Canva's **canary**, the check that watches a new release on a
small share of traffic before it goes everywhere, looked at the error rate and saw nothing, because
the requests did not fail; they did not finish. When the download completed, all of them carried on
at once and sent 1.5 million requests a second to Canva's API gateway (the front door all API calls
pass through), three times its usual peak, and canva.com was down for about 50 minutes
([Canva, 2024](https://www.canva.dev/blog/engineering/canva-incident-report-api-gateway-outage/)).
Canva's fixes included timeouts on downloads of files like that one and using "did the page finish loading" as a
canary signal. The lesson: alert on latency and on requests in progress, not only on error rates,
and give every wait a limit so that a hang becomes an error the alerts can see.
:::

:::caution[A timeout handler that only logs]
Node's `http` `'timeout'` event, Java socket read timeouts caught and ignored, a `select` call (which
waits on several sockets at once) with a timeout that loops back to wait again: each looks like a
timeout in code review and does not end anything. The test is to point the client at a server that
never answers and check that the call fails, and when.
:::

:::caution[Retries inside a timeout, or a timeout inside retries]
Three attempts with a 2 s timeout each can take 3 × 2 = 6 s, plus the pauses between them. Decide
which number is the caller's real limit: give the whole operation a deadline, and let each attempt
use what is left of it, as Envoy's `per_try_timeout` inside the route timeout does. The
[retries page](/resilience/retries-and-backoff/) covers the rest.
:::

:::caution[Streaming needs a different shape]
A total deadline kills a long download or a WebSocket (a connection that stays open for messages in
both directions) that is working fine. For streams, limit the gaps (an idle timeout), limit the time
to the first byte, and put a separate, generous limit on the whole stream. Envoy's 15 s route
timeout is a common surprise for teams who add streaming behind it.
:::

:::caution[Timeouts measured on the wall clock]
`time.time()` and `Date.now()` follow the wall clock, which time sync can move by seconds in either
direction. A deadline computed from them can fire early or never. Use `time.monotonic()` and
`performance.now()` for anything that measures a duration.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

A timeout turns slowness into failure. That is the point, and also its cost: a request that would
have succeeded in 400 ms fails at 300 ms, and the caller now has an error to handle where it had a
slow answer. Set too short, timeouts create failures that would not otherwise exist, and with
retries on top, extra load on a dependency that is already slow.

A timeout also does not make anything faster, and it does not protect the dependency. When a
dependency is failing, the timeout limits what each call costs you, but you still pay it on every
call; a circuit breaker stops making the calls. Work that is legitimately long, such as exports,
reports and video processing, should not be squeezed under a request timeout at all; move it to a
background job and return `202`.

Finally, deadlines need everyone to take part. A passed-on deadline that one service in the middle
ignores stops at that service. Start with the services that do expensive work: the database, the
payment provider, the search cluster.

## Interview corner

Timeouts come up in every design with service-to-service calls, and in debugging questions about
outages that spread from one dependency.

- **"A service you call got slow and your whole service went down. Why, and what do you
  change?"** Hung or slow calls held workers for the full timeout, the pool ran out, and healthy
  requests were rejected. Do the arithmetic with Little's law. Lower the timeout to a little above
  the dependency's p99.9, add a circuit breaker, and give the dependency's calls their own small
  pool so they cannot take every worker.
- **"What is the difference between a timeout and a deadline?"** A timeout is a duration for one
  wait; a deadline is a moment in time for the whole operation. Deadlines hold across retries and
  hops; per-call timeouts do not add up.
- **"How do you pass a deadline between services?"** As time remaining, not as a timestamp, because
  clocks differ; turn it into a deadline on the receiving machine's monotonic clock; subtract time
  spent in queues; fail fast when too little is left. gRPC does this with `grpc-timeout`.
- **"The client timed out. Is the server still working?"** Probably. Explain cancellation: the
  server must check the deadline, and the database needs `statement_timeout` or a cancel request.

<summary>1. A call uses requests.get(url, timeout=10). The server sends its headers at once, then one chunk every 8 seconds for 15 chunks. When does the call end?</summary>

After about 120 seconds, successfully: 15 chunks, 8 seconds apart, take 15 × 8 = 120 seconds. The
read timeout limits each gap between chunks, and every gap is 8 s, under 10, so it never fires. To
limit the whole call, keep a deadline and shrink the timeout before each read, or use a client with
a total timeout.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A service has 50 workers and handles 400 requests a second. Each request calls a dependency that takes 30 ms. One day 2% of calls hang. With a 5 s timeout, what happens? What timeout would keep the service up?</summary>

Hanging calls: 2% of 400 is 400 × 2 ÷ 100 = 8 a second. Healthy calls: 400 − 8 = 392 a second,
each holding a worker for 30 ms, which is 0.03 s, so 392 × 0.03 = 11.76, about 11.8 workers. Hanging calls with a 5 s timeout:
8 × 5 = 40 workers. Total: 11.8 + 40 = 51.8 workers wanted, more than the 50 there are, so the pool
fills and requests are rejected. With a 1 s timeout the hanging calls need 8 × 1 = 8 workers, the
total is 11.8 + 8 = 19.8, and the service stays up. The timeout must still sit above the
dependency's p99.9 on a good day.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Service A has a 2 s deadline from its caller and spends 1.7 s before calling B, whose calls take about 500 ms on average. What should A do?</summary>

Fail fast with an error such as 504, without calling B. Only 2 − 1.7 = 0.3 s, 300 ms, is left, and B
takes 500 ms on an average call, so most calls could not finish in time. Calling it anyway spends
B's capacity on an answer nobody will read.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does gRPC send the time remaining instead of the deadline as a timestamp?</summary>

Because the two machines' clocks may disagree. If the caller's clock runs 500 ms
ahead, the deadline it sends is 500 ms too late by the receiver's clock, so the receiver thinks it
has 500 ms more than the caller will give it. Time remaining, turned into a deadline on the receiver's own clock,
does not depend on the two clocks agreeing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Your web app's database driver has a 5 s query timeout. Under load you see queries running for 40 seconds in pg_stat_activity, PostgreSQL's list of running queries. How?</summary>

The driver stops waiting after 5 s, but if it only closes the connection, PostgreSQL keeps running
the query until it finishes or tries to send a result. Set `statement_timeout` for the web app's
database role, check whether the driver sends a cancel request on timeout, and consider
`client_connection_check_interval`.

</details>

## Recap

- A call can fail by never ending, and that is the expensive case: it holds a worker and raises no
  error. Most libraries default to no limit or to minutes.
- Workers busy = calls per second × time each holds a worker. Hung calls hold one for the full
  timeout, so set the timeout a little above the dependency's p99.9, within what the caller can
  afford, and check it against the pool.
- Read timeouts limit silence between bytes, not the call: a 3 s timeout waited 20 s for a
  trickling server. Limit every call with a total deadline.
- Pass the deadline as time remaining, turn it into a deadline on each machine's monotonic clock,
  subtract queue time, and fail fast when too little is left.
- A timeout stops the waiting, not the work: check the deadline in handlers, and use
  `statement_timeout`, `lock_timeout` or a cancel request in the database.
- Servers need their own limits on slow clients: header, body and request timeouts.
