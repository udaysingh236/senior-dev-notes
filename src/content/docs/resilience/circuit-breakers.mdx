## The question

Your product page calls a Reviews service to show customer reviews. You did everything right: the
call has a short timeout, and if it fails, the page is shown without reviews. Then Reviews goes down
completely, and your product page goes down with it. Two customers in three get an error instead of
a page, a page that could have been shown perfectly well without reviews. How does a feature you
can live without take down the page? And how do you stop it?

The short answer: a timeout limits how long each call waits, but when every call fails, every page
still waits the full timeout, and those waits use up your server. A **circuit breaker** watches the
calls to Reviews. When most of the recent ones have failed, it stops making them for a few seconds:
the page skips Reviews and is served at once without reviews. Every few seconds it lets one call
through to check whether Reviews is back, and when that call succeeds, normal calls resume.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one page through one bad minute. The **Product** service builds product pages. For every
page it calls the **Reviews** service and waits for the answer. Reviews is a **dependency** of
Product: a service Product calls while it works.

1. **The setup.** Product can work on 20 pages at the same time and no more. Each of those 20
   places is called a **worker** (the building blocks below explain workers). A page keeps its
   worker from the moment it starts until it is sent, including the time spent waiting for Reviews.
2. **A normal day.** 200 pages are requested every second. Each page needs 10 ms of Product's own
   work plus a call to Reviews that takes about 40 ms, so a page holds its worker for
   10 + 40 = 50 ms. Every second, Product needs 200 × 50 ms = 10,000 ms of worker time, which is 10
   seconds of work packed into each second. Each worker gives one second of its time per second, so
   about 10 workers are busy at any moment, out of 20. Plenty of room.
3. **The timeout.** Product waits at most 300 ms for Reviews. On a normal day almost no call comes
   near that, so the limit never fires. It is there for the bad day.
4. **The bad day.** At 10:00:00, the database behind Reviews stops answering, and Reviews stops
   answering too. Every call from Product now waits the full 300 ms and then fails. So every page
   holds its worker for 10 + 300 = 310 ms, which is 0.31 seconds.
5. **Product runs out of workers.** 200 pages a second, each holding a worker for 0.31 seconds,
   need 200 × 0.31 = 62 workers busy at any moment. Product has 20. Turned around, 20 workers that
   are each held for 0.31 s can finish at most 20 ÷ 0.31 ≈ 64 pages a second. The other
   200 − 64 = 136 pages a second find no free worker and fail. That is 136 out of every 200, about
   2 pages in 3. The timeout worked exactly as designed; there are too many timeouts.
6. **Catching the error does not help.** Product already shows the page without reviews when the
   call fails. But the error arrives only after the 300 ms wait. The fallback is right; it comes
   300 ms too late, and the worker is held all that time.
7. **The idea: stop calling.** Product keeps a record of how its last 20 calls to Reviews ended.
   When at least 10 of those 20 have failed, it concludes that Reviews is down and stops calling it
   for 5 seconds. This switch is the circuit breaker, and "stopped" is called **open**.
8. **While the breaker is open.** A page does not call Reviews at all. It gets an immediate "not
   calling Reviews" error, shows the page without reviews, and is done in 10 ms. 200 pages a second
   × 0.01 s = 2 workers busy. Every page is served again, without reviews.
9. **Reviews gets room to recover.** Without the breaker, Reviews kept receiving about 64 calls a
   second, all the calls Product's 20 workers could send, each one asking a struggling service for
   work. With the breaker open it receives almost nothing, which makes restarting much easier.
10. **Checking whether Reviews is back.** After 5 seconds the breaker lets one call through, a
    **trial call**. If it fails, the breaker stays open for another 5 seconds. If it succeeds, the
    breaker **closes**: normal calls resume and pages show reviews again.

The picture shows the trade. Without the breaker, the page pays 300 ms per call to learn something
it already knows: Reviews is down. With the breaker, it pays that price for the first few calls,
then stops paying and serves a slightly poorer page at full speed.

Here is the bad minute with real times, from the runnable example on this page. Reviews stops
answering at 10:00:00.000. The first call fails when its 300 ms timeout runs out, at
10:00:00.300. Product makes a call every 1,000 ÷ 200 = 5 ms, so the calls behind it fail 5 ms apart:
the tenth failure comes 9 gaps of 5 ms after the first, 9 × 5 = 45 ms later, at 300 + 45 = 345 ms,
which is 10:00:00.345. The other 10 places in the 20-call record still hold successes from right
before the outage, so that makes 10 failures in the last 20 calls, and the breaker opens. Trials at 10:00:05.345, 10:00:10.645 and 10:00:15.945 each fail after 300 ms. Each
trial starts 5 seconds after the last one failed, so the trials are 5 + 0.3 = 5.3 seconds apart.
Reviews comes back at 10:00:20. The next trial, at 10:00:21.245, succeeds, and the breaker closes.
For the rest of its 20-second outage Reviews received 3 trial calls, instead of about
64 × 20 = 1,280 calls without a breaker, and pages showed reviews again 1.2 seconds after Reviews
recovered.

An everyday picture: the circuit breaker in your home's fuse box (in India, the MCB). When a fault
makes too much current flow, the breaker trips and cuts the circuit, so the wires do not overheat
and the rest of the house keeps working. The software breaker borrowed the name and its words: in a
home, a **closed** circuit lets current flow, so a closed software breaker lets calls flow, and an
open one stops them. The picture stops working in one place: a home breaker stays off until a
person flips it back. A software breaker tests the line itself, with a trial call, and closes on
its own.

## The building blocks

Seven ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A worker and a worker pool

**What it is.** A **worker** is the part of a server that handles one request at a time. A **worker
pool** is the fixed set of workers the server has.

**Why it exists.** Every request in progress uses memory and other resources, so a server cannot
take on unlimited requests at once. It sets a fixed number and makes the rest wait or turns them
away.

**Example.** Product has a pool of 20 workers. Page request number 21 arrives while all 20 are
busy. It waits in a line, called a **queue**, until a worker is free. If the queue is full too,
Product refuses it at once, usually with `503 Service Unavailable`.

**The technical version.** A worker can be a **thread** (one line of work inside a program), a
**process** (one running copy of the program), or a slot under a limit on how many requests an
**async** server handles at once (an async server handles many requests on one thread, switching
between them while they wait). In every case a request holds its worker until it finishes, and
waiting for a dependency counts as holding it. The [timeouts page](/resilience/timeouts/) covers
workers in depth.

### How many workers a load keeps busy

**What it is.** The number of workers busy at any moment is, on average, the number of requests per
second times how many seconds each request holds its worker.

**Why it exists.** It turns "Reviews is slow" into a number you can compare with the pool. Double
how long each page holds a worker and you need twice the workers, at the same traffic.

**Example.** 200 pages a second, each holding a worker for 50 ms. First turn milliseconds into
seconds: 50 ms is 0.05 s. Then multiply: 200 × 0.05 = 10 workers busy. If each page instead holds
its worker for 310 ms, that is 0.31 s, and 200 × 0.31 = 62 workers.

**The technical version.** This is **Little's law**: items in a system = arrival rate × time each
item spends there. Read the other way round, a pool can serve at most workers ÷ time held:
20 ÷ 0.31 s ≈ 64 requests a second.

### A timeout

**What it is.** A **timeout** is a limit on how long the caller waits for one call. When it runs
out, the caller stops waiting and gets an error instead of an answer.

**Why it exists.** A call can end three ways: an answer, an error, or never. "Never" holds a worker
for ever. A timeout turns "never" into an error after a known time.

**Example.** Product's timeout on Reviews is 300 ms. A call that gets no answer fails at 300 ms, and
the worker is free again.

**The technical version.** A timeout limits what one failing call costs. It does nothing about how
many calls fail. When a dependency is completely down, every call costs the full timeout, which is
the problem this page solves. How to choose the number is on the
[timeouts page](/resilience/timeouts/#choosing-the-number).

### Failing fast

**What it is.** **Failing fast** means returning an error at once, without waiting, when you
already know the work cannot succeed.

**Why it exists.** An error after 300 ms and an error after 0 ms tell the caller the same thing. The
fast one does not hold a worker for 300 ms first.

**Example.** A page that waits for a dead Reviews holds a worker for 310 ms. A page that fails fast
on the Reviews call holds it for 10 ms, only Product's own work. That is 310 ÷ 10 = 31 times fewer
workers for the same traffic.

**The technical version.** Failing fast needs a reason to believe the call will fail without making
it. A circuit breaker provides that reason: its record of recent calls.

### A fallback

**What it is.** A **fallback** is what your code does instead when a call fails: a simpler answer
that is still useful.

**Why it exists.** Most pages need some dependencies and could live without others. A product page
without reviews is still a product page; without the price, it is not.

**Example.** When the Reviews call fails, or the breaker refuses it, Product shows the page with
the line "Reviews are not available right now". Another fallback could be the reviews it saved a
few minutes ago.

**The technical version.** A fallback can be a default value, a saved copy (a **cache**: a store of
recent answers kept close by so they can be reused), a smaller version of the feature, or an error
returned fast. Part 2 covers what makes a fallback safe.

### A failure rate over a window

**What it is.** A **failure rate** is the share of calls that failed. A **window** is the set of
recent calls it is counted over, such as the last 20 calls or the last 10 seconds.

**Why it exists.** One failure tells you nothing; healthy services fail now and then. Many failures
among the recent calls tell you something is wrong now. Old calls must drop out of the count, or a
failure from last week would still count today.

**Example.** A share is often written as a percentage: a number out of 100. 10 failures out of 20
calls is 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5, and 0.5 × 100 = 50%. Now the next call fails too: it enters the window and
the oldest call leaves. If the oldest one had succeeded, the window now holds 11 failures out of 20,
which is 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55, or 55%.

**The technical version.** The breaker opens when the failure rate in the window reaches a
**threshold**, such as 50%, and only once the window holds enough calls to judge, a **minimum
number of calls**. Part 2 compares windows counted in calls with windows counted in seconds.

### Three states: closed, open and half-open

**What it is.** A circuit breaker is always in one of three **states**, and each state has its own
rule for what to do with a call.

**Why it exists.** The breaker has to do three different jobs: let calls through and watch them,
refuse calls while the dependency is down, and test carefully whether it is back. One state for each
job keeps the rules small.

**Example.** **Closed**: Product calls Reviews and records each result. 10 of the last 20 fail, so
the breaker moves to **open**: for 5 seconds, every call is refused at once. Then it moves to
**half-open**: the next call is a trial and goes through, and the others are still refused until
it ends. The trial succeeds, so the breaker moves to closed.

**The technical version.** A set of states with fixed rules for moving between them is called a
**state machine**. The breaker's has four moves: closed to open when the failure rate reaches the
threshold, open to half-open when the open time is up, half-open to closed when the trial
succeeds, and half-open to open when it fails.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- A timeout limits what each failing call costs, but when a dependency is down, every call fails
  and every one costs the full timeout. Those waits can use up all your workers.
- A **circuit breaker** watches the recent calls to one dependency. When too many failed, it
  **opens**: calls are refused at once, without being made, and your code uses a **fallback**.
- After a few seconds it goes **half-open** and lets a trial call through. Success **closes** it and
  calls resume; failure keeps it open a while longer.
- It protects twice: your workers stop waiting for a dead service, and the dead service stops
  receiving calls while it tries to recover.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1's breaker had round numbers and one outage. Real breakers decide from a small number of recent calls, which can mislead by chance,
alone, inside each running copy of your service (a busy service runs many identical copies on many
servers), whether a whole other service is down. How many
failures prove that it is, and which errors count as failures? How long should it stay open, when
every extra second open is a second without reviews after recovery? And when "Reviews" is really
four servers and only one of them is broken, does your breaker cut off the three healthy ones too?

A breaker is a guess, made on every call, from the last few seconds of evidence. A wrong guess
costs in both directions. Open too late, or never, and Product loses its workers, as in Part 1.
Open when Reviews is fine, or stay open after it recovers, and every customer loses reviews they
could have had. Each knob of a breaker trades one of those mistakes against the other, and the
rest of this page is about how to set each one.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

The first breaker most people write counts failures and stops after a limit:

```python
class NaiveBreaker:
    def __init__(self, max_failures: int = 10):
        self.max_failures = max_failures
        self.failures = 0  # never reset, never forgets

    def allow(self, now_ms: float) -> bool:
        return self.failures < self.max_failures  # once off, off for ever

    def record(self, ok: bool, now_ms: float, trial: bool = False) -> None:
        if not ok:
            self.failures += 1
```

It has two flaws, and the runnable example shows both. First, it never forgets. A healthy Reviews
where 1 call in 1,000 fails still collects failures: at 200 calls a second it fails about 200 ÷
1,000 = 0.2 times a second, so 10 failures arrive in about 10 ÷ 0.2 = 50 seconds. In the example's
run the tenth failure came after 7,518 calls, which is 7,518 ÷ 200 = 37.6 seconds of a
perfectly healthy day. Second, it never checks again. Once off, it stays off, through the outage
and after it, until someone restarts the service.

The common upgrade counts **consecutive** failures instead: open after about 5 failures in a row, and any
success resets the count. It forgets, and that is the default in two popular libraries, `pybreaker`
for Python and `gobreaker` for Go. But a streak measures luck, not a rate. Here is why, with one
idea from school.

A **probability** is a number from 0 to 1 for how likely something is: 1 in 4 is 0.25. When two
things happen independently (one does not affect the other), the chance that both happen is the two chances multiplied: two calls
that each fail 1 time in 4 both fail with chance 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.0625, about 1 in 16.

Now suppose one Reviews server in four is broken, so 1 call in 4 fails. The chance that a given
call and the 4 after it all fail is 0.25 × 0.25 × 0.25 × 0.25 × 0.25 ≈ 0.001, about 1 in 1,000. At
200 calls a second there are 200 new places a second where such a run can start, so one appears
about every 1,000 ÷ 200 = 5 seconds (a little less often in practice, because overlapping runs
count once, but still every few seconds). The breaker opens every few seconds, cutting off the three
healthy servers each time. Meanwhile a dependency that fails 1 call in 50 almost never strings 5
together (0.02 multiplied by itself five times is about 3 in a billion), even when those failures cost a 300 ms timeout each. The threshold you meant ("too many
failures") is not the one you got ("an unlucky streak").

What the breaker needs is a failure **rate** over a window that forgets, a minimum number of calls
before it judges, and a way to test for recovery. Before building that, it helps to separate the
breaker from five ideas it is often confused with:

- A **timeout** limits how long one call waits. A breaker decides whether to make the call at all.
  You need both: the timeout produces the failures the breaker counts.
- A **retry** sends a failed call again. A breaker that is open stops retries along with first
  attempts. A **retry budget** caps only the extra calls retries add (the
  [retries page](/resilience/retries-and-backoff/#retry-budgets-capping-the-extra-load) compares the
  two).
- **Rate limiting** is the opposite side: a server capping how many requests one client may send
  ([rate limiting](/resilience/rate-limiting/)). A breaker is a client protecting itself from a
  server.
- A **bulkhead** gives each dependency its own small pool of workers, so one slow dependency can
  hold at most its own pool. It limits the damage while calls still fail; the breaker stops the
  calls.
- **Load shedding** is a server refusing work it cannot finish because it is itself overloaded.
  A breaker refuses work because someone else is failing.

## How it works, piece by piece

### The three states, precisely

Part 1 named the states. Here is exactly what a breaker does with a call in each one, and with the
calls that are still running when it changes state.

<Figure caption="A breaker's three states and four moves. Closed counts results; when 10 of the last 20 failed it opens. Open refuses every call; after 5 s it goes half-open. Half-open lets one trial through: success closes the breaker, failure opens it again.">
  <StateMachine />
</Figure>

The figure shows that only two things move a breaker: results of calls, and time passing. Each
state reads one of them.

**Closed.** Every call is allowed. When a call ends, its result goes into the window, and the
breaker checks the window: if it holds at least the minimum number of calls and the failure rate is
at or above the threshold, the breaker opens and notes the time.

**Open.** Every call is refused before it is made. The caller gets a specific error, such as
`CallNotPermittedException` in Resilience4j or `BrokenCircuitException` in Polly (popular breaker
libraries for Java and .NET; "How real systems do it" below compares the main libraries), so the code can
tell "the breaker refused" apart from "Reviews failed". Calls that started before the breaker
opened still finish, and their results are ignored: the decision is already made.

**Half-open.** Most libraries move to half-open lazily: the first call after the open time ends
finds the time is up, switches the state and becomes the trial. Only a fixed number of trial calls
are allowed through, one in `pybreaker`, `gobreaker` and opossum, 10 by default in Resilience4j.
Every other call is refused while the trials run, exactly as when open. If the trials succeed, the
breaker closes and clears the window, so the outage's failures do not count against the recovered
service. If a trial fails, the breaker opens again.

Many libraries add states for the people who run the service. Resilience4j has `FORCED_OPEN` (refuse everything, for
example to switch off a feature during an incident), `DISABLED` (always allow) and `METRICS_ONLY`
(count but never open). Polly calls its forced state "isolated". These are worth having on day
one: they let you test the fallback in a **staging environment** (a copy of production used for
testing) by forcing the breaker open.

### What counts as a failure

The breaker's window is only as good as what goes into it. The question is not "did the call
fail?" but "does this failure say that Reviews is unwell?"

**Count these:** a timeout, a refused or reset connection, and a `5xx` status such as `500` or
`503`. Each says the dependency could not do the work.

**Do not count these:** `4xx` statuses such as `400 Bad Request` or `404 Not Found`. They say the
caller asked for something wrong, and Reviews answered correctly. Imagine a bot requests 2,000 product pages
for products that do not exist, and Reviews answers `404` to each. Counting those would open the
breaker against a healthy Reviews and hide reviews from every real customer. The production example
on this page sends 2,000 calls that all return `404` and stays closed. Also do not count calls
your own code cancelled, for example because the user closed the page.

**Decide about these:** `429 Too Many Requests` and `503` with a `Retry-After` header mean "slow
down". Counting them lets the breaker back off for everyone, which is usually what the dependency
wants; also honour `Retry-After` itself, as the
[retries page](/resilience/retries-and-backoff/#listening-to-the-server) describes. The example code
on this page takes the other choice: it treats every `4xx`, `429` included, as not a failure and
leaves slowing down to the retry logic.

Library defaults are blunt here. Polly counts every exception except a cancellation. `gobreaker`
counts every error. Resilience4j counts every exception unless you list some as ignored. So the
classifier is code you write: a function that looks at the error or the status and says
"counts" or "does not count", like `counts_as_failure` in the example below.

**Slow calls are failures too.** A Reviews that answers every call in 2.5 seconds is almost as bad
as one that does not answer, because each call holds a worker for 2.5 s. Resilience4j can count a
call as failed when it takes longer than `slowCallDurationThreshold`, but by default that is 60
seconds, and it only opens when 100% of calls are slow, so out of the box this check almost never
fires.

There is a subtlety in when a slow call is judged. A call that takes 2.5 s reaches the window only
when it ends, 2.5 s after it started, and by then the window also holds seconds of earlier
successes. The production example makes Reviews answer in 2.5 s from 10 s onward, with a
10-second window and a 1-second slow threshold. From 10 s to 12.5 s no slow call has ended yet, so
the window sees nothing wrong. From 12.5 s, slow calls end at 200 a second, but the window still
holds about 7 seconds of fast calls from before 10 s, which drop out one second at a time. Only at
16 s do the slow ones (701) outnumber what is left (608 fast), and the breaker opens, 6 seconds
into the slowdown. Setting the timeout close to the slow threshold fixes most of this: a call is then cut off,
and judged, at 1 s instead of 2.5 s.

### Counting: windows and minimum calls

There are two ways to keep "the recent calls", and the libraries split between them.

<Figure caption="Top: a count window keeps the results of the last 10 calls; 5 failed, so the rate is 50%, and each new result pushes out the oldest. Bottom: a time window keeps one bucket per second for the last 10 seconds, each holding a count of calls and failures; each new second wipes the oldest bucket.">
  <Window />
</Figure>

The top half reads as a fixed list of results, the bottom half as a short history. Both answer the same
question, "what share of recent calls failed?", with different meanings of "recent".

**A count window** holds the last *N* results in a fixed list. When a new result arrives, the
oldest one leaves. Its memory never grows: *N* entries. At 200 calls a second a 100-call window
covers the last 100 ÷ 200 = 0.5 seconds; at 2 calls a second the same window covers the last 50
seconds, which may include an outage that ended long ago. Resilience4j uses a count window of 100
calls by default.

**A time window** covers the last *T* seconds, kept as one **bucket** per second. Each bucket holds
two counters, calls and failures. When a new second starts, its bucket replaces the oldest one,
wiping it. The rate is the failures in all buckets divided by the calls in all buckets. Memory is
*T* buckets of two numbers, whatever the traffic. Polly uses 30 seconds by default, opossum 10
seconds in 10 buckets.

**The minimum number of calls** stops the breaker judging from a handful. Without one, a single
failure out of a single call is a 100% failure rate. The production example sends 4 calls in 2
seconds, 3 of which fail: a 75% rate, but under its minimum of 20 calls, so it stays closed.

The minimum cuts both ways. Polly's defaults need 100 calls in 30 seconds before the 10% threshold
counts at all, so a dependency called fewer than 100 ÷ 30 ≈ 3.3 times a second can never open its
breaker. opossum's default minimum, `volumeThreshold`, is 0, so its breaker can open on 1 failure
out of 1 call. Neither default is wrong; each is a choice about traffic you have to check against
your own.

**Small windows are noisy.** In a window of 20 calls with 1 call in 4 failing, you expect 5
failures. Getting 10 or more is rare, about 1 time in 70 by a statistics formula we will not need here. But
the breaker checks after every call, 200 times a second. Those checks overlap (each window shares 19
calls with the one before), so it does not open 200 ÷ 70 ≈ 3 times a second, but a rare pattern
with that many chances still turns up within seconds. The sim below shows it: with a window of 20, a 25% failure rate opens the
breaker by chance; with a window of 100, it does not. In practice, size the window so it holds at
least 50–100 calls at your normal traffic: then a stretch of bad luck is too short to fill half of
it, as the sim's 100-call window shows.

### How long to stay open, and how to test

The open time is a bet on how long outages last. Short open times find recovery quickly but send
more trials into a dependency that may still be down. Long ones are quiet but keep the breaker open
after the dependency has recovered.

**What the trials cost you.** In Part 1, each cycle is 5 seconds open plus a trial that holds one
worker for 0.3 seconds. That is one worker busy for 0.3 s out of every 5.3 s:
0.3 ÷ 5.3 ≈ 0.057, which is about 6% of one worker's time. Negligible for one breaker. But a trial is a full-length call: if trials are
allowed to wait the full timeout, a slow trial is as expensive as any other call. Shopify's Semian
library gives trials their own, shorter timeout (`half_open_resource_timeout`) for that reason. Its
team's 2020 write-up, "Your Circuit Breaker is Misconfigured", works through how these costs add up
across many processes, and argues that a slightly misconfigured breaker can be as bad as none.

**What the trials cost the dependency.** Every running copy of Product has its own breaker. Suppose
Product runs as 50 copies, each as busy as Part 1's. Each sends one trial per 5.3 s, so Reviews
receives 50 ÷ 5.3 ≈ 9.4 calls a second while it is down. Without breakers, each copy would send
about 64 a second, the most its 20 workers can, so 50 × 64 = 3,200 calls a second. Small, unless all 50 open in the same instant and
then send their trials in the same instant, every 5.3 seconds, as a burst of 50.

**Two improvements fix most of that:**

- **Jitter the open time.** Instead of exactly 5 s, wait a random time between 4 s and 6 s. Copies
  that opened together then test at different moments. The production example uses ±20%.
- **Grow the open time after each failed trial.** 5 s, then 10, then 20, up to a cap. A dependency
  that is still down after three trials is probably having a long outage, and trials every 5 s add
  nothing. Envoy, a widely used proxy, grows its time this way when it takes a failing server
  out of rotation: 30 s times the number of times in a row that server was taken out, up to 300 s. Polly allows it through `BreakDurationGenerator`.

The cost of growth is the recovery delay. In the production example's run (its output is in "Build
it yourself" below), the third failed trial set the open time to 5 × 2 × 2 = 20 s (with jitter,
19.6 s), so the breaker closed 14 seconds after the
dependency recovered. A cap of 30–60 s keeps that bounded.

**How many trials.** One trial is a sample of one: a lucky success closes the breaker on a
dependency that is still half broken, and full traffic lands on it again at once. Several trials,
say 3 to 10, judged together, make a false recovery less likely. The production example needs all 3
of its trials to succeed; Resilience4j needs its 10 to stay under the failure threshold. When the breaker does close, all of the traffic
returns in one step; a dependency that has only now restarted, with its cache empty, may fail again
under it. If you see the breaker flapping between open and closed every few seconds, that is the
usual cause.

### Where the breaker lives, and what it covers

**Where the state lives.** By default a breaker is an object inside one process. Each copy of
Product has its own breaker, learns alone that Reviews is down, and tests alone. Semian goes
further: its state lives in each worker process, so a server running 16 worker processes has 16
breakers. Envoy calls its own limits "fully distributed (not coordinated)": each proxy decides for
itself.

That has a cost and a benefit. The cost: each breaker sees only its share of the traffic. This time
suppose 50 copies share Part 1's 200 calls a second between them: they see 200 ÷ 50 = 4 calls a second each, so a 100-call window covers 100 ÷ 4
= 25 seconds, and each copy needs its own failures to open. The benefit: no coordination. A shared
breaker, for example state kept in Redis (a fast in-memory database; `pybreaker` supports this), sees all the traffic, but it
adds a network round trip to every call and a new dependency that can itself fail. Most teams keep
breakers local and size the window to the traffic each copy actually sees.

**What it covers.** One breaker should cover a set of calls that fail together. That sounds obvious
and is where most breakers are wrong. Imagine Reviews runs on four servers behind a **load balancer** (a server that spreads incoming
requests across several servers),
or stores reviews in four **shards** (four slices of the data, each on its own database), and one
of the four dies.

The top half is the trap. One breaker for "Reviews" sees 25% of calls fail. Below its threshold,
it does nothing, and Product pays the 300 ms timeout on 1 call in 4: in the sim's "1 call in 4
hangs" mode with the window set to 100, about 15 of the 20 workers end up stuck, and up to a fifth
of pages fail. The working: 200 × ¼ = 50 calls a second hang, each holding a worker for 0.31 s, so
50 × 0.31 ≈ 15.5 workers; the other 150 pages need 150 × 0.05 = 7.5 more. 15.5 + 7.5 = 23 workers
wanted, 20 exist, so the queue grows and some pages fail. Above its
threshold, it opens and refuses all calls, including the 75% that would have worked: a partial
failure becomes a total one. Polly's default threshold is 10%, so a one-in-four failure opens it.
Marc Brooker made this argument in 2022: modern services are built to fail partially, shard by
shard, and a breaker that cannot tell which calls will fail works against that design.

The bottom half is the fix: a breaker per server, per shard, or per customer, whatever unit
actually fails together. Polly's docs recommend one breaker per **authority** (scheme, host and
port) rather than one per service. When the servers sit behind a load balancer you do not control
from the client, the balancer can do the per-server version for you: it stops sending traffic to a
server that keeps failing, which Envoy calls **outlier detection** (by default, 5 failures in a row
eject a server for 30 s, and never more than 10% of the servers at once; the
[load balancing page](/traffic/load-balancing/#ejection-and-the-panic-guard) covers it).

### Fallbacks: what to do instead

An open breaker needs an answer for every refused call. Four kinds, from best to worst for the
customer:

- **A saved answer.** The reviews from the last successful call, kept in a cache for a few minutes.
  Slightly old, often good enough.
- **A smaller feature.** The page without reviews, or with only the average star rating, which
  Product may store itself.
- **A default.** "No reviews yet" is a lie when there are reviews; "Reviews are not available right
  now" is honest.
- **A fast error.** For a dependency the page cannot do without, such as Payments, failing at once
  with a clear message is still far better than holding a worker for 300 ms and then failing.

Three rules keep fallbacks safe. **A fallback must not depend on the thing that failed**: falling
back from Reviews to a second Reviews endpoint on the same database fails the same way. **A
fallback must be cheaper than the call it replaces**: a fallback that runs a heavy database query
moves the overload from Reviews to your database. **A fallback that is never used is never
tested**: it fails for the first time during the outage. Force the breaker open in a test
environment and look at the page.

:::caution[A fallback hides the outage]
With a good fallback, customers see a slightly poorer page and nobody gets an error. That is the
point, and also the danger: if no one is told, Reviews can be down for hours. Send every state change
of the breaker to your metrics and alert on "open for more than a few minutes". The examples on
this page all have an `on_change` hook for exactly that.
:::

### Breakers with timeouts and retries

A breaker rarely works alone. On a call to Reviews, three policies wrap each other, and their order
matters. From the outside in:

1. **Retry**, with backoff and a budget, decides whether to try again
   ([retries and backoff](/resilience/retries-and-backoff/)).
2. **Circuit breaker**, asked before every attempt, including retries.
3. **Timeout**, on each attempt, so a hung attempt becomes a failure the breaker can count.

In code the order looks like `retry(lambda: breaker.call(lambda: get_reviews(timeout=0.3)))`: each
layer calls the one inside it. With this order, an open breaker refuses retries as well as first attempts, and each attempt's
timeout feeds the breaker. Configure the retry not to retry the breaker's own "refused" error: it
will be refused again, at once. Polly's docs make the same point: the breaker does not retry, and
it is meant to be combined with a retry strategy.

**A breaker or a retry budget?** Both stop retries from piling onto a failing dependency, in
different ways. Marc Brooker's 2022 simulations compared them. A breaker adds no extra load at
high failure rates, because it stops all calls. But it also stops the first attempts that would have
succeeded, and it trips early when only some calls fail. A retry budget keeps every first attempt
and caps only the retries, so it degrades smoothly, and Brooker preferred it for retries. They
combine well: a budget for retries, a breaker (scoped per server or shard) for the case where a
dependency is truly down.

**A dimmer instead of a switch.** A breaker is a switch: all calls or none. **Adaptive throttling**
refuses a share of calls that grows with the failure rate. Envoy's admission control filter, based
on client-side throttling from Google's SRE book, works it out over a 30-second window like this.
Suppose 6,000 calls were made and 1,500 succeeded, and the target success rate is 95%. Divide the
successes by the target: 1,500 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 1,579, the number of calls the successes "justify". Take
that from the calls made: 6,000 − 1,579 = 4,421 calls more than justified. Divide by the calls made
plus one (the one keeps the sum from dividing by zero when no calls were made): 4,421 ÷ 6,001 ≈ 0.74. So about 74% of new calls are refused at once, and 26% still go
through and keep measuring. On a healthy day, with 5,990 successes out of 6,000,
5,990 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 6,305 is more than 6,000, the result is below zero, and nothing is refused. In
general: refuse probability = (calls − successes ÷ target) ÷ (calls + 1), never below 0, and Envoy
caps it at 80% by default so some traffic always keeps testing. There is no open state to wait out,
so recovery shows up as soon as the success rate improves.

## Choosing between them

Five ways to decide "stop calling", side by side:

| | Decides from | A dependency 1 in 4 broken | Low traffic | Recovery found | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consecutive failures | the current streak | opens by luck, every few seconds | works, but noisy | next trial after a fixed wait | `pybreaker`, `gobreaker` defaults |
| Count window | rate over the last *N* calls | stays closed or cuts everything, by threshold | window spans minutes | trials after the open time | Resilience4j default |
| Time window | rate over the last *T* seconds | same as count window | may never reach the minimum | trials after the open time | Polly, opossum |
| Adaptive throttling | success rate, as a share to refuse | refuses about the broken share | small samples, noisy share | continuously, no open state | Envoy admission control |
| Per-server ejection | each server's own failures | ejects only the broken server | per-server counts are small | after the ejection time | Envoy outlier detection |

Notice that only the last row handles the one-broken-server case well, and only because it is
scoped per server, not because of its counting.

How to choose. Start with a time window of 10–30 seconds, a minimum of calls that your traffic
reaches within that window, a threshold of 50%, an open time of 5–10 seconds with jitter and
growth, and 3 or more trial calls. Scope it to the unit that fails together: per host when you call
hosts directly, per shard when the data is sharded. If you already run a **service mesh** (a proxy
next to every copy of every service, handling the calls between them) or a proxy such as Envoy, use its outlier detection for per-server failures and keep an application breaker
for the case where the whole dependency is down and you have a fallback to serve.

```python title="breaker.py"
from collections import deque
from typing import Callable

class CircuitBreaker:
    def __init__(self, window: int = 20, threshold: float = 0.5, open_ms: float = 5000,
                 on_change: Callable[[str, float], None] | None = None):
        self.window, self.threshold, self.open_ms = window, threshold, open_ms
        self.on_change = on_change or (lambda state, now: None)
        self.state = "closed"
        self.outcomes: deque[bool] = deque(maxlen=window)  # True = failed
        self.opened_at = 0.0
        self.trial_in_flight = False

    def _move(self, state: str, now_ms: float) -> None:
        self.state = state
        self.on_change(state, now_ms)

    def allow(self, now_ms: float) -> bool:
        """Ask before every call. False means: do not call, use the fallback."""
        if self.state == "open" and now_ms - self.opened_at >= self.open_ms:
            self._move("half_open", now_ms)
        if self.state == "closed":
            return True
        if self.state == "half_open" and not self.trial_in_flight:
            self.trial_in_flight = True  # this call is the trial
            return True
        return False

    def record(self, ok: bool, now_ms: float, trial: bool = False) -> None:
        """Report how an allowed call ended. `trial` marks the half-open trial."""
        if trial:
            self.trial_in_flight = False
            if ok:
                self.outcomes.clear()  # start counting afresh
                self._move("closed", now_ms)
            else:
                self.opened_at = now_ms
                self._move("open", now_ms)
            return
        if self.state != "closed":
            return  # started before the breaker opened; ignore it
        self.outcomes.append(not ok)
        full = len(self.outcomes) == self.window  # never decide from fewer
        if full and sum(self.outcomes) / self.window >= self.threshold:
            self.opened_at = now_ms
            self._move("open", now_ms)
```

```js title="breaker.mjs"
export class CircuitBreaker {
  constructor({ window = 20, threshold = 0.5, openMs = 5000, onChange = () => {} } = {}) {
    Object.assign(this, { window, threshold, openMs, onChange });
    this.state = 'closed';
    this.outcomes = []; // true = failed; the last `window` calls
    this.openedAt = 0;
    this.trialInFlight = false;
  }

  #move(state, nowMs) {
    this.state = state;
    this.onChange(state, nowMs);
  }

  /** Ask before every call. false means: do not call, use the fallback. */
  allow(nowMs) {
    if (this.state === 'open' && nowMs - this.openedAt >= this.openMs) this.#move('half_open', nowMs);
    if (this.state === 'closed') return true;
    if (this.state === 'half_open' && !this.trialInFlight) {
      this.trialInFlight = true; // this call is the trial
      return true;
    }
    return false;
  }

  /** Report how an allowed call ended. `trial` marks the half-open trial. */
  record(ok, nowMs, trial = false) {
    if (trial) {
      this.trialInFlight = false;
      if (ok) {
        this.outcomes = []; // start counting afresh
        this.#move('closed', nowMs);
      } else {
        this.openedAt = nowMs;
        this.#move('open', nowMs);
      }
      return;
    }
    if (this.state !== 'closed') return; // started before the breaker opened; ignore it
    this.outcomes.push(!ok);
    if (this.outcomes.length > this.window) this.outcomes.shift();
    const full = this.outcomes.length === this.window; // never decide from fewer
    const failed = this.outcomes.filter(Boolean).length;
    if (full && failed / this.window >= this.threshold) {
      this.openedAt = nowMs;
      this.#move('open', nowMs);
    }
  }
}
```

```python title="breaker_prod.py (excerpt)"
def counts_as_failure(status: int | None, elapsed_ms: float, slow_ms: float) -> bool:
    """status None means no answer: a timeout or a connection error."""
    if status is None or status >= 500:
        return True
    if 400 <= status < 500:
        return False   # the caller's mistake: Reviews is fine
    return elapsed_ms >= slow_ms

def _open(self, now_ms: float, why: str) -> None:
    wait = min(self.max_open_ms, self.base_open_ms * 2 ** self.failed_trials)
    wait *= 1 - self.jitter + 2 * self.jitter * self.rand()   # ±20% by default
    self.state, self.open_until = "open", now_ms + wait
```

## How real systems do it

All values below are as of November 2026, read from each project's source or docs on GitHub.

**Resilience4j (Java)**, the library Netflix points to for new projects. A count window of 100
calls, a minimum of 100 calls, a 50% failure threshold, 60 s open, and 10 trial calls in half-open.
Slow calls count only past 60 s, and only when 100% are slow, so set those two settings yourself. Extra
calls in half-open get `CallNotPermittedException`, the same as when open. A typical Spring Boot
setup for Reviews:

```yaml title="application.yml"
resilience4j.circuitbreaker:
  instances:
    reviews:
      slidingWindowType: TIME_BASED
      slidingWindowSize: 10            # seconds
      minimumNumberOfCalls: 20
      failureRateThreshold: 50
      slowCallDurationThreshold: 1s
      slowCallRateThreshold: 50
      waitDurationInOpenState: 5s
      permittedNumberOfCallsInHalfOpenState: 3
      ignoreExceptions:
        - com.example.reviews.NotFoundException   # 4xx: do not count
```

**Polly (.NET)**, version 8. A 30-second time window, a minimum of 100 calls, a 10% failure
ratio, 5 s open and one probe call. `BreakDurationGenerator` makes the open time dynamic. Below the
minimum, the ratio is ignored, so quiet dependencies never open. The docs warn against checking
the breaker's state before calling: the breaker then never reaches half-open, because no call ever
arrives to become the trial.

**Envoy**, the proxy behind many service meshes, uses the name "circuit breaking" for something
else: limits on concurrent connections, pending requests, requests and retries per **upstream
cluster**, the group of servers behind one service (1,024 connections and 3 concurrent retries by default). Those are bulkheads, not breakers;
they refuse the excess, not everything. The classic breaker idea lives in **outlier detection**,
per server:

```yaml title="envoy cluster (excerpt)"
outlier_detection:
  consecutive_5xx: 5          # 5 failures in a row eject the server
  interval: 10s               # how often ejections are evaluated
  base_ejection_time: 30s     # × the number of times ejected in a row
  max_ejection_time: 300s
  max_ejection_percent: 10    # never eject more than 10% of servers
```

**opossum (Node.js)**, version 10. A 10-second window in 10 buckets, a 50% error threshold, a
10-second timeout of its own on every call, 30 s open, one trial in half-open, and
`volumeThreshold` 0: no minimum, so set one.

**gobreaker (Go) and pybreaker (Python)** count failures in a row: `gobreaker` opens on the sixth
(more than 5), `pybreaker` on the fifth. Both stay open 60 s. `pybreaker` can keep its state in Redis to share it across processes, and takes
an `exclude` list for errors that should not count.

**Semian (Ruby)**, Shopify's library, counts errors per worker process within
`error_threshold_timeout`, stays open for `error_timeout`, needs `success_threshold` successes to
close, and gives trials a shorter `half_open_resource_timeout`. A newer mode uses an error rate
with a minimum of 10 requests in a 10-second window. It also has bulkheads.

**Hystrix**, Netflix's Java library that made the pattern famous, has been in maintenance mode for
years, with 1.5.18 as its final release. You will meet it in older codebases and in many tutorials.

Notice how far apart the defaults are: Polly opens at 10%, Resilience4j at 50%, `gobreaker` on a
streak of 6; open times run from 5 s to 60 s. There is no standard breaker. Read the defaults of
the one you use, and set every number for your own traffic.

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[One breaker for a sharded or multi-server dependency]
A breaker that covers a whole service turns one dead shard or server into a full outage, or, below
its threshold, does nothing while a quarter of your calls time out. A classic pattern: a breaker
with a 10% threshold in front of a database split into eight shards, where one shard going down
blacks out all eight. Scope breakers to the unit that fails together.
:::

**The breaker blames the dependency for your own trouble.** Your calls to Reviews can time out
because *your* process is struggling: its CPU is maxed out, its language runtime pauses the program to free unused memory (garbage
collection), or
its pool of connections to Reviews is used up. The breaker sees timeouts and opens against a
healthy Reviews. When a breaker opens, check the caller's own metrics before paging the
dependency's team.

**A breaker that never opens.** Two common reasons. The traffic per copy never reaches the minimum
number of calls (Polly's 100 calls in 30 s per process is a lot for a quiet endpoint). Or the
dependency got slow rather than dead, and slow calls are not counted. Test the breaker by
injecting both kinds of failure in a staging environment, not only connection errors.

:::caution[Counting your callers' mistakes]
If `4xx` responses count as failures, a buggy client, a crawler or a bad deploy of your own code
that sends malformed requests can open the breaker against a healthy dependency, for every
customer. Classify errors explicitly; the default "every exception counts" is wrong for HTTP.
:::

**Flapping.** The breaker opens, closes, opens, closes every few seconds. Usual causes: a window so
small that chance trips it (the sim's window of 20), a single trial call that succeeds by luck, or
full traffic returning at once to a dependency that can carry only part of it. More trials, a
bigger window and a growing open time calm it; alert on the number of state changes, not only on
"open".

**The fallback that was never run.** It throws a null-pointer exception, or calls the same failing
database, or renders a broken page, and nobody knew, because the breaker had never opened in
production. Force breakers open regularly in a test environment (`FORCED_OPEN`, Polly's manual
isolate) and look at the result.

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

What a breaker costs:

- **False opens.** A breaker that guesses wrong removes a working feature for everyone, at least
  for one open time.
- **Recovery delay.** Up to one open time after the dependency recovers, longer with growth.
- **Partial becomes total**, unless the breaker is scoped per server or shard.
- **Tuning and testing.** Five or six numbers per breaker, each tied to your traffic, and a
  fallback path that only runs on bad days.

What it does not do: it does not make a call faster, it does not fix the dependency, and it does
not protect the dependency from callers that have no breaker. It is a client-side tool.

When not to reach for one first:

- **No timeout yet.** A breaker counts failures; without a timeout, a hung call never becomes a
  failure. Timeouts come first.
- **Per-server failures behind a proxy you run.** Outlier detection in the proxy or mesh handles
  them better, per server.
- **Retry storms.** A retry budget caps retries without cutting off first attempts.
- **Very low traffic.** A dependency called a few times a minute gives the breaker too few samples
  to judge; a timeout and a fast fallback may be all you need.
- **Work that must not be dropped**, such as writing an order. Refusing at once is wrong there;
  put the work in a queue and process it when the dependency is back.

## Interview corner

Circuit breakers come up in system design interviews whenever one service calls another, and in
"how would you make this resilient?" follow-ups.

Typical questions:

1. "Service A calls B, and B goes down. What happens to A, and how do you protect it?"
2. "Explain the states of a circuit breaker."
3. "How would you choose the thresholds?"
4. "What is the difference between a circuit breaker, a retry and rate limiting?"

A strong answer to the first walks through the arithmetic: with a timeout of *T*, every failing
call holds a worker for *T*, so workers needed = calls per second × *T*, and that can exceed the
pool even with a short timeout. Then the breaker: it stops making calls the caller already expects
to fail, serves a fallback, and checks for recovery with trial calls.

For the states, describe what each does with a call and what moves the breaker between them, and
mention that calls in flight when it opens are ignored and extra calls in half-open are refused.
For thresholds, say that the window and minimum come from your traffic, that `4xx` should not
count and slow calls should, and that the open time trades recovery delay against trial load,
with jitter across copies.

What separates good answers: scope. Mention that one breaker in front of a sharded dependency
turns a partial failure into a total one, and that per-host breakers or the load balancer's outlier
detection fix it. Mention that the breaker's state is per process unless you pay for sharing it.
And mention alerting on the open state, because a good fallback hides the outage.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Product's timeout on Reviews is 300 ms and Product has 20 workers. Reviews dies completely. Why can Product still go down, and with what numbers?</summary>

Every call now waits the full 300 ms before failing, so every page holds a worker for 10 ms + 300 ms
= 310 ms. At 200 pages a second that needs 200 × 0.31 = 62 workers, and Product has 20. The pool can
finish only 20 ÷ 0.31 ≈ 64 pages a second, so about 136 of every 200 fail. The timeout limits each
call's cost but not the number of failing calls.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A breaker is open. Five calls were already in flight to Reviews when it opened, and all five succeed. What does the breaker do?</summary>

Nothing. Results of calls that started before the breaker opened are ignored; the decision to open
was already made. Only the trial calls in half-open decide whether it closes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your breaker counts every exception. A crawler starts requesting thousands of products that do not exist, and Reviews answers 404 to each. What happens, and what is the fix?</summary>

The 404s count as failures, the failure rate passes the threshold, and the breaker opens against a
healthy Reviews: every customer loses reviews. Fix: classify errors so that `4xx` responses do not
count; only timeouts, connection errors, `5xx` and slow calls do.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Reviews stores its data in four shards and one shard dies. Your one breaker for Reviews opens at 10%. What do customers see, and what would you change?</summary>

25% of calls fail, which passes 10%, so the breaker opens and refuses all calls to Reviews,
including the 75% that would have worked. Every page loses reviews. Scope the breaker per shard (or
per host, or let the load balancer eject the failing server), so only the dead shard's calls are
refused.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. 50 copies of Product each have their own breaker with exactly 5 s open. Reviews restarts at the same moment all 50 open. What does Reviews see, and how do you smooth it?</summary>

All 50 breakers go half-open at the same moment and send their trials together: a burst of 50
calls every 5.3 seconds while Reviews is trying to start. Jitter the open time (for example a random
4–6 s) so the copies test at different moments, and grow the open time after each failed trial.

</details>

## Recap

- A timeout limits what one failing call costs; when a dependency is down, every call fails, and
  workers needed = calls per second × timeout can exceed the pool.
- A circuit breaker watches the failure rate over a window. Closed: calls flow and are counted.
  Open: calls are refused at once and a fallback is served. Half-open: a few trials decide.
- Count timeouts, connection errors, `5xx` and slow calls; never the caller's own `4xx` mistakes
  (decide `429` on purpose). Use a window that holds
  50–100 calls at your traffic, and a minimum before it judges.
- The open time trades recovery delay against trial load. Jitter it, grow it after failed trials,
  cap it, and use more than one trial.
- Scope a breaker to what fails together: per server or shard, not per service, or a partial
  failure becomes a total one.
- Wrap retries outside the breaker and the timeout inside it, test the fallback by forcing the
  breaker open, and alert on its state changes.
