## The question

A server runs at 70% of its capacity. For six seconds it slows to half speed, then it is fully
healthy again. Its clients time out after 1 s and retry three times, with exponential backoff and
jitter, as every guide recommends. A minute later the server is at 100% busy and answers none of
its requests in time, with no more users than before. What keeps it down? And why did the backoff
not help?

## Why this problem exists

Most failures between services are **transient**: they go away on their own. A connection was
reset by a deploy, a packet was lost, one replica was in a garbage collection pause, a leader
election took two seconds. A **permanent** failure is one a second try repeats: a malformed
request, a missing permission, a record that does not exist. For a transient failure, trying
again is the cheapest fix there is. That is why almost every client library retries, often
before your own code sees the error.

A few words used on the rest of the page:

- An **attempt** is one try at a call. A **retry** is every attempt after the first.
- **Amplification** is attempts divided by requests: how much load the server sees for the work
  the callers wanted. With no retries it is 1. If every request needs two attempts, it is 2.
- **Goodput** is the requests answered in time to be useful. **Throughput** counts everything the
  server finished, including answers whose caller had already given up.

The trouble is when the retries arrive. A transient failure at one client is noise. A transient
failure at the server, a slowdown, a restart or an overload, hits every client at the same moment,
and they all retry at the moment the server has the least capacity to spare. Retries are a bet that
the failure is brief and independent; when it is long and shared, the bet doubles the load on the
loser.

One more distinction decides how much any retry policy can help. In a **closed system**, a fixed
set of clients each sends its next request only after the previous one finishes: ten batch workers
draining a job table. If a worker waits 2 s before retrying, it sends nothing else for 2 s, so
waiting removes load. In an **open system**, requests arrive from outside whatever happens to the
earlier ones: users opening an app, upstream services under their own traffic. At 70 new requests a
second, a client that waits 2 s before its retry does not stop the 140 new requests that arrive in
those 2 s. Most services you call from a web backend are open systems.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

The retry loop most code starts with:

```python
for attempt in range(4):
    try:
        return call()
    except Exception:
        if attempt == 3:
            raise
```

It has three faults, and each fails in a different way.

1. **It retries everything.** A `422` or a bug in your own code gets four tries and fails four
   times, slower. A `POST /payments` that timed out may charge twice
   (the [REST API design page](/networking/rest-api-design/) covers which requests are safe to
   repeat, and why).
2. **It retries at once.** If the server dropped a connection because it restarted, the retry
   lands before the new process is listening. And every client that failed at the same moment
   retries at the same moment.
3. **Nothing limits the total.** Each request can cost four attempts, and the loop does not care
   how long they take: three attempts with a 2 s timeout each take 6 s, perhaps after the caller
   has stopped waiting (the [timeouts page](/resilience/timeouts/) covers deadlines).

**"Then add exponential backoff."** Waiting 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms between tries fixes the second
fault for one client and helps a dependency that is restarting. It does not fix the third. Each
request still sends up to four attempts; backoff only moves them later. In the opening question the
clients used backoff, and the server stayed down. Marc Brooker's 2022 analysis of backoff makes the
same point: with an unbounded population of clients, per-request backoff barely changes how much
retries amplify a long overload, because new clients keep arriving and do not know the others are
waiting.

Retries sit next to five ideas they get confused with. **Idempotency keys** make a repeated write
safe; they decide *whether* you may retry, not how much. A **circuit breaker** stops calling a
dependency that keeps failing, first tries included. **Rate limiting** caps what one client may
send. **Load shedding** is the server refusing work it cannot finish in time. And **hedging**,
covered below, sends a second copy for speed rather than after a failure.

## The core idea

A retry is extra load you borrow on the hope that the failure is brief. Decide three things
separately. **Whether**: only failures a second attempt can fix, only requests that are safe to
repeat, and only while the caller's deadline leaves time for another answer. **How much**: cap the
extra load at a share of normal traffic with a retry budget, so that retries help when a few
attempts fail and stop when most fail. **When**: back off and add jitter, so that clients that
failed together do not retry together.

Backoff answers "when". Most retry storms come from a policy that only answered "when".

## How it works, piece by piece

### Which failures to retry

Every retry policy starts with a yes or no for each failure. Three questions decide it.

**Could a second attempt succeed?** Timeouts, reset connections, `503`, `429` and `502` from a
proxy usually describe the moment, not the request. `400`, `401`, `403`, `404` and `422` describe the
request; repeating it repeats the mistake.

**Is it safe to repeat?** `GET`, `PUT` and `DELETE` are idempotent by definition (RFC 9110);
`POST` is not, unless the server stores an idempotency key. The dangerous case is the ambiguous
failure: a timeout tells you nothing about whether the server did the work.

**Did the request reach the application at all?** This is the case libraries retry on their own.
gRPC retries a call that never left the client, and once, immediately, a call that reached the
server's HTTP/2 layer but was refused before the application saw it (`REFUSED_STREAM`, or a
`GOAWAY` naming an earlier stream). These **transparent retries** do not count against your retry
policy. And gRPC stops retrying at the **commit point**: once response headers arrive, the server
has started answering, and the call will not be sent again.

| Failure | Retry? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Connection refused, reset before the request was sent | yes | nothing reached the server |
| Timeout, `502`, `504` on a `GET` or a keyed `POST` | yes | safe to repeat; the server may still be working |
| Timeout on a `POST` with no idempotency key | no | it may have succeeded |
| `503`, `429` | yes, after `Retry-After` if given | the server says "not now", not "no" |
| `400`, `401`, `403`, `404`, `422` | no | the same request fails the same way |
| Your own `TypeError`, a bug | no | retrying a bug only delays the error |

AWS's SDKs make this list concrete. Their standard mode retries `500`, `502`, `503` and `504`,
connection errors, two timeout error codes and fourteen throttling codes, from `Throttling` and
`TooManyRequestsException` to S3's `SlowDown`, and nothing else
([aws-sdk-go-v2 source](https://github.com/aws/aws-sdk-go-v2/blob/main/aws/retry/standard.go), November 2026).

### How retries multiply load

Start with one request and a server where each attempt fails with probability *p*, independently.
The first attempt always goes out. The second goes out only if the first failed, with
probability *p*; the third if the first two failed, *p*², and so on. With up to *N* retries, the
expected attempts per request are

1 + *p* + *p*² + … + *p*ᴺ

With 3 retries and 10% of attempts failing, that is 1 + 0.1 + 0.01 + 0.001 = 1.11: retries add 11%
to the load, and almost every request succeeds. At 50% failing it is 1.875. When every attempt
fails, it is 4. The extra load is smallest when the server is healthy and largest when it is
failing, which is the opposite of what a struggling server needs.

<Figure caption="Retries add little load while few attempts fail and the most load when all fail: with 3 retries, a full outage quadruples the attempts. A 10% retry budget follows the same curve while failures are rare, then stays at 1.1× however bad the failure gets.">

</Figure>

The curve assumes failures are independent. Overload makes them depend on the load itself:
more attempts mean a longer queue, which means more timeouts, which means more attempts. That
feedback is what the sim below lets you see. It is an open system: new requests keep arriving at
the same rate, one server completes 100 attempts a second from a first-in, first-out queue, and
clients give up on an attempt after 1 s. A client that gives up does not remove its attempt from
the queue, because the server does not know; when the server reaches it, the work is wasted.

<StormSim />

What the sim shows has a name. A **metastable failure** (Bronson and others, HotOS 2021) is a bad
state that persists after its trigger is gone, held in place by a **sustaining effect**, here the
retries. A system can run for months in a **vulnerable** state, healthy but with too little headroom
to absorb its own retries, and nothing looks wrong until a trigger arrives.

The arithmetic of the defaults shows where the line is. Normal load is 70 requests a second. Once
the queue wait passes 1 s, every attempt times out, and 3 retries make it 4 × 70 = 280 attempts a
second against a server that completes 100. The queue can only grow, so every answer keeps arriving
late, and the state holds itself. The trigger matters only for whether the queue wait crosses 1 s.
At half speed the queue grows by 20 attempts a second; a 2 s trigger leaves 40 queued, a wait of
0.4 s at full speed, and it drains. A 6 s trigger leaves the wait past 1 s, and the retries take it
from there. A system is safe from this when its load times its maximum amplification stays below
capacity: 70 × 1.1 = 77 is, and 70 × 4 = 280 is not.

This is not a thought experiment. Huang and others studied 22 metastable failures at 11
organisations (OSDI 2022) and found the retry policy was the sustaining effect in more than half of
them; at least 4 of 15 major AWS outages in a decade were metastable. Getting out of the state
needs the load to fall below capacity: fewer retries, load shed at the server, or more capacity.
Waiting for the trigger to pass does nothing, because it already has.

This page owns the arithmetic for one caller. When several layers each retry, the numbers
multiply, 3 × 3 = 9 executions for two layers; the
[proxy, load balancer and gateway page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/#what-every-extra-hop-costs)
covers that case and why only one layer should retry.

### Exponential backoff: what it is for

**Backoff** is the wait between attempts. **Exponential** backoff doubles it each time, from a
base, up to a cap. With a base of 100 ms, a multiplier of 2 and a cap of 2 s, the waits are 100,
200, 400, 800 ms, 1.6 s, then 2 s for every retry after that. The cap matters because without it the
seventh wait is 6.4 s and the tenth 51 s, long after anyone wants the answer.

The waits add up, and they come out of the caller's deadline along with the attempts. Three
attempts with a 500 ms per-try timeout and the waits above take up to 3 × 500 + 100 + 200 = 1.8 s
before the last one has finished. With a 2 s deadline that fits; a fourth attempt, 400 ms later,
would not, and a policy that checks the deadline before each wait fails at once instead of
sending an attempt whose answer nobody will read. Envoy does this by design: the route timeout
includes all retries, so a request with 3 s that spends 2.7 s on its first try has 0.3 s left
for every retry and wait together.

What backoff buys:

- **Time for a dependency to come back.** A restarting process, a leader election, a cold cache: a
  retry 100 ms later fails, a retry 1.6 s later succeeds.
- **Less load in a closed system.** A batch worker that waits sends nothing meanwhile.
- **Smaller bursts.** Retries spread over seconds instead of arriving in the same tick.

What it does not buy, in an open system: fewer attempts. Each request still sends 1 + *N* attempts
during an outage; backoff delays the 4× by a second or two. The sim showed the result: backoff
moved the tipping point a little, not the outcome. Brooker's 2022 conclusion is the one to keep:
backoff is a good idea for short spikes and does not reduce the total work or the amplification of
retries. Something else has to do that.

### Jitter: why clients that fail together retry together

**Jitter** is randomness added to each wait. It exists because failures are correlated in time.
When a deploy resets 600 connections, all 600 clients see the error in the same millisecond. With
the same backoff formula and no randomness, they compute the same wait, 500 ms, and send 600
retries in the same millisecond. The server takes 60 of them; the other 540 fail together, wait
together and return together. The clients stay synchronised for as long as they keep retrying.

The common jitter formulas, for a wait *d* = min(cap, base × 2ᵏ⁻¹) before retry *k*. For the third
retry with a 100 ms base, *d* = 400 ms:

| Variant | Wait before retry *k* | Third retry, *d* = 400 ms | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | *d* | exactly 400 ms | urllib3 (jitter is off by default) |
| ±20% | *d* × random(0.8, 1.2) | 320–480 ms | gRPC |
| Equal | *d*/2 + random(0, *d*/2) | 200–400 ms | |
| Full | random(0, *d*) | 0–400 ms | AWS SDKs, Envoy |
| Decorrelated | drawn from a range set by the *previous* wait | varies | Polly (.NET), when jitter is on |

The diagram runs the deploy example: 600 clients fail at once against a server that completes 60
attempts per 100 ms, and retry with a 500 ms base, up to 6 times. Look at the width of each burst.

<Figure caption="With no jitter, the 540 clients that fail together retry together, and each burst is as large as the last: after 8.5 s only half have succeeded. Full jitter spreads the first retries over half a second, close to what the server can take, and every client is done by 2.5 s with 40% fewer attempts.">
  <Jitter />
</Figure>

Full jitter wins here because it spreads each round of retries over the whole interval, from 0 to
*d*, instead of a narrow band around *d*. The ±20% that gRPC uses breaks the exact synchronisation
but leaves each round of retries in a band only 0.4 *d* wide, so the server still sees humps.
Full jitter's one cost is that some waits are close to zero; that is what the cap and the
budget are for.

Jitter is not only for retries. Anything that many machines schedule with the same timer
synchronises the same way: cache entries written together that expire together, cron jobs at
`0 * * * *`, connection lifetimes. The [load balancing page](/traffic/load-balancing/) shows gRPC adding ±10% to
connection ages for exactly this reason.

### Retry budgets: capping the extra load

Backoff and jitter shape *when*. A **retry budget** limits *how many*: retries may add at most a
fixed share to normal traffic, for example 10%, however many attempts fail.

The usual way to build one is a token bucket. A **token** is permission to send one retry. A
**bucket** holds a limited number of tokens. Two rules:

- Every new request (a first attempt) adds a fraction of a token, say 0.1, up to the bucket's size.
- Every retry spends one whole token. A retry that finds less than one token is not sent; the
  request fails at once with the error it already has.

Tiny example: a bucket that holds 10 tokens and starts full. Twenty new requests add 2 tokens; the
bucket is still at its cap of 10. Three of them fail and retry: 7 left. If every request starts
failing, the 10 tokens pay for 10 retries in a burst, and after that each 10 new requests earn one
retry. The ratio of 0.1 means retries can never be more than about 10% of first attempts for long.

<Figure caption="On a normal day the bucket earns more than retries spend, stays full, and every retry is sent. In an outage, 3 retries per request would mean 300 retries a second; the budget lets 10 through, and the other requests fail at once without adding load.">
  <Budget />
</Figure>

That is the property the sim showed: with a budget, amplification is bounded at about 1.1
whatever happens, so a server at 70% load can absorb its own retries, and the queue drains when
the trigger ends. The cost is visible too. In our example run, a dependency failing 20% of
attempts has 99.84% of requests succeed with 3 retries and no budget, and 87.58% with a 10% budget,
because the clients wanted 26% extra attempts and the budget allowed 10%. A budget trades some
success during a brownout for survival during an outage, which is why its ratio is a choice and
not a constant.

Real budgets, as of November 2026:

- **gRPC retry throttling** keeps a count per server name that starts at `maxTokens`. Every failed
  call subtracts 1, every successful call adds `tokenRatio`, and retries stop while the count is at
  or below half of `maxTokens`. With the documented example, 10 and 0.1, the count holds steady when
  0.1 × (1 − *f*) = *f*, a failure rate *f* of about 9.1%. Above that, the count falls to 5 and
  retries stop until successes refill it.
- **AWS SDKs** (standard mode) give each client a 500-token quota. A retry costs 5 tokens, a retry
  after a timeout 10, and a first attempt that succeeds adds 1. That is 100 retries in a burst, then
  about one retry per 5 successful requests.
- **Envoy** limits concurrent retries per upstream cluster: by default 3 at a time (`max_retries`
  in the circuit breaker settings); with a `retry_budget`, 20% of active and pending requests, never
  fewer than 3.
- **Finagle** allows by default about 20% of requests to be retried, on top of 10 retries a
  second so that a new or quiet client can still retry at all.

The minimum matters more than it looks. A pure ratio gives a client that sends 2 requests a minute
no retries at all, so budgets add a floor: Finagle's 10 a second, Envoy's 3 concurrent, a bucket
that starts full.

:::caution[A budget per process is a budget per process]
The bucket lives in each client process. A fleet of 2,000 serverless instances, each with a fresh
SDK client and a full 500-token quota, can send 2,000 × 100 retries before any bucket runs dry.
Brooker's 2022 comparison found the same: a token bucket that starts full, in short-lived
clients, behaves almost like plain N retries. Keep clients alive across requests, share one per
process, and give short-lived workers small buckets.
:::

A circuit breaker is the other way to stop retrying a failing dependency: it watches the failure
rate and stops all calls, first attempts included, for a while. A budget only removes retries, so
first attempts keep probing the dependency and recovery is seen at once. The
circuit breakers page, later in this track, covers when you want the stronger tool.

### Listening to the server

The server knows things the client does not: whether it is overloaded, when a limit resets, and
whether the request can ever succeed. Three ways to say it:

- **`Retry-After`** on a `429` or `503`, as seconds or a date. A client waits at least that long,
  plus jitter, so that clients told the same time do not return in the same second (the
  [rate limiting page](/resilience/rate-limiting/#429-retry-after-and-the-client-on-the-other-side)
  measures the difference). urllib3 honours it on `413`, `429` and `503` by default. Envoy can read
  `Retry-After` or `X-RateLimit-Reset` with `rate_limited_retry_back_off`.
- **gRPC pushback**: the server sets `grpc-retry-pushback-ms` in its response metadata. A
  non-negative value means "retry after exactly this long", and backoff resets after it; a negative
  value means "do not retry". It is the only way a gRPC server can switch its clients' retries off
  during an incident without a config push.
- **Throttling errors get a longer base.** In the 2026 update to the AWS SDKs, a throttling error
  backs off from a 1 s base and other transient errors from 50 ms. A throttle means the limit will
  still be there in 50 ms.

On the server side, the same thinking says: when you are overloaded, answer quickly with `503` and
`Retry-After` instead of slowly with a timeout. A fast refusal costs the server microseconds; a
timeout costs it the whole request, and the client retries either way.

### Hedged requests: retrying for speed

A retry waits for a failure. A **hedged request** does not: if the first attempt has not answered
within a delay, usually around the p95 latency, the client sends a second copy to another replica
and uses whichever answer arrives first, cancelling the other. It cuts the tail, the slowest few
percent of calls, when slowness comes from one replica (a slow disk, a pause) rather than from the
request itself.

<Figure caption="The first copy waits behind a slow disk; the hedge sent at the p95 answers in 12 ms. Only requests slower than the p95 send a second copy, so the cost is about 5% more load on a normal day.">
  <Hedging />
</Figure>

The costs follow from the mechanism. A hedge sends duplicates, so the request must be safe to run
twice: reads, or writes with an idempotency key. On a normal day about 5% of requests send a copy.
On a bad day, when every replica is slow, every request passes the p95 and the extra load is up to
100%, which is why gRPC throttles hedges with the same token count as retries: once failures pass
the threshold, only first attempts are sent. gRPC's `hedgingPolicy` sets `maxAttempts` (5 at most),
`hedgingDelay`, and `nonFatalStatusCodes`, the errors that send the next copy at once; a method has a
retry policy or a hedging policy, never both. Hedging is supported in gRPC's Java, .NET and Node
implementations, not in Go or C-core, according to the gRFC.

## Choosing between them

| Policy | Extra load on a normal day (5% failing) | Extra load in a full outage, 3 retries | What it fixes | Pick it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No retries | 0 | 0 | nothing | the caller (a user, a queue) will retry anyway |
| Retry at once | 5% | 300%, in synchronised bursts | one dropped packet | never as a default |
| Exponential backoff | 5% | 300%, a little later | a dependency restarting; closed systems | always, but never alone |
| Backoff + full jitter | 5% | 300%, spread out | clients failing together | always |
| + retry budget | 5% | about 10% | retry storms, metastable failures | every client of a shared service |
| Hedging | about 5% | up to 100% unless throttled | tail latency from one slow replica | idempotent reads with a long p99 |

The order I would build in: classify failures and check the deadline first, because a wrong
retry is worse than none; then full jitter on a capped exponential; then a budget sized for the
failure rate you want to ride through, 10% to 20%. Retry at one layer, the one closest to the
caller that still knows whether the request is safe to repeat. Add hedging only after you have
measured a tail it would fix.

## Build it yourself

The naive loop from the start of the page, and two steps beyond it: a correct retry that
classifies errors, backs off with full jitter and respects the caller's deadline, then the same
with a retry budget. The full files run each policy for 10,000 requests against a fake dependency;
`sleep` moves a virtual clock, so a run takes a second.

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="retries.py"
# runnable version: examples/retries-and-backoff/python/retries.py
class TransientError(Exception):
    """Worth retrying: a timeout, a reset connection, 503, 429. May carry the server's Retry-After."""

    def __init__(self, message: str = "", retry_after: float | None = None):
        super().__init__(message)
        self.retry_after = retry_after


def backoff_delay(retry: int, base: float, cap: float, rand: Callable[[], float] = random.random) -> float:
    """Full jitter: anywhere between 0 and the capped exponential (base, 2×base, 4×base...)."""
    return rand() * min(cap, base * 2 ** (retry - 1))


def call_with_retries(
    fn: Callable[[], T],
    *,
    deadline: float,  # a time.monotonic() value: the caller's whole budget
    retries: int = 3,
    base: float = 0.1,
    cap: float = 2.0,
    budget: RetryBudget | None = None,
    rand: Callable[[], float] = random.random,
    clock: Callable[[], float] = time.monotonic,
    sleep: Callable[[float], None] = time.sleep,
) -> T:
    if budget:
        budget.on_request()
    retry = 0
    while True:
        try:
            return fn()
        except TransientError as err:  # PermanentError and bugs propagate on the first try
            retry += 1
            if retry > retries:
                raise
            delay = max(err.retry_after or 0.0, backoff_delay(retry, base, cap, rand))
            if clock() + delay >= deadline:
                raise  # the caller will have given up before the next answer arrives
            if budget and not budget.try_spend():
                raise  # many attempts are failing: fail fast, don't add load
            sleep(delay)
```

```js title="retries.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/retries-and-backoff/js/retries.mjs
/** Worth retrying: a timeout, a reset connection, 503, 429. May carry the server's Retry-After. */
export class TransientError extends Error {
  constructor(message = '', retryAfter = null) {
    super(message);
    this.retryAfter = retryAfter;
  }
}

/** Full jitter: anywhere between 0 and the capped exponential (base, 2×base, 4×base...). */
export function backoffDelay(retry, base, cap, random = Math.random) {
  return random() * Math.min(cap, base * 2 ** (retry - 1));
}

const realSleep = (s) => new Promise((resolve) => setTimeout(resolve, s * 1000));

export async function callWithRetries(fn, {
  deadline, // a performance.now()/1000 value: the caller's whole budget, in seconds
  retries = 3, base = 0.1, cap = 2.0, budget = null,
  random = Math.random, clock = () => performance.now() / 1000, sleep = realSleep,
}) {
  budget?.onRequest();
  for (let retry = 1; ; retry++) {
    try {
      return await fn();
    } catch (err) {
      if (!(err instanceof TransientError)) throw err; // PermanentError and bugs: no retry
      if (retry > retries) throw err;
      const delay = Math.max(err.retryAfter ?? 0, backoffDelay(retry, base, cap, random));
      if (clock() + delay >= deadline) throw err; // the caller will have given up by then
      if (budget && !budget.trySpend()) throw err; // many attempts are failing: fail fast
      await sleep(delay);
    }
  }
}
```

Two details carry most of the value. The deadline check happens *before* the wait, so a retry
that cannot finish in time is never sent. And `Retry-After` sets a floor under the backoff rather
than replacing it, so clients told the same time still spread out.

The budget is a dozen lines. One instance per process and dependency, shared by every request:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">

```python title="retries.py"
class RetryBudget:
    """Retries may add at most `ratio` extra load. Share one per client process and dependency."""

    def __init__(self, ratio: float = 0.1, max_tokens: float = 10.0):
        self.ratio, self.max_tokens, self.tokens = ratio, max_tokens, max_tokens
        self._lock = threading.Lock()

    def on_request(self) -> None:  # every new request earns a fraction of a retry
        with self._lock:
            self.tokens = min(self.max_tokens, self.tokens + self.ratio)

    def try_spend(self) -> bool:  # every retry costs a whole one
        with self._lock:
            if self.tokens < 1:
                return False
            self.tokens -= 1
            return True
```
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="JavaScript">
```js title="retries.mjs"
/** Retries may add at most `ratio` extra load. Share one per client process and dependency. */
export class RetryBudget {
  constructor(ratio = 0.1, maxTokens = 10) {
    Object.assign(this, { ratio, maxTokens, tokens: maxTokens });
  }
  onRequest() { this.tokens = Math.min(this.maxTokens, this.tokens + this.ratio); } // earn a fraction
  trySpend() { // spend a whole one
    if (this.tokens < 1) return false;
    this.tokens -= 1;
    return true;
  }
}
```

## How real systems do it

**AWS SDKs** have three retry modes. *Standard* retries up to 3 attempts with full jitter, a
1 s base and a 20 s cap, under the 500-token quota described above. *Adaptive* adds a client-side
rate limiter that can delay first attempts when throttled; AWS calls it experimental. *Legacy* is
each SDK's older behaviour, with no quota, and it is still the default in some: botocore (boto3)
uses legacy, with 5 attempts and 10 for DynamoDB, unless you set `AWS_RETRY_MODE=standard`. A 2026
update changes standard mode: transient errors back off from 50 ms and throttling errors from
1 s, the cap applies before the jitter, and a retry costs 14 tokens (5 after a throttle), so a full
quota pays for about 35 retries rather than 100. As of November 2026 it is opt-in with
`AWS_NEW_RETRIES_2026=true`; AWS's announcement (May 2026) says it becomes the default this month,
and it moves botocore's default mode from legacy to standard. Check your SDK version before
relying on either set of numbers.

**gRPC** retries nothing unless the service config says so. A policy names `maxAttempts`
(counting the first, at most 5), `initialBackoff`, `maxBackoff`, `backoffMultiplier` and the
`retryableStatusCodes`; the deadline covers all attempts. The service config can be served by the
service owner rather than compiled into every client, which puts the retry policy, and the
throttling that stops it, under the control of the team that runs the server:

```json
{
  "methodConfig": [{
    "name": [{ "service": "inventory.Stock" }],
    "retryPolicy": {
      "maxAttempts": 3,
      "initialBackoff": "0.1s",
      "maxBackoff": "1s",
      "backoffMultiplier": 2,
      "retryableStatusCodes": ["UNAVAILABLE"]
    }
  }],
  "retryThrottling": { "maxTokens": 10, "tokenRatio": 0.1 }
}
```

**Envoy** retries only when a route or virtual host has a retry policy, and then once by default
(`num_retries`). Backoff is fully jittered from a 25 ms base: the first retry waits 0–24 ms, the
second 0–74 ms, the third 0–174 ms, capped at 250 ms. `retry_on` names the conditions (`5xx`,
`gateway-error`, `reset`, `connect-failure`, `retriable-4xx`, `refused-stream`, gRPC status names),
and a host predicate such as `previous_hosts` sends the retry to a different backend from the one
that failed, which is most of the value of a retry at a proxy. The route timeout includes every
retry, and the cluster's retry circuit breaker or `retry_budget` caps them.

:::note[From the trenches]
The useful exercise is to list every retry between your user and your database: the mobile app,
the gateway, the service mesh sidecar, your code, the SDK, the database driver. Teams that do it
the first time usually find three or four, each configured by a different person who assumed the
others did not retry.
:::

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[Retries that keep an outage going]
On December 7, 2021, an automated scaling activity in AWS us-east-1 triggered a surge of connection
activity from a large number of clients on AWS's internal network, which overwhelmed the devices
between that network and the main one. AWS's summary describes the loop on this page: the delays
increased latency and errors, which caused even more connection attempts and retries, and the
congestion persisted; a latent issue kept those clients from backing off adequately. Removing the
trigger does not end a metastable failure. Plan how you will shed load or switch retries off,
before you need it.
:::

:::caution[Retries stacked in the libraries you did not write]
Defaults as of November 2026: `requests` retries nothing (`max_retries=0`), but urllib3 used directly
retries 3 times with no backoff. AWS SDKs make 3 to 5 attempts. Polly (.NET) defaults to 3 retries,
2 s apart with no jitter, on any exception. Envoy retries once when a policy exists. Your own loop of
3 around an SDK call of 3 is 9 attempts per request, before the gateway adds its own. Find the
library defaults, turn retries off in all but one layer, and log `attempt` numbers so that a storm
is visible.
:::

:::caution[Retrying a timeout duplicates work]
A timed-out attempt is usually still running on the server (the
[timeouts page](/resilience/timeouts/#a-timeout-stops-the-waiting-not-the-work) covers why). The
retry starts a second copy of the same expensive query beside the first. That is exactly the
wasted work in the sim. Pass the deadline to the server so it can stop, and do not retry a timeout
on an endpoint you know is overloaded.
:::

:::caution[Retrying while holding a lock]
A retry loop inside a database transaction, or while holding a lock or a connection from a pool,
holds it through every backoff wait. Three retries with backoff up to 2 s each can keep a row lock
for 6 s. Retry outside the transaction: roll back, wait, start again.
:::

:::caution[Budgets that hide failures]
A budget that refuses retries turns "slow and recovering" into "fast errors", which is the point,
but the errors must still be counted and alerted on. Log refused retries as their own metric; a
rising count is the earliest signal that a dependency is in trouble.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

Every retry policy trades load for success. No retries puts the failure in front of the user at
once and adds nothing to a struggling server. Unbounded retries hide every blip and turn a long
failure into an overload. A budget sits between, and its ratio decides where: 10% rides through
blips and stops storms; 20%, Finagle's default, rides through worse brownouts and adds more load
during outages.

Retries also cost latency. Each one adds a wait and another attempt to the request's time, and a
request that succeeds on its third try took longer than the user wanted. For interactive paths with
a tight deadline, one retry is often all that fits.

Do not retry when:

- **Someone else will.** A message queue redelivers, a user taps again, a batch job reruns. Retrying
  inside a consumer that the queue will also redeliver multiplies attempts, as stacked layers do.
- **The request is not safe to repeat** and has no idempotency key.
- **The server said no**: a `4xx` other than `408`, `425` and `429`, or a gRPC pushback that says stop.
- **The deadline cannot fit another attempt.**
- **The dependency is known to be overloaded.** Then the server needs less load: shed it there, and
  let callers fail fast.

## Interview corner

Retries come up as a follow-up to any design with a network call: "what happens when the payment
service is slow?" A strong answer covers four things: which failures you retry and why the request
is safe to repeat; that each layer's retries multiply and only one layer should retry; capped
exponential backoff with full jitter; and a budget that caps retries during an outage, with numbers.

Typical questions:

- *"Why add jitter to exponential backoff?"* Clients that fail together compute the same wait and
  retry together, in bursts the size of the first failure. Full jitter spreads each round of
  retries over the whole interval.
- *"Does exponential backoff protect an overloaded server?"* Only from short spikes. In an open
  system new requests keep arriving; each request still sends up to 1 + *N* attempts. A budget
  bounds the extra load; backoff only delays it.
- *"The service recovered, but errors stayed high for an hour. Why?"* A metastable failure:
  retries keep the queue past the clients' timeout, so every answer arrives late. Shed load or
  switch retries off; waiting does not help.
- *"How would you retry a payment?"* Only with an idempotency key, only on errors that a retry can
  fix, at one layer, inside the user's deadline, and with a budget.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Each attempt to a dependency fails with probability 0.3, independently. How many attempts per request do 2 retries send, and what share of requests fail after all of them?</summary>

1 + 0.3 + 0.09 = 1.39 attempts per request. A request fails only if all three attempts fail:
0.3³ = 2.7%. Retries turned a 30% failure rate into 2.7% for 39% more load.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. You change your clients from "retry at once" to exponential backoff with full jitter, still 3 retries. During a full outage, by how much does the load on the dependency fall?</summary>

Barely. Each request still sends 4 attempts; backoff delays the last three by up to a few seconds,
and in an open system new requests keep arriving meanwhile. The load is still close to 4× after
the first few seconds. Only a budget, fewer retries or a circuit breaker reduces it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A gRPC client has retryThrottling with maxTokens 10 and tokenRatio 0.1. Roughly what failure rate stops its retries?</summary>

The count is steady when successes add as much as failures take: 0.1 × (1 − *f*) = *f*, so *f* is
about 9%. Above that, the count falls to half of `maxTokens`, 5, and retries stop while it stays
there. First attempts keep going, and successes refill the count.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. In the simulation, why does a 2 s trigger recover and a 6 s trigger not, with the same retry policy?</summary>

At half speed the queue grows by 20 attempts a second. After 2 s it holds about 40, a wait of
0.4 s at full speed: below the 1 s timeout, so attempts succeed and the queue drains. After 6 s the
wait passes 1 s, attempts time out, each request sends up to 4, and 280 attempts a second against a
capacity of 100 keep the wait above 1 s for good. The trigger decides whether the system crosses the
line; the retries decide whether it stays there.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. 2,000 serverless functions each create a new AWS SDK client per invocation and call a throttled API. Why does the SDK's retry quota not stop a retry storm?</summary>

Each client has its own 500-token quota, full at creation. A client that lives for one invocation
never drains its bucket, so each behaves like plain "3 attempts". Create the client once per
execution environment so its quota persists across invocations, and add a budget or a circuit
breaker that is shared more widely.

</details>

## Recap

- Retries amplify load by 1 + *p* + … + *p*ᴺ: about 1.1× on a healthy day, and 1 + *N* times when
  everything fails, which is when the dependency can least afford it.
- In an open system, retries can hold a server down after its trigger has gone: a metastable
  failure. Safe means load × maximum amplification stays below capacity.
- Exponential backoff with a cap decides when to retry. It helps restarts and closed systems; it
  does not reduce the number of attempts.
- Full jitter, random(0, *d*), desynchronises clients that failed together; ±20% only blurs them.
- A retry budget, tokens earned by new requests and spent by retries, caps the extra load at a ratio.
  It costs some success during brownouts and saves the service during outages.
- Retry only transient failures, only safe requests, only inside the deadline, at one layer, and
  listen to `Retry-After` and pushback.
