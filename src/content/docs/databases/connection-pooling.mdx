## The question

Opening a new connection to a PostgreSQL database took about 10 milliseconds on our test server.
Sending a small query over a connection that was already open took about a twentieth of a
millisecond. So an app should keep its connections open and reuse them. But how many? On the same
server, 8 connections got almost three times as much work done as 512 connections. Why would more
connections make a database do less, and where should the requests wait that do not get one?

The short answer is a **connection pool**. The app opens a small, fixed number of connections once,
keeps them open, and lends each one to one request at a time. A request that finds them all busy
waits its turn inside the app, which costs almost nothing, instead of crowding into the database,
where every extra connection competes for the same few processors. Part 1 walks through this with
one app and real numbers; Part 2 measures each piece.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one small online shop. Its API runs on one server and talks to one PostgreSQL database on a
machine with 4 processor cores. The app works on 8 requests at the same time, and the request we
follow, "show my orders", runs one query that takes the database about 1 ms.

1. **Opening a connection.** Before the app can send its first query, it has to open a connection to
   the database. A few messages go back and forth, the app proves it knows the password, and the
   two sides set up encryption. On our test server that took 9.5 ms. Call it 10 ms.
2. **Using an open connection.** Once a connection is open, the app can send query after query over
   it, and each one starts at once. Our "show my orders" query takes 1 ms.
3. **First try: a new connection for every request.** Each request opens a connection (10 ms), runs
   its query (1 ms) and closes the connection. So each request takes 10 + 1 = 11 ms, and 10 of those
   11 ms go on getting in the door. Eight requests at a time, each taking 11 ms, means at most
   8 ÷ 0.011 s = about 727 requests a second. Our example program, which you can run yourself in
   Part 2, measured 695, a little lower because closing a connection takes time too.
4. **Second try: keep 8 connections open and share them.** The app opens 8 connections the first
   time it needs them and never closes them. That set of open connections is the **pool**. A
   request **borrows** a free connection, runs its 1 ms query, and **returns** it, still open, for
   the next request. Each connection can now serve 1,000 ms ÷ 1 ms = 1,000 requests a second, so the
   8 together can serve up to 8 × 1,000 = 8,000. Our example measured 5,408 requests a second,
   almost eight times as many as before (5,408 ÷ 695 = 7.8), and it opened 8 connections in total
   instead of 400.
5. **When all 8 are busy.** A ninth request arrives while all 8 connections are lent out. It waits
   in a line inside the app until one comes back, usually within a millisecond. Waiting in the app
   is cheap: the database does no work for it, and the app spends a little memory. The pool also
   puts a limit on the wait, say 300 ms. A request that has not got a connection by then fails
   with a clear error instead of hanging forever.
6. **Why not 500 connections, so nobody ever waits?** Because the database cannot run 500 queries
   at once. It has 4 cores, and each core works on one query at a time. With 500 connections all
   sending queries, the queries take turns on the 4 cores, and when they change the same rows they
   also wait for each other. We measured this with a standard test that writes data. 8 connections
   finished 6,598 transactions a second. 512 connections finished 2,370, about a third as many
   (2,370 ÷ 6,598 = 0.36). The slowest request in every hundred went from 3.6 ms to 1,667 ms,
   almost 2 seconds.
7. **How long each request keeps its connection matters as much as how many there are.** Suppose
   the shop's "place order" request keeps its connection while it also waits 200 ms for a payment
   service to answer. One connection can then serve only 1,000 ms ÷ 200 ms = 5 requests a second,
   and the pool of 8 only 8 × 5 = 40, while the database sits almost idle. A request should borrow a
   connection for its database work and return it straight after.

So a pool does three things. It keeps connections open, so requests do not pay 10 ms to get in. It
keeps their number small, about twice the database's cores, so they do not get in each other's way.
And it makes everyone else wait their turn inside the app, with a time limit.

An everyday picture: a restaurant kitchen with 4 stoves. Hiring a new cook for every order and
sending them home afterwards wastes most of the evening on hiring; that is a connection per
request. Keeping 8 cooks on staff works well: while one waits for the oven, another uses a stove.
Hiring 500 cooks for the same 4 stoves fills the kitchen with people waiting and bumping into each
other, and fewer meals come out. Orders that wait are tickets on the rail outside the kitchen. The
picture stops working in one place: a cook with no order costs only a salary, while an open
connection that does nothing still takes memory and a slot in the database, as Part 2 shows.

## The building blocks

Six ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### A database connection

**What it is.** A **database connection** is an open, logged-in line between your app and the
database, over which the app sends queries and gets rows back.

**Why it exists.** The database must check who is asking and what they may see, and the two sides
must agree on how to encrypt what they send. Doing that once and then sending many queries is much
cheaper than doing it for every query.

**Example.** The shop's app opens a connection as user `shop_app` to the database `shop`. That takes
9.5 ms. Over the same connection it then sends thousands of queries, each starting at once.

**The precise version.** Underneath, a connection is a TCP connection: the protocol that gives two
programs a reliable stream of bytes over a network. On the same machine it can instead be a **Unix
socket**, a cheaper channel between two programs that never touches the network. It is usually
wrapped in TLS, the encryption behind HTTPS, and has passed a password check.

On the database side, each connection carries a **session**: the state the database keeps for that
one client. Settings changed with `SET`, an open transaction and the locks it holds all belong to
the session, and they disappear when the connection closes. PostgreSQL runs each connection in its
own **process**, a separate running program with its own memory, which PostgreSQL calls a
**backend**.

### A transaction

**What it is.** A **transaction** is a group of SQL statements that the database applies all
together or not at all.

**Why it exists.** Placing an order changes two tables: it lowers the stock count and adds an order
row. If the app crashed between the two, an item would vanish from stock with no order to show for
it. A transaction makes the pair one unit.

**Example.** The app sends `BEGIN`, then `UPDATE stock SET qty = qty - 1 WHERE item_id = 7`, then
`INSERT INTO orders (user_id, item_id) VALUES (42, 7)`, then `COMMIT`. Only at `COMMIT` do both
changes become real, together. If anything fails before that, the app sends `ROLLBACK` and the stock
change is undone.

**The precise version.** A transaction belongs to the connection, not to the request or the code
that started it. Every statement sent on that connection between `BEGIN` and `COMMIT` is part of it.
That is why one connection can serve only one request at a time, and why a pool must make sure a
returned connection has no transaction left open.

### A lock

**What it is.** A **lock** is a mark the database puts on a row while a transaction changes it, so
that no other transaction can change that row until the first one finishes.

**Why it exists.** Two people ordering the last copy of item 7 must not both see "1 in stock" and
both succeed. The lock makes the second order wait and then see the true count.

**Example.** Transaction A updates the stock row for item 7 at 0 ms and locks it. Transaction B wants
the same row at 1 ms and has to wait. A commits at 2 ms, which releases the lock, and B goes ahead
and sees 0 left. B waited 2 − 1 = 1 ms.

**The precise version.** A row lock is held until the transaction commits or rolls back, not only
while one statement runs. So anything slow inside a transaction, including waiting for another
service, keeps every other transaction that needs those rows waiting too. A transaction that is
waiting for a lock still holds its connection the whole time.

### CPU cores: how much a machine does at once

**What it is.** A **core** is one processor inside a computer's CPU. Each core runs one piece of
work at a time.

**Why it exists here.** To know how many queries a database can really run at the same moment,
count its cores. Every query beyond that number takes turns.

**Example.** Our test database has 4 cores, and one of our test queries needs 2 ms of processor time.
Four such queries at once each get a core and each finish in 2 ms. Eight at once means each core
switches between two queries, so each query finishes in about 2 × 2 = 4 ms. The total work done per
second is the same; every query only waits longer.

**The precise version.** The operating system shares cores between processes by switching between
them many times a second, and every switch costs a little time. A few more busy connections than
cores help, because at any moment some of them are waiting for the disk or for a lock and leave a
core free. Hundreds more add switching and waiting, and nothing else.

### Waiting in line, with a time limit

**What it is.** When every connection in the pool is lent out, a request that asks for one joins a
**queue**: a line of waiting requests, served in the order they arrived.

**Why it exists.** The other choices are worse. Failing at once is wasteful when a connection will
probably be free in a millisecond. Opening another connection brings back the problem of too many.

**Example.** The pool of 8 is fully lent out. Requests 9 and 10 arrive, and request 9 is first in
line. Half a millisecond later a connection comes back and goes to request 9. Request 10 gets the
next one.

**The precise version.** Taking a connection from the pool is called **acquiring** it, and giving it
back is **releasing** it. The **acquire timeout** is the longest a request will wait in the queue
before it gives up with an error, for example 300 ms. Without one, a request can wait forever. The
[latency and throughput page](/foundations/latency-vs-throughput/) explains why lines like this
grow so fast as they near full.

### Hold time, and how much a pool can carry

**What it is.** **Hold time** is how long a request keeps a connection: from the moment it acquires
it to the moment it releases it.

**Why it exists.** While one request holds a connection, no other request can use it, even if no
query is running on it. So hold time, not query time, decides how many requests a pool can serve.

**Example.** A connection held for 2 ms per request can serve 1,000 ms ÷ 2 ms = 500 requests a
second. A pool of 10 such connections can serve 10 × 500 = 5,000 a second. Now let each request also
hold its connection during a 200 ms call to a payment service. Hold time becomes 2 + 200 = 202 ms.
One connection serves 1,000 ms ÷ 202 ms = about 5 requests a second, and the pool of 10 about
10 × 5 = 50.

**The precise version.** The most a pool can serve per second is its size divided by the hold time
in seconds: 10 ÷ 0.002 s = 5,000 a second, and 10 ÷ 0.202 s = 49.5 a second. This is a form of
**Little's law** from the latency page: the number of connections in use equals requests per
second times the time each one is held. Requests beyond that rate pile up in the queue until the
acquire timeout fails them.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- Opening a database connection is slow (about 10 ms on our server); a query on an open one is fast.
  So apps keep a **pool** of open connections and lend each one to one request at a time.
- Keep the pool small: a database with 4 cores did the most work with about 8 busy connections, and
  far less with 512, because they take turns on the cores and wait for each other's locks.
- A request that finds every connection busy waits in a line inside the app, which is cheap, and
  gives up after an **acquire timeout** instead of hanging.
- What a pool can carry is its size divided by how long each request **holds** a connection, so
  never keep a connection while waiting for something else, such as a payment service.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 had one app and one pool. Real services raise harder questions. When fifty copies of your
app each bring their own pool and the database allows 100 connections, who decides the total, and
what happens during a deploy? Why did 512 connections do about a third of the work of 8 on the same
4 cores? And when you put a shared pooler such as PgBouncer in front of the database, why can a
setting one client changes break a different client's query?

To answer them, start with what a connection really costs the database: to open, to keep and to
use. Every number on this page comes from a PostgreSQL 16 database we ran on 4 virtual CPUs (cores
rented in the cloud). The scripts and our full output are in
[`examples/connection-pooling`](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/connection-pooling).

**Opening it.** `pgbench`, the benchmark tool that ships with PostgreSQL, can open a new connection
for every query, which isolates the cost of opening one. Each row below runs the query `select 1`,
which does almost no work, so what is left is the cost of connecting. "trust" means no password
check at all, and SCRAM is PostgreSQL's password check, explained under the table.

| How each `select 1` connects | Average time per query |
|---|---|
| reuses an open connection, Unix socket | 0.040 ms |
| reuses an open connection, TCP + TLS + password (SCRAM) | 0.054 ms |
| new connection, Unix socket, no password (trust) | 2.26 ms |
| new connection, TCP, no password (trust) | 2.35 ms |
| new connection, TCP, password (SCRAM) | 6.68 ms |
| new connection, TCP + TLS + password (SCRAM) | 9.52 ms |

The table says one thing: a new connection costs tens to hundreds of times more than a query on an
open one. With TLS and a password, 9.52 ms ÷ 0.054 ms = about 176 times more. Each step down the
table adds one piece of work:

- **2.3 ms with no password** is mostly the database starting a new backend process and loading the
  first data it needs about its tables.
- **SCRAM adds about 4.3 ms** (6.68 − 2.35 = 4.33 ms). **SCRAM** (SCRAM-SHA-256, PostgreSQL's default
  password method) lets the app prove it knows the password without sending it. It does this by
  **hashing** the password, turning it into a scrambled value that cannot be turned back, and it
  does so 4,096 times in a row on purpose. That makes each attempt slow, so a thief who steals the
  stored password data needs far longer to try guesses. The cost runs in your application, on every
  new connection.
- **TLS adds about 2.8 ms** (9.52 − 6.68 = 2.84 ms), for agreeing on encryption keys.

All of this ran on one machine. Across a network, every message exchange in the handshake also adds
the network's travel time, which the step-through below counts.

**Keeping it open.** We opened 500 connections and left them idle. Each backend held 1.29 MB of
memory of its own, so the 500 together held 500 × 1.29 MB = 646 MB doing nothing. Each connection
also takes one of the database's **slots**: PostgreSQL accepts at most `max_connections`
connections, 100 by default, and keeps 3 of them for **superusers** (administrator accounts), so
applications get 100 − 3 = 97.

Until PostgreSQL 14 (2021), idle connections also slowed down the busy ones. Andres Freund measured
one busy connection running 33,457 transactions a second on its own, and 14,496 with 10,000 idle
connections next to it: less than half (14,496 ÷ 33,457 = 0.43). The cause was work every query did
to find out which other connections had transactions running, and it grew with the number of
connections. His fix went into version 14
([Citus, 2020](https://www.citusdata.com/blog/2020/10/08/analyzing-connection-scalability/)).

**Using it.** A backend runs one query at a time on one core. With more busy backends than cores
they take turns, and past a point they get in each other's way. That is the 6,598 against 2,370 from
Part 1, and it gets its own section below.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

Part 1 measured the first fix, a new connection for every request: the pool of 8 served almost
eight times as many requests a second. Connecting per request also makes the database start and stop a process for every request, and
each closed connection leaves a record behind on the client machine for a while, in a TCP state
called TIME-WAIT that the [TCP page](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/) explains.

**"Then share one connection for the whole app."** A connection runs one statement at a time, so
every request queues behind every other one. Worse, the transaction belongs to the connection, not
to the request. If two requests mix their `BEGIN`, `UPDATE` and `COMMIT` on the same connection, one
request's `COMMIT` commits the other's half-finished work.

**"Then a big pool, so nobody ever waits."** Services run several copies of the app. Each running
copy is often called a **pod**, the name the Kubernetes platform uses, and each pod has its own pool. Ten pods
with a pool of 50 each can open 10 × 50 = 500 connections, against the 97 slots applications get.
Connection number 98 fails with `FATAL: sorry, too many clients already`. Raise `max_connections` to
1,000 and the connections fit, but now they all try to run at once, which is how 512 connections did
about a third of the work of 8. The waiting has not gone away. It has moved inside the database,
where it costs processor time and holds locks.

Three neighbours get confused with a connection pool:

- A **thread** is one line of work inside a program; a program can run several threads at once,
  each handling one request. A **thread pool** limits how many requests your app works on at once.
  A connection pool limits how many it sends to the database at once. The two are sized
  separately, and the connection pool is usually the smaller.
- **HTTP keep-alive** reuses connections to other web services for the same reason: opening one is
  slow. But HTTP/2 can carry many requests on one connection at the same time, while a database
  connection carries one transaction at a time.
- An **external pooler** such as PgBouncer is a separate program that pools connections for many
  apps at once. It comes at the end of the next section.

## How it works, piece by piece

### Opening a connection, round trip by round trip

**What a round trip is.** A **round trip** is one message sent and its answer received.

**Why it matters.** Each round trip costs the network's travel time there and back, however small
the messages are. That travel time is called the **round-trip time**, or RTT.

**Example.** If the database is 1 ms away there and back, a handshake of 6 round trips costs
6 × 1 ms = 6 ms before any work is done.

**The precise version.** Here is a PostgreSQL 16 connection with TLS and a SCRAM password, message
by message. Two new words appear in it: a **nonce** is a random number used only once, so an old
message cannot be replayed, and a **salt** is a random value stored with the password data, so two
users with the same password get different hashes.

On one machine the round trips cost almost nothing, so the 9.5 ms we measured is nearly all
processor work. Across a network, the six round trips add their travel time on top. Cloud regions
are split into **availability zones**, separate data centres a few kilometres apart, usually about
1 ms from each other there and back; between two zones the handshake adds 6 × 1 ms = 6 ms. From an
app in another region with a 50 ms round trip, it adds 6 × 50 ms = 300 ms before the first query.
Those two travel times are illustrative, but the six round trips are not, and they are why a
function running far from its database should never open a connection per request.

### Borrow, use, return: a pooled connection's life

Part 1 described acquire and release. A real pool does four things, and every pool library has a
setting for each.

<Figure caption="acquire() tries the cheap options first and fails rather than hanging. A released connection is cleaned up before reuse, and closed for good when it is too old, idle too long or broken.">
  <Lifecycle />
</Figure>

The top half of the figure is acquire; the bottom half is the life of one connection.

- **Acquire.** Take an idle connection if there is one. Otherwise, open a new one if the pool is
  below its maximum. Otherwise, wait in the queue until a connection is released or the acquire
  timeout runs out.
- **Check.** A connection can die while it sits idle: the database restarted, or a firewall (a network
  device that filters traffic) on the way forgot about it. Some pools test every connection before lending it, which costs a round
  trip each time (SQLAlchemy's `pool_pre_ping`). Most test idle connections in the background, or
  rely on retiring them early, and let a failed query report the error.
- **Release.** If the request left a transaction open, the pool rolls it back. Most pools inside
  your app stop there: anything changed with `SET` stays on the connection for the next request, a
  problem the gotchas come back to.
- **Retire.** Close a connection after a **maximum lifetime**, even if it is healthy. That way old
  connections move over after a **failover** (the database switching to a standby copy when the
  main one fails) or after the database's address changes in DNS, the internet's address book. It also replaces them before a
  network device on the way drops them without telling anyone. Good pools add **jitter**, a small
  random amount, to the lifetime, so connections opened together do not all close in the same
  second. Pools also close connections that sit idle too long, down to a minimum number.

The table lists the most common pool libraries for Node.js, Python, Java and Go. Their default
settings differ more than most people expect. We read them from each library's source
code or documentation in October 2026. What to notice: two of them never stop a request from
waiting or a pool from growing.

| Pool | Maximum connections | Waiting for a connection | Maximum lifetime |
|---|---|---|---|
| node-postgres `pg.Pool` 3.14 | 10 | forever, unless `connectionTimeoutMillis` is set | off |
| psycopg `ConnectionPool` 3.3 | `min_size`, which is 4 | 30 s | 1 hour, up to 3 minutes earlier at random (5% of 60 minutes) |
| SQLAlchemy 2.1 `QueuePool` | 5, plus 10 "overflow" ones it opens under load and closes later: 15 at most | 30 s | off (`pool_recycle` −1) |
| HikariCP (Java) | 10 | 30 s | 30 min |
| Go `database/sql` | unlimited | never waits: it opens another | unlimited |

node-postgres will queue a request for a connection forever, and Go will open as many connections
as there are requests running at once, so the database's `max_connections` becomes the only limit
([Go docs](https://go.dev/doc/database/manage-connections)). Set both explicitly.

### Hold time, not query time, sets the capacity

Part 1 worked out that a pool of 10 serves at most 10 ÷ 0.202 s = 49.5 requests a second when each
request holds its connection through a 200 ms payment call. Here is what that looks like from the
database's side.

The database is almost idle, yet requests time out waiting for a connection. PostgreSQL has a
built-in view, `pg_stat_activity`, that lists every connection and what it is doing. These
connections show up there as `idle in transaction`: a transaction is open, but no query is running.
They also keep their row locks for the whole 200 ms, so other transactions that touch the same rows
wait too.

The fix is in the code, not the pool size: make the slow call before `BEGIN` or after `COMMIT`. Our
example program shows the size of the effect. With a pool of 4 and a 50 ms call, moving the call out
of the transaction took the service from 74 to 618 requests a second. The database has a guard as
well: `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout` ends any session that sits in an open transaction longer
than you allow.

The simulation below puts a pool in front of a database with 4 cores. Every request needs 10 ms of
processor time, split over two queries, so the 4 cores can finish at most 4 ÷ 0.010 s = 400 requests
a second. You choose the pool size, how many requests arrive per second, how long each request holds
its connection while doing something outside the database, and the acquire timeout.

<PoolSim />

What the sim shows, in two moves. First, a pool can run out while the database is idle. With
50 ms held outside the database, each request holds its connection for at least 10 + 50 = 60 ms, so
a pool of 8 serves at most 8 ÷ 0.060 s = about 133 requests a second, below the 250 arriving; a
pool of 24 serves up to 24 ÷ 0.060 s = 400. Second, a pool can be too big: when everyone gets a
connection, the cores are shared so thinly that less work gets done.

### How many connections the database wants

Why would more connections ever mean less work? To see it, we need a way to describe slow requests.

**Percentiles.** A **percentile** says how slow a request is compared with all the others. Sort the
times of 100 requests from fastest to slowest: the 50th is the **p50**, or median, the time half of
the requests beat. The 99th is the **p99**: only 1 request in 100 was slower. We use p99 because
averages hide the slow requests that users notice.

Start with the plain case: a query that needs about 2 ms of processor time and nothing else (adding
up 20,000 generated numbers). We ran it with 1 to 512 connections, each sending queries back to
back. What to notice: queries per second stop growing at 8 connections, and the times keep growing.

| Connections | Queries/s | p50 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 516 | 1.8 ms | 3.3 ms |
| 4 | 1,753 | 1.9 ms | 6.8 ms |
| 8 | 1,966 | 3.7 ms | 9.7 ms |
| 64 | 2,007 | 35 ms | 83 ms |
| 512 | 1,826 | 186 ms | 624 ms |

**Throughput**, the number of queries finished per second, stops growing once the 4 cores are busy, and from there every extra connection only adds
waiting. Little's law, turned around, gives the number: the time each query takes is the number in
progress divided by the number finished per second. 64 queries ÷ 2,007 queries per second =
0.032 s, about 32 ms. Compared with 8 connections, the other
64 − 8 = 56 added nothing but that waiting.

Writes are worse, because transactions wait for each other, not only for cores. `pgbench`'s
standard `tpcb-like` test imitates a bank: each transaction runs 7 statements, 3 of them updates.
At the size we used, there are only 10 rows in its `branches` table, and every transaction updates
one of them. A transaction holding a row lock blocks the next one that needs that row, and a blocked
backend still holds its connection and its process. With 512 connections and 10 rows, about
512 ÷ 10 = 51 connections compete for each row.

| Connections | Transactions/s | p50 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,486 | 0.6 ms | 1.5 ms |
| 4 | 3,727 | 1.0 ms | 2.6 ms |
| 8 | **6,598** | 1.0 ms | 3.6 ms |
| 64 | 4,802 | 8.2 ms | 74 ms |
| 128 | 4,118 | 15 ms | 201 ms |
| 512 | 2,370 | 93 ms | 1,667 ms |

What to notice: the peak is at 8 connections, twice the 4 cores, and both columns get worse after
it. Why 8 and not 4? Before a commit counts as done, PostgreSQL writes it to a log file on disk,
the **write-ahead log** or WAL. While one backend waits for that write, or for a lock, another can
use the core.

Past the peak, three things go wrong. Lock queues get longer. The operating system switches between
more processes. And each process's recent data falls out of the **CPU cache**, a small, very fast
memory on each core, so it runs slower when it gets its turn.

Real workloads differ, so the honest rule is: start near **twice the number of cores** for a
database whose data fits in memory, which is 2 × 4 = 8 for ours. Prisma ORM 6, a popular database library for
Node.js, defaults to twice the cores plus one, which is 2 × 4 + 1 = 9 for 4 cores
([Prisma docs](https://www.prisma.io/docs/orm/v6/prisma-client/setup-and-configuration/databases-connections/connection-pool)).
Go higher only when queries spend most of their time waiting on disk, and measure throughput and
p99 while you change it.

That number is for the whole database, not for one app server. Every process that opens its own
pool takes its share of the budget. In the figure, 6 pods with a pool of 10 each use
6 × 10 = 60 connections. Add 10 kept for migrations (scripts that change the tables), scheduled jobs and people, and the 3 superuser
slots: 60 + 10 + 3 = 73, leaving 100 − 73 = 27 free. During a **rolling deploy** (replacing old pods
with new ones, starting each new pod before the old one stops), 12 pods run for a while: 12 × 10 =
120 connections, plus the same 13, is 133. That is 133 − 100 = 33 more than the database accepts.

<Figure caption="Pods × pool size must fit under max_connections with room for jobs and people. A rolling deploy runs old and new pods side by side and doubles the demand; a pooler takes the multiplication out of the database.">
  <Budget />
</Figure>

The multiplication hides in more places:

- Python web servers such as Gunicorn run several **worker processes** in each pod, and each worker
  has its own pool. 12 pods × 4 workers = 48 workers, and 48 × a pool of 10 = 480 connections.
- **Autoscaling**, adding pods automatically when traffic rises, adds them exactly when the database
  is busiest.
- A rolling deploy starts new pods before old ones stop, as the figure shows.

So write the budget down. Pods at their maximum scale, times processes per pod, times pool size,
plus migrations, scheduled jobs and people typing into `psql`, PostgreSQL's command-line tool, must stay under `max_connections`
minus the reserved slots.

### Waiting: the queue, the timeout, and failing fast

When every connection is busy, a request waits somewhere. The pool's queue is the cheap place to
wait: a waiting request costs a little memory in your app and nothing in the database. The
expensive place is inside the database, which is what an oversized pool does. Two settings keep the
cheap queue honest.

**The acquire timeout must be shorter than the request's deadline.** A **deadline** is the total
time a request is allowed before its caller gives up. If an HTTP request has 2 seconds and the pool
will wait 30, the caller has gone long before the pool gives up, and the app may still run the
query for nobody. With an acquire timeout of 300 ms, the request fails fast. The error says "pool
exhausted" instead of "timeout", which tells the load balancer in front of your servers, and any code that retries, what happened.

**A bounded queue.** psycopg can also cap the number of waiting requests (`max_waiting`) and reject
new ones beyond it. That is **load shedding** at the pool's door: refusing some work on purpose so
the work already accepted can finish in time.

The second trap is **pool deadlock**. A **deadlock** is two parties each waiting for something the
other holds, so neither can move. In a pool it looks like this. A request holds one connection and,
before releasing it, asks the same pool for a second one, say to write a row to an audit log
"outside" its transaction. With a pool of 2 and two such requests at the same time, each holds one connection and
waits for the other's.

No database lock is involved, so the database's own deadlock detector never sees it. Without an
acquire timeout, both requests wait forever. With one, one of them fails and frees its connection,
and the other finishes:

```text
3. pool of 2, two requests that each need a second connection while holding the first
   request 1: PoolTimeout at 0.5s: no connection within 0.5s (2 in use)
   request 2: done at 0.5s
```

Any pool can do this once the number of requests that each hold one connection and want another
reaches the pool size. The fix is in the structure of the code: pass the connection you hold down to
the code that needs it, or give the second kind of work its own small pool. The Go documentation
warns about the same thing as soon as you set a maximum.

:::note[From the trenches]
The first metric to add for a pool is not the number of connections; it is how long requests wait
to acquire one. Waiting near zero while requests are slow means the database is slow. Waiting high with an
idle database means connections are being held, or leaked, somewhere in your code.
:::

### External poolers: one pool for many processes

A pool inside your app can only share connections within one process. When there are hundreds of
processes, the budget cannot be met that way. The same goes for **serverless functions**: small
pieces of code a cloud platform starts on demand, sometimes thousands at once, each with its own
pool of one or two connections.

An **external pooler** is a **proxy** for the database: a separate program that sits between the
apps and the database and passes messages along. Apps open many cheap connections to it, and it
keeps a few real connections to the database and lends them out. PgBouncer is the classic one for
PostgreSQL. It is **single-threaded**, doing all its work on one thread and so on one core, and it
can hold thousands of app connections.

Two words keep the two sides apart: **client connections** go from the apps to the pooler, and
**server connections** go from the pooler to the database. The question that defines a pooler is
when it takes a server connection back from a client. That choice is its **pool mode**:

- **Session mode** (PgBouncer's default): when the client disconnects. A client owns one server
  connection the whole time it is connected, so this saves the cost of opening connections, not the
  number of them.
- **Transaction mode**: when the client's transaction ends. Between transactions the client holds
  nothing, so many mostly idle clients share a few server connections.
- **Statement mode**: after every statement, which rules out transactions of more than one statement.

The figure shows two clients, A and B, each running two short transactions with idle time between
them.

<Figure caption="In session mode each client owns a server connection while it is connected, idle or not. In transaction mode a server connection is lent for one transaction, so idle clients cost nothing, and whatever a client leaves on the connection is still there for the next one.">
  <PoolModes />
</Figure>

We put PgBouncer 1.22 in transaction mode, with 8 server connections, in front of the same database,
and ran the same two tests with 512 clients. What to notice: for writes, more transactions and a far
shorter p99 through the pooler.

| 512 clients | Direct: tx/s, p99 | Through PgBouncer: tx/s, p99 |
|---|---|---|
| `tpcb-like` writes | 2,370, 1,667 ms | 3,347, 199 ms |
| 2 ms CPU query | 1,826, 624 ms | 1,826, 331 ms |

PgBouncer did not make the database faster. It kept only 8 transactions inside the database at
once, so the writes stopped fighting over locks. And it served its queue in order, so nobody's wait
ran to 1.7 seconds.

The median got worse: 152 ms through the pooler against 93 ms direct, for the writes. That follows
from Little's law: 512 clients ÷ 3,347 transactions per second = 0.153 s each, about 150 ms,
wherever the waiting happens.

The pooler also has a price when there are few clients. With only 8 clients, PgBouncer did 3,813
transactions a second against 6,598 direct, a little more than half (3,813 ÷ 6,598 = 0.58). Each of
the 7 statements makes an extra hop through a process that shares the same 4 cores with everything
else. An external pooler earns its place when there are many more clients than the database can
serve at once, not before.

**What transaction mode breaks.** A client's "session" is now a different server connection from
one transaction to the next, and each server connection's session belongs to whoever used it last.
To see it, we gave PgBouncer a pool of one server connection and connected clients one after the
other. Two pieces of session state are in the output. `statement_timeout` is a setting that cancels
any statement running longer than the limit. An **advisory lock** is a lock the app takes on a
number of its own choosing, not on a row, to coordinate its own work, such as making sure only one
copy of a nightly job runs.

```text
client A: backend 5765, statement_timeout 50ms
client B: backend 5765, statement_timeout 50ms
client B: ERROR:  canceling statement due to statement timeout
after client C disconnected, advisory lock 42 is still held by backend 5765
```

Read it like this. All three clients got the same backend, number 5765. Client A ran
`SET statement_timeout = 50`, and that setting was still in force for client B, so B's query was
cancelled after 50 ms. Client C took advisory lock 42 and disconnected, and the lock outlived C's
connection.

Other session state leaks the same way:

- `SET search_path`, which picks the schema (a named group of tables) that queries look in first;
- `LISTEN`, which subscribes the session to notifications;
- temporary tables, which exist only for one session;
- SQL-level `PREPARE`, explained next.

The safe forms are scoped to the transaction: `SET LOCAL` inside a transaction, and
`pg_advisory_xact_lock`, which releases the lock at commit.

**Prepared statements.** A **prepared statement** is a query the database has already read and
planned once and kept under a name, so later runs only send the values. Many **drivers**, the libraries
your app uses to talk to the database, use them for every query inside the messages they send,
without you writing `PREPARE`. They used to fail in transaction
mode too: a statement prepared on one server connection did not exist on the next.

PgBouncer 1.21 (2023) learned to track prepared statements and prepare them again where needed, and
1.24 (January 2025) turned this on by default with `max_prepared_statements = 200`
([PgBouncer changelog](https://www.pgbouncer.org/changelog.html)). On our 1.22 with tracking off,
`pgbench -M prepared` stopped at once with `prepared statement "P_0" already exists`. With tracking
on, it ran at 44,321 transactions a second.

## Choosing between them

The table puts the five setups side by side. What to notice: each step down saves more connections
and costs either an extra hop or some session features.

| Setup | What it saves | What it costs | Pick it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| No pool, connect per request | nothing | 2–10 ms and a process per request; no limit on connections | scripts and one-off jobs |
| Pool inside the app | connection setup; caps one process | the budget multiplies by processes and pods | a few long-running app servers |
| Pooler, session mode | connection setup for short-lived clients | an extra hop; idle clients still hold a connection | clients that need session features |
| Pooler, transaction mode | connections: many clients, few server connections | an extra hop per statement; session state leaks | many pods or serverless functions, short transactions |
| Managed proxy (RDS Proxy, Supavisor) | the same, run for you | falls back to one connection per client when it cannot share safely | you already run on that platform |

How to choose: start with a pool inside every app process, sized from the database's budget rather
than from the app's traffic, with an acquire timeout shorter than the request deadline. Add an
external pooler in transaction mode when the sum of all pools no longer fits, when connection counts
swing with autoscaling, or when serverless functions connect to the database. Keep the pools inside
the apps behind it, smaller.

## Build it yourself

A pool is about 60 lines once you see the three cases of acquire. Both versions use only the
standard library, with a stand-in database whose `connect()` takes 10 ms, as ours did with TLS and
SCRAM, and whose queries take 1 ms. Swap in a real driver's connect and the pool works unchanged.

  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="pool.py (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/connection-pooling/python/pool.py
class Pool:
    """A bounded pool: at most max_size connections, callers wait up to `timeout` seconds."""

    def __init__(self, connect, max_size: int, timeout: float, max_lifetime: float = 1800.0):
        self.connect, self.max_size, self.timeout = connect, max_size, timeout
        self.max_lifetime = max_lifetime
        self.idle: deque[FakeConnection] = deque()
        self.size = 0  # open connections, idle or in use
        self.cond = threading.Condition()

    def acquire(self) -> FakeConnection:
        deadline = time.monotonic() + self.timeout
        with self.cond:
            while True:
                if self.idle:  # 1. reuse an idle connection
                    return self.idle.pop()
                if self.size < self.max_size:  # 2. room to open one
                    self.size += 1
                    break
                left = deadline - time.monotonic()  # 3. wait for a release, but not forever
                if left <= 0:
                    raise PoolTimeout(f"no connection within {self.timeout}s ({self.size} in use)")
                self.cond.wait(left)
        try:
            return self.connect()  # outside the lock: connecting is slow
        except BaseException:
            with self.cond:
                self.size -= 1
                self.cond.notify()
            raise

    def release(self, conn: FakeConnection, broken: bool = False) -> None:
        if conn.in_transaction and not broken:
            conn.execute("ROLLBACK")  # never hand the next caller someone else's transaction
        # Retire old connections, a little early at random so they do not all expire together.
        too_old = time.monotonic() - conn.created > self.max_lifetime * random.uniform(0.95, 1.0)
        with self.cond:
            if broken or too_old:
                conn.close()
                self.size -= 1
            else:
                self.idle.append(conn)
            self.cond.notify()
```

```js title="pool.mjs (excerpt)"
// runnable version: examples/connection-pooling/js/pool.mjs
class Pool {
  constructor({ connect, maxSize, timeoutMs, maxLifetimeMs = 1_800_000 }) {
    Object.assign(this, { connect, maxSize, timeoutMs, maxLifetimeMs });
    this.idle = [];
    this.size = 0; // open connections, idle or in use, plus ones being opened
    this.waiters = []; // FIFO of { resolve, timer }
  }

  async acquire() {
    if (this.idle.length) return this.idle.pop(); // 1. reuse an idle connection
    if (this.size < this.maxSize) { // 2. room to open one
      this.size++;
      try {
        return await this.connect();
      } catch (err) {
        this.size--;
        this.#wakeOne(null);
        throw err;
      }
    }
    return new Promise((resolve, reject) => { // 3. wait for a release, but not forever
      const waiter = { resolve };
      waiter.timer = setTimeout(() => {
        this.waiters.splice(this.waiters.indexOf(waiter), 1);
        reject(new PoolTimeout(`no connection within ${this.timeoutMs} ms (${this.size} in use)`));
      }, this.timeoutMs);
      this.waiters.push(waiter);
    });
  }

  async release(conn, { broken = false } = {}) {
    if (conn.inTransaction && !broken) await conn.execute('ROLLBACK'); // never pass on a transaction
    // Retire old connections, a little early at random so they do not all expire together.
    const tooOld = performance.now() - conn.created > this.maxLifetimeMs * (0.95 + 0.05 * Math.random());
    if (broken || tooOld) {
      conn.close();
      this.size--;
      this.#wakeOne(null);
    } else {
      this.#wakeOne(conn);
    }
  }

  /** Hands conn to the oldest waiter, or keeps it idle. With conn = null, a slot freed up. */
  #wakeOne(conn) {
    const waiter = this.waiters.shift();
    if (!waiter) {
      if (conn) this.idle.push(conn);
      return;
    }
    clearTimeout(waiter.timer);
    if (conn) return waiter.resolve(conn);
    this.size++;
    waiter.resolve(this.connect().catch((err) => { this.size--; throw err; }));
  }
}
```

</Tabs>

Read the Python version like this. `idle` is the list of open connections nobody is using, and
`size` counts every open connection, idle or lent out. `acquire()` tries the three cases from the
figure in order: reuse an idle connection, open a new one if `size` is below the maximum, or wait.
The waiting uses a `threading.Condition`, which is a lock plus a way for a thread to sleep until
another thread calls `notify()`; `release()` calls it when it puts a connection back. The
JavaScript version does the same with a list of waiting promises, each with a timer that rejects it
when the acquire timeout runs out.

Two details carry the design. The slow `connect()` runs outside the lock, after reserving a slot,
so one slow handshake does not block every other caller. And the JavaScript version hands a
released connection straight to the oldest waiter instead of putting it back on the idle list,
which keeps the queue first-come, first-served.

The full files add a `with`-style helper that releases the connection on every path, errors
included, and three experiments. Both languages print the same results (timings vary by machine):

```text
1. 400 requests, 8 threads, one query each
   new connection per request:   695 req/s, 400 connections opened
   pool of 8:                   5408 req/s, 8 connections opened
2. 200 requests, 40 threads, pool of 4: what is done while holding the connection
   payment call after COMMIT              618 req/s
   payment call inside the transaction     74 req/s
3. pool of 2, two requests that each need a second connection while holding the first
   request 1: PoolTimeout at 0.5s: no connection within 0.5s (2 in use)
   request 2: done at 0.5s
```

The second experiment is the hold-time arithmetic made visible. Each request runs three queries of
1 ms (`BEGIN`, `UPDATE`, `COMMIT`) and, in the slow version, a 50 ms call between them, so it holds
its connection for 3 + 50 = 53 ms. A pool of 4 can then serve at most 4 ÷ 0.053 s = 75 requests a
second, and the run got 74. The PostgreSQL and PgBouncer measurements on this page come from
[`examples/connection-pooling/postgres/run.sh`](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/connection-pooling/postgres),
which starts a throwaway database and runs them all in about 9 minutes.

## How real systems do it

**PostgreSQL** still runs one process per connection, as of version 18 (2025). Version 14 removed the
worst cost of idle connections, the slowdown Freund measured. Version 16 added
`reserved_connections`, which keeps a few slots for roles (database user accounts) granted `pg_use_reserved_connections`, so
an operator can still log in when the apps have taken every other slot. The number to plan with is
still the count of busy connections, and every managed PostgreSQL service either ships a pooler or
recommends one.

**Figma** ran PgBouncer in front of its PostgreSQL databases and in 2026 replaced it with PGKeeper, a
pooler written in Go
([Figma, 2026](https://www.figma.com/blog/pgkeeper-building-the-bouncer-we-needed-for-postgres/)).
Their reasons match this page's trade-offs. PgBouncer's single thread capped how far one instance
could grow. It could not put important traffic ahead of misbehaving traffic. And nothing stopped a
**reconnection storm**: after an outage, every pooler reopening its connections at once and
overloading the database again. PGKeeper limits how fast connections are opened and closed and lets
queries in by priority. Figma gives each database a pool of about 100 connections, shared by
hundreds of copies of the service that routes queries: the budget idea at large scale.

**Amazon RDS Proxy**, the pooler for Amazon's managed database service, pools in transaction mode by default, which AWS calls **multiplexing**: many
client connections taking turns on a few server connections. It handles the session-state problem
by **pinning**: when it cannot tell whether reusing a server connection is safe, it ties the client
to one server connection until the client disconnects. For PostgreSQL, one `SET` is enough to pin a
session. The metric `DatabaseConnectionsCurrentlySessionPinned` in CloudWatch, AWS's monitoring
service, shows how much sharing you have lost
([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/UserGuide/rds-proxy-pinning.html)).

**Supabase** built Supavisor, a pooler written in Elixir (a language built to run very many small tasks at once), for platforms with huge numbers of mostly
idle clients. Their 2023 test held one million client connections open through the pooler
([Supabase, 2023](https://supabase.com/blog/supavisor-1-million)).

**Vercel** meets the same problem from the serverless side. Its Fluid compute keeps a pool in each
function instance and offers `attachDatabasePool`, which closes idle connections before an instance
is paused, instead of leaving them open on the database
([Vercel docs](https://vercel.com/kb/guide/efficiently-manage-database-connection-pools-with-fluid-compute)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A pooler cannot fix blocked transactions]
On 18 September 2026, an account deletion at Inngest held locks on its main PostgreSQL database long
enough that the queries needing those rows queued behind it. Each blocked query kept its place in
the pooler, and client connections to PgBouncer rose from 427 to 6,066, about 14 times the normal
level (6,066 ÷ 427 = 14.2). For 24 minutes, from 16:18 to 16:42 UTC, both PgBouncer instances
refused new connections. Restarting PgBouncer did not help: the blocking transaction was still
running, and the pools filled again with queries waiting on it
([Inngest, 2026](https://www.inngest.com/blog/2026-09-18-incident-report-for-september-18-2026)).
A pool limits how many requests wait inside the database. How long they wait is decided by
`lock_timeout` (the longest a statement waits for a lock), `statement_timeout`, and splitting large
deletes into small batches.
:::

:::danger[Client deadlines and the pooler's queue must agree]
On 10 October 2026, Inngest's poolers lost almost all their database connections twice. Some
lookups had short deadlines. When one expired while its query was still queued in PgBouncer, the
driver cancelled it by closing its client connection. PgBouncer later ran the abandoned query
anyway, then closed that server connection instead of returning it. Each pooler opens new server
connections one at a time, so they were destroyed faster than they were replaced
([Inngest, 2026](https://www.inngest.com/blog/2026-10-15-incident-report-for-october-9-and-10-2026)).
Know what your driver does when a deadline expires, keep client deadlines longer than the pooler's
normal queue time, and alert on server connections being opened and closed, not only on how many
are open.
:::

:::caution[A leaked connection is a slow outage]
A code path that acquires a connection and returns early on an error, without releasing it, removes
one connection from the pool each time it runs. Nothing fails until the last one is gone. Always
acquire in a `with` block, a `try`/`finally`, or a helper like `withConnection`. HikariCP's
`leakDetectionThreshold` logs where in the code any connection was taken that is held longer than
you expect. On any pool, a count of connections in use that never returns to zero at quiet times is
the sign.
:::

:::caution[What the last request set, the next one inherits]
This happens with pools inside the app too, not only with PgBouncer. Picture an app that serves many
customer companies, each called a **tenant**, from one database with one schema per tenant. It runs
`SET search_path = tenant_42` on a pooled connection and does not reset it. The next request, for a
different tenant, gets a connection pointed at tenant 42's data. Use `SET LOCAL` inside the
transaction, or a reset hook on the pool (psycopg's `reset` callback) that runs `RESET ALL` or
`DISCARD ALL`, which clear the session's settings, when the connection is released.
:::

:::caution[Idle connections die quietly]
Firewalls, load balancers and NAT gateways (devices that let many private machines share one public
address) keep a table of open TCP connections, and drop the ones idle past their timeout without
telling either end. The next query on such a connection hangs until TCP gives up. Set the pool's
maximum lifetime and idle timeout below the shortest idle timeout on the path, or turn on **TCP
keepalives**, small empty messages TCP sends on an idle connection so devices on the path see it is
still in use. After a failover, the same maximum lifetime is what moves old connections to the new
main database.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

A pool trades waiting in your app for work in the database. That is a good trade only while the pool
is the right size. Too small, and requests fail with timeouts while the database has cores to spare.
Too big, and the database does less work and every request is slower. No size is right for all
traffic, which is why the time spent waiting to acquire matters more than the setting.

A pool does not make slow queries fast, and it does not fix transactions that hold locks or wait on
other services; it only limits how many of them pile up. An external pooler in transaction mode
gives up session features, and adds a hop that costs throughput when there are few clients.

Some work gains nothing from a pool: a batch job that runs one long transaction, a migration, or a
nightly script with a single connection. And when the clients are many short-lived serverless
functions, a pool inside each one cannot help, because each has a pool of one; that is the case for
a pooler, or an HTTP data API, in front of the database.

## Interview corner

Connection pooling comes up whenever a design puts many app servers in front of one relational
database. A strong answer gives numbers and talks about the budget, not only "use a pool".

- **"Your service has 50 pods. How do you size the pool?"** Start from the database: about twice
  its cores for busy work, and its `max_connections`. Divide by the number of pods at maximum scale
  and the processes per pod. If each pod gets only one or two connections, put an external pooler in
  transaction mode in front.
- **"Requests time out waiting for a connection, but database CPU is at 20%. What is happening?"**
  Connections are held while not querying: a slow external call or a long computation inside a
  transaction, or a leak. Check for `idle in transaction` sessions and the pool's hold-time metric.
- **"Why not set the pool to 500 so nobody waits?"** More busy connections than cores means taking
  turns on the cores and waiting on each other's locks; throughput falls and p99 explodes. The
  waiting moves into the database, where it is expensive.
- **"What breaks with PgBouncer in transaction mode?"** Session state: `SET`, advisory locks,
  `LISTEN`, temporary tables and SQL `PREPARE`. Protocol-level prepared statements work from 1.21
  when tracking is on.

Give one number to show you have measured: "a new connection costs about 10 ms with TLS and SCRAM; a
small query on an open one costs 0.05 ms".

<summary>2. 8 pods, each running 4 Gunicorn workers with a pool of 10, against max_connections = 200. What happens in a rolling deploy that brings up all 8 new pods before stopping the old ones?</summary>

In normal running: 8 pods × 4 workers = 32 workers, and 32 × 10 = 320 possible connections. That is
already over the limit of 200 − 3 reserved = 197 if the pools fill. During the deploy, 16 pods run,
so it is 2 × 320 = 640. Every connection past 197 fails with "too many clients". Shrink the pools,
cap how many new pods a deploy starts at once, or put a pooler in front.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A request acquires a connection, opens a transaction, then calls a helper that acquires another connection from the same pool. Under load the service hangs. Why, and what fixes it?</summary>

Pool deadlock: when every connection is held by a request waiting for a second one, no connection
is ever released. Pass the held connection to the helper, give the helper its own pool, or at the
least set an acquire timeout so one request fails and frees its connection.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. After moving to PgBouncer in transaction mode, some queries fail with "canceling statement due to statement timeout", though nobody changed the timeout. What would you look for?</summary>

A client that runs `SET statement_timeout` for its whole session, outside a transaction. In
transaction mode that setting stays on the server connection and applies to whichever client gets
it next. Use `SET LOCAL` inside the transaction, set the timeout per role or per database, or set it
in the pooler's connection settings.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why did 512 clients through PgBouncer with 8 server connections complete more write transactions per second than 512 direct connections, while the median latency got worse?</summary>

Behind the pooler only 8 transactions were inside the database at once, so they rarely waited on each
other's row locks and the database did more useful work. The median is set by the queue: 512 clients
sharing about 3,300 transactions a second wait 512 clients ÷ 3,300 transactions per second = about
0.15 s, or 150 ms, each, wherever the queue is. The pooler made the waiting orderly and the slowest waits far shorter,
not shorter for everyone.

</details>

## Recap

- Opening a PostgreSQL connection costs 2–10 ms and six round trips with TLS and SCRAM; a small query
  on an open one costs 0.05 ms. Keep connections open.
- Past about twice the database's cores, more busy connections did less work: 6,598 transactions a
  second at 8 connections, 2,370 at 512, with p99 going from 3.6 ms to 1.7 s.
- A pool's capacity is pool size ÷ hold time, and hold time includes everything done between acquire
  and release. Keep slow calls out of transactions.
- Budget across the fleet: pods × processes × pool size, plus jobs and people, under
  `max_connections`, at maximum scale and during deploys.
- Always set an acquire timeout shorter than the request deadline; nested acquires from one pool can
  deadlock.
- Transaction-mode poolers share few connections among many clients, at the cost of an extra hop
  and of session state: `SET`, advisory locks, `LISTEN`, temporary tables.
