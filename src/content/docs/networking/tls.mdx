## The question

You are on café Wi-Fi, paying ₹100 from your bank's website. Every byte you send passes through
the café's router, and whoever runs that router can read it, change it, or answer in the bank's
place. You and the bank share no password for this connection. So how does your payment reach the
bank unread and unchanged, and how does your browser know the reply came from the bank and not
from the router?

The short answer: before your payment leaves, your browser and the bank's server run **TLS**
(Transport Layer Security), the protocol behind the padlock and `https://`. First they agree on a
fresh secret key, in a way that someone who reads every message still cannot work out. Then the
server proves it is the bank, by making a signature that only the bank's own secret key can make.
After that, everything you send is locked with the shared key and sealed, so that any change is
detected.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one payment. You are in a café, on its Wi-Fi. The router belongs to Mallory, who is
curious. You open `https://bank.example.in` and pay ₹100 to `ravi@okbank`. A message from your
laptop to the bank's server and its reply take 40 ms in total: that is one **round trip**.

1. **The connection opens.** Your browser opens a TCP connection to the bank, as on the
   [TCP vs UDP page](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/). Every packet in both directions passes through
   Mallory's router.
2. **Hello from the browser.** Your browser sends a first message, the **ClientHello**. It holds
   the site name it wants (`bank.example.in`), the kinds of encryption it can use, a fresh random
   number, and its half of a key exchange, called a **key share**. Mallory can read all of it.
3. **Hello from the bank.** The server answers with its own random number and its own key share.
   Each side now combines a private number it never sent with the other side's share, and both get
   the same secret key. Mallory saw both shares and still cannot compute the key; the building
   blocks below show why, with small numbers. Everything after this message is encrypted.
4. **The bank shows its certificate.** A **certificate** is a public document that says "this
   public key belongs to `bank.example.in`, valid until 17 November 2026", stamped by a
   **certificate authority** (CA), a company your device already trusts. A public key is one half
   of a matched pair of keys; the other half, the **private key**, never leaves the bank. Anyone
   can download the bank's certificate, Mallory included, so showing it proves nothing on its own.
5. **The bank signs this conversation.** The server takes a fingerprint of every message so far,
   including both key shares and your fresh random number, and signs it with the private key that
   belongs with the certificate. Only the bank has that key. Your browser checks the
   signature with the public key printed in the certificate.
6. **Your browser checks everything.** The certificate's name matches `bank.example.in`. Today is
   inside its validity dates. The stamp on it comes from a CA in your device's trusted list. The
   signature over this conversation is valid. If any check fails, the browser stops and shows a
   warning page instead of sending anything.
7. **The payment travels locked.** Your request, with `{"to": "ravi@okbank", "amount_inr": 100}`
   inside, is encrypted with the shared key and sealed with a 16-byte **tag**. Mallory sees a
   block of random-looking bytes, 17 bytes longer than your request (the 16-byte tag plus one byte
   Part 2 explains). If she changes even one bit, the tag no longer matches and the bank throws
   the block away.
8. **The cost.** Steps 2 to 6 take one round trip before the payment can leave: the browser's
   hello goes out, and the bank's hello, certificate and signature come back together. That is
   40 ms here.
   After that, encryption costs very little, because modern processors have instructions built
   for it.

That answers the café puzzle. Mallory has three moves, and each one fails. She can listen, but the
key never crossed the wire. She can change bytes, but the tag catches it. She can pretend to be the
bank and send the bank's certificate, but she cannot make the bank's signature over this
conversation, because she does not have the bank's private key.

TLS does not hide everything. Mallory still sees which address you connected to, usually the site
name from step 2, and how many bytes went each way and when. Part 2 shows how much that alone can
give away.

An everyday picture: a passport check. Anyone can photocopy your passport, so a photocopy proves
nothing; the officer compares the photo with the face in front of them. The certificate is the
passport, issued by a government (the CA) the officer trusts. The signature over this conversation
is the face: the part a copier cannot bring along. The picture stops working in one place: a face
can be imitated with enough effort, while nobody knows a way to make the signature without the
private key.

## The building blocks

Seven ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### Bits, bytes and hex

**What it is.** A **bit** is a single 0 or 1. A **byte** is 8 bits, so it can hold one of
2 × 2 × … × 2 (eight times) = 256 values, from 0 to 255.

**Why it exists.** Computers store keys, fingerprints and messages as bytes, and TLS describes
everything in bytes: 32-byte key shares, 16-byte tags, 5-byte headers. We need a way to write
bytes down.

**Example.** The letter `s` is stored as the byte 115, which in bits is `01110011`. Writing long
runs of 0s and 1s is unreadable, so programmers use **hexadecimal** (hex): base 16, with the digits
`0`–`9` and then `a`–`f` for 10 to 15. One hex digit stands for 4 bits, so a byte is always two hex
digits: 115 is `73` in hex (7 × 16 + 3).

**The technical version.** A key of 32 bytes is 256 bits, so it has 2<sup>256</sup> possible
values, a number with 78 digits. Nobody guesses a key like that; the attacks on this page go after
everything around the key instead. Sizes here use KB for 1,024 bytes, so 16 KB is 16,384 bytes.

### A key, and symmetric encryption

**What it is.** **Encryption** scrambles data using a secret number, the **key**, so that only
someone with the same key can unscramble it.

**Why it exists.** Data on the internet crosses machines you do not control: the café router, your
internet provider, the networks in between. Encryption makes whatever they copy useless to them.

**Example.** With a 256-bit key, your payment request turns into the same number of bytes that look
random. The bank, holding the same key, turns them back into the request. Mallory, without the key,
would have to try keys one by one, and there are 2<sup>256</sup> of them.

**The technical version.** When one key both locks and unlocks, the encryption is **symmetric**.
A specific method of encrypting is called a **cipher**. The standard one is **AES** (Advanced Encryption Standard), with 128- or 256-bit keys, and
almost every modern processor has AES instructions, so it is fast. **ChaCha20** is the common
alternative, fast on phones without those instructions. The catch is the starting point: both
sides must already hold the same key, and two strangers do not. Key exchange, further down, solves
that.

### An integrity tag

**What it is.** A **tag** is a short value, computed from the key and the data, that proves the
data was not changed on the way.

**Why it exists.** Encryption hides data; it does not stop someone changing it. The ciphers TLS uses
work by mixing the data with a random-looking stream of bytes made from the key. Flip a bit in the
encrypted bytes, and the same bit flips in the decrypted message. Without a tag, the bank would
decrypt the changed message and never know.

**Example.** The characters `1` and `9` differ in exactly one bit (they are bytes 49 and 57). If
Mallory knows where the amount sits in your request, she can flip that one bit in the encrypted
bytes, and `100` decrypts as `900`. With a tag, the bank recomputes the 16-byte tag over what
arrived, gets a different value from the one attached, and drops the block. Mallory cannot compute
the right tag without the key, and a random guess of 16 bytes (128 bits) is right once in
2<sup>128</sup> tries.

**The technical version.** The tag is also called a **MAC** (message authentication code). TLS 1.3
allows only ciphers that encrypt and tag in one operation, called **AEAD** (authenticated
encryption with associated data; the associated data is a header that is checked but left
readable). In practice those are AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305, both with a 16-byte tag.

### A hash: a fingerprint of data

**What it is.** A **hash function** turns any input, of any length, into a short fingerprint of a
fixed size.

**Why it exists.** A signature (next) works on a short value of fixed size, not on pages of
messages. And two sides often need to check that they saw exactly the same messages without
sending them all again. Comparing fingerprints does both.

**Example.** SHA-256, the hash TLS usually uses, gives 32 bytes, written as 64 hex digits. The
SHA-256 of `pay ₹100` starts `14695baf`; that of `pay ₹900` starts `044dcdf1`. One character
changed, and the whole fingerprint changed with no visible pattern.

**The technical version.** The same input always gives the same output, nobody can work back from
an output to its input, and nobody can construct two inputs with the same output. A hash has no key,
so anyone can compute one; that is what lets two sides compare, and also why a hash on its own is
not a tag.

### Public and private keys, and signatures

**What it is.** A **key pair** is two linked keys: a **private key** the owner keeps secret and a
**public key** it gives to anyone. A **signature** is a value that only the private key can make,
and that anyone holding the public key can check.

**Why it exists.** A symmetric key has to be shared before it is useful. A key pair lets the bank
prove something to a stranger who holds only its public key, without sharing any secret first.

**Example.** Toy numbers, with RSA, the oldest common signature scheme. The public key is n = 33 and
e = 3; the private key is d = 7. Write `x mod n` for the remainder after dividing x by n:
16,384 mod 33 = 16, because 16,384 = 33 × 496 + 16. To sign a message whose hash is 4, the bank
computes 4<sup>7</sup> mod 33 = 16,384 mod 33 = 16, and sends 16 with the message. You compute
16<sup>3</sup> mod 33 = 4,096 mod 33 = 4, hash the message you received yourself, and accept only
if both give 4. If Mallory changes the message so its hash becomes 5, the signature 16 still opens
to 4 and the check fails. The signature that opens to 5 is 14, and finding it needs d.

**The technical version.** Real signatures use RSA keys with 2,048-bit numbers, or elliptic-curve
keys (a different kind of arithmetic that gives the same strength with much smaller numbers), where
the private key cannot be worked out from the public one. Public-key operations are far slower than
AES, so TLS uses them only at the start of a connection. A public key can also *encrypt*, so that
only the private key decrypts; Part 2 shows why TLS 1.3 stopped using that.

### Key exchange: agreeing on a key in public

**What it is.** A **key exchange** lets two parties who share nothing agree on a secret key while a
listener hears every message.

**Why it exists.** Symmetric encryption needs both sides to hold the same key, and sending the key
across the café Wi-Fi would hand it to Mallory.

**Example.** The classic method is **Diffie-Hellman**. Both sides agree in public on two numbers,
p = 23 and g = 5. Each picks a private number, sends g to that power mod p, and raises what it
receives to its own private number:

<Figure caption="Each side keeps its private number, sends 5 to that power mod 23, and raises the other's share to its own private number. Both reach 2, while the wire carried only 23, 5, 8 and 19.">
  <KeyExchange />
</Figure>

You kept 6 and the bank kept 15; the wire carried 8 and 19; both sides reached 2, and 2 never
travelled. Mallory has 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get 2 she needs 6 or 15, which means answering "5 to
what power, mod 23, gives 8?". With numbers this small she can try every power, but at real sizes
nobody knows a shortcut.

**The technical version.** Both sides reach the same number because (g<sup>a</sup>)<sup>b</sup> =
(g<sup>b</sup>)<sup>a</sup>. The value each side sends is its key share. TLS 1.3 usually uses
**X25519**, an elliptic-curve version of the same idea with 32-byte shares. Both private numbers
are fresh for every connection and deleted afterwards, which matters in Part 2.

### A certificate and a certificate authority

**What it is.** A **certificate** is a small public document that says "this public key belongs to
this name", signed by a **certificate authority** (CA).

**Why it exists.** A signature proves "whoever holds this private key signed this". Your browser
still needs to know that the public key is the bank's and not Mallory's. Someone your browser
already trusts has to vouch for that.

**Example.** The bank's certificate says: name `bank.example.in`; the bank's public key; valid from 1 October to 17 November 2026, which is 47 days; issued by a CA; and the CA's signature
over all of that. Mallory can copy the certificate and send it, but the public key in it is the
bank's, so only the bank can sign with the matching private key. If she edits the name or puts her
own public key in, the CA's signature no longer matches.

**The technical version.** Your device carries a list of CAs it trusts, its **trust store**, shipped
with the operating system or the browser. The CAs on that list are called **root** CAs: Mozilla's
list held 121 of them as of September 2026 ([curl's extract](https://curl.se/docs/caextract.html)).
The CA that signed the bank's certificate is usually an **intermediate** CA, whose own certificate
is signed by a root; Part 2 walks that chain. Certificates use a format called X.509.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- TLS gives a connection three things: nobody else can read it, nobody can change it unnoticed, and
  you know who is on the other end.
- The two sides agree on a fresh secret key with a key exchange: each sends a key share in public,
  and the key itself never travels.
- A certificate links the bank's public key to its name, signed by a CA your device trusts. Anyone
  can send the certificate; only the bank can sign the conversation with the matching private key,
  and that signature is the real proof.
- After the handshake, data is encrypted and tagged, so reading or changing it without the key
  fails.
- TLS does not hide who you talk to, when, or how much: addresses, usually the site name, sizes and
  timing stay visible.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 left the hard questions out. The bank's certificate is public, so Mallory can send it byte
for byte: which message, exactly, stops her from being the bank, and what would she need to beat
it? If she cannot be the bank, how can she still tell which page of a clinic's website you read,
or copy your encrypted payment and have the bank run it twice, without breaking any encryption? And
which keys, stolen next year, open traffic she recorded today?

Start with what the handshake actually promises. Stated precisely, after a successful handshake
the client knows this:

> The bytes I receive were sent by someone holding the private key of a certificate that names the
> host I asked for and chains to a root in my trust store, and nobody has changed, reordered,
> dropped or replayed them *within this connection*.

Every qualifier in that sentence is a place where real systems break, and each has a section below:

- **"Someone holding the private key"**: the certificate is public; the key is the secret. A thief
  with the key is the bank until the certificate expires, because cancelling a certificate early
  rarely works in practice.
- **"The host I asked for"**: only if the client checks the name. `verify=False`, the setting that
  turns the checks off, drops this, and with it everything else.
- **"A root in my trust store"**: about 120 roots, any of which may sign for any name, plus any
  root your employer installed on your laptop.
- **"Within this connection"**: a copy of your first message, sent again on a new connection, is
  outside the promise. The section on 0-RTT, sending a request before the handshake has finished,
  shows when that runs a payment twice.
- **What is not in the sentence at all**: who you talk to, when and how much. Sizes and timing are
  visible, and so is the site name unless a newer extension hides it.

Once the handshake has produced a key, data travels in **records**: chunks of up to 16 KB, each
encrypted and tagged on its own, with Part 1's AEAD ciphers.

Each record also needs a **nonce**, a number used only once with a given key. It exists because the
stream of bytes the cipher mixes into the data depends only on the key and the nonce: reuse both,
and two records are mixed with the same stream, so an observer can combine them and cancel the
stream out. With a fresh nonce per record, even two identical requests encrypt to different bytes.

TLS takes the nonce from a record counter that both sides keep and never send: record 1, record 2,
and so on. That gives ordering for free. Swap records 1 and 2 on the wire, and the receiver opens
the first to arrive with nonce 1, but it was sealed with nonce 2: the tag fails and the connection
is torn down. Dropped and repeated records fail the same way. This record machinery is the part of
TLS that rarely goes wrong. The handshake that creates its keys is where the interesting failures are.

## The obvious fix, and why it breaks

Part 1's key exchange already gives both sides a key that no listener can compute. Why not stop
there: exchange shares, then encrypt and tag everything? Because a key exchange defeats anyone who
only listens, and does nothing against someone who answers.

A **man in the middle** is an attacker who sits on the path and talks to each side while pretending
to be the other. Mallory's router is exactly that position. Here is the attack:

channel Mallory cannot touch, such as a phone call, would expose her; SSH, the tool for logging in
to remote servers, asks you to do exactly that the first time you connect. TLS needs the same check
to happen automatically, for billions of clients and servers that have never met.

## How it works, piece by piece

### The fix: the server signs the conversation

Part 1's step 5 is the fix, and now we can see exactly why it works. The **transcript** is every
handshake message so far, in order, with both random values and **both key shares** inside it. After
sending its certificate, the server hashes the transcript and signs that hash with the private key
that matches the certificate. The message carrying this signature is called **CertificateVerify**.

Both sides then send a message called **Finished**: a tag over the same transcript, computed with a
key made from the shared secret (the next section shows how). It shows each side that the other saw the identical handshake,
byte for byte.

<Figure caption="The server's signature covers a hash of the whole handshake so far, including the client's key share. Swap any byte and the hash changes; only the holder of the private key can produce a new signature.">
  <Transcript />
</Figure>

Now replay Mallory's attack. If she forwards your key share to the bank unchanged, the bank's
signature is valid, but the key is shared between you and the bank, and she cannot read anything.
If she swaps in her own share, the bank signs a transcript containing *her* share; you check the
signature against a transcript containing *yours*, and the hashes differ. To fix that, she would
have to sign your transcript herself, with the bank's private key. Sending the bank's certificate
costs her nothing. Producing that signature is the one thing she cannot do.

That is the whole of server authentication in TLS 1.3: **a certificate that the client's trust store
vouches for, plus a fresh signature from its key over this particular handshake.** Everything else
on this page is about what that sentence does not cover.

### What the handshake proves, message by message

Five terms appear in the handshake below.

A **group** is a named, standard choice of key exchange settings: the real-size version of p = 23
and g = 5. X25519 is one group. The client does not know in advance which groups the server
supports, so it guesses and sends shares for one or two.

**Key derivation** turns one shared secret into several keys. It exists so that each direction and
each stage of the connection gets its own key: a record that Mallory sends back to its own sender
then fails to decrypt. TLS feeds the shared secret and the transcript hash into **HKDF**, a standard
recipe built from a hash such as SHA-256, and gets, for example, one key for the client's messages
and a different one for the server's. Change any input and every key changes.

**SNI** (server name indication) is the site name in the ClientHello. It exists because one server
address often hosts many sites, and the server must know which certificate to send before any keys
exist, so it travels in the clear.

**ALPN** (application-layer protocol negotiation) is the list of protocols the client can speak
inside TLS, such as `h2` for HTTP/2 or `http/1.1`. The server picks one, so no extra round trip is
spent agreeing on it later.

A **flight** is the group of messages one side sends before it waits for an answer. The server's
reply below is one flight of five messages, which is why the whole handshake fits in one round trip.

Each way of attacking the handshake meets one specific defence. One row needs a word first: a
**downgrade** is an attacker editing the ClientHello so both sides settle on an older, weaker
version or cipher than they both support.

| Attack | What stops it |
|---|---|
| Swap a key share | CertificateVerify covers both shares; the signature fails |
| Replay a recorded handshake from yesterday | The client's random value is new, so yesterday's signature covers a different transcript |
| Downgrade: edit the ClientHello to remove strong ciphers or force TLS 1.2 | Finished covers the ClientHello; a TLS 1.3 server pushed to 1.2 also writes a fixed marker into the last 8 bytes of its random value, which TLS 1.3 clients check |
| Present a certificate for the name from a CA you made | Chain building fails: your root is not in the client's store |
| Present a certificate from a CA the client *does* trust | Nothing in TLS. Certificate Transparency makes it visible afterwards |

The last row is the honest limit. The client authenticates "a key some trusted CA vouched for", not
"the bank". In 2011 attackers who broke into the Dutch CA DigiNotar issued a `*.google.com`
certificate (valid for every name under `google.com`) that intercepted Gmail for around 300,000 IP addresses, almost all in Iran
([Fox-IT interim report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1044777/000119312511241796/dex992.htm)).

**Certificate Transparency** (CT) is the answer that followed. It is a set of public logs, lists that
anyone can read and that can be added to but never edited, into which CAs must put every
certificate they issue ([RFC 6962](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962)). It exists because a
wrongly issued certificate cannot always be prevented, only exposed. Chrome refuses a certificate without proof that it
was logged by at least two log operators
([Chrome CT policy](https://googlechrome.github.io/CertificateTransparency/ct_policy.html)). A
wrongly issued certificate still works for a while, but it can no longer stay secret: search
[crt.sh](https://crt.sh/) for your own domains.

A **CAA** record in your DNS zone narrows the field before issuance: it names the CAs allowed to
issue for your domain, and every public CA must check it
([RFC 8659](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8659)). It stops an honest CA from being tricked into
issuing for you; it cannot stop a compromised one that ignores it.

A corporate inspection proxy, a machine that relays and reads all traffic between company laptops
and the internet, uses the same limit on purpose. It issues certificates on the fly from a company
root installed on managed laptops, and from the browser's point of view every check passes. TLS
worked perfectly; the endpoint you authenticated was the proxy.

Notice also what the server learned about the client: nothing. In ordinary TLS the client is
anonymous until the application asks for a password or token, and that token can be replayed from
anywhere. Mutual TLS, later on this page, fixes that between services.

### Forward secrecy, and the keys that quietly undo it

**Forward secrecy** means that stealing a server's long-term private key next year does not decrypt
traffic recorded today. It matters because recording is cheap: anyone on the path can store
encrypted traffic for years and wait for a key to leak. Say Mallory saves the encrypted bytes of
your ₹100 payment on 7 November 2026, and the bank's private key leaks in 2027. With forward
secrecy, her recording stays sealed.

It comes from the word **ephemeral**, which means "used once and thrown away": both sides pick fresh
Diffie-Hellman private numbers for each handshake and delete them afterwards. In the
[toy exchange](#key-exchange-agreeing-on-a-key-in-public), once both sides forget 6 and 15, the only
way from a recording of 8 and 19 back to 2 is the hard reverse step, for the bank as much as for
anyone. The certificate's key only *signs*; it never protects the **session secret**, the shared
secret this connection's keys are made from.

TLS 1.2's older **RSA key exchange** did the opposite. The client picked the session secret itself,
encrypted it to the public key in the server's certificate, and sent it. So one stolen certificate
key decrypts every recording ever made with it. TLS 1.3 removed this mode.

The part teams miss is **session resumption**: a returning client skipping most of the handshake by
reusing a secret from its last connection. It exists because a full handshake costs a key exchange,
a signature and a chain check, and a client that returns a minute later would rather not pay again.
The server could keep those secrets in a table, but in a **cluster**, several servers behind one
address with a load balancer spreading connections over them, the next connection may land on any
server.

So the server hands the secret to the client, locked. It encrypts the secret with a symmetric key
of its own, the **session ticket encryption key** (STEK), and sends the result as a **session
ticket**. The client cannot read the ticket; it only stores it. On Monday the client receives
ticket T. On Tuesday it puts T in its ClientHello, server 7 of 10 decrypts T with the same STEK,
recovers the secret, and both sides continue from it without the certificate or signature. Every
server in the cluster must therefore hold the same STEK.

- **In TLS 1.2**, the ticket contains the connection's **master secret**, the one value from which
  every key of that connection was derived. Anyone who records traffic and later steals the STEK
  can decrypt the ticket and then the whole connection, including the original full handshake.
  Many servers created one STEK at startup and kept it for months, so in practice forward secrecy
  lasted only as long as the server program ran without a restart ([Langley, 2013](https://imperialviolet.org/2013/06/27/botchingpfs.html)).
- **In TLS 1.3**, the ticket holds a resumption secret derived one way from the old session, so it
  cannot be turned back into the old keys. Resumption normally also mixes in a fresh
  Diffie-Hellman exchange (the mode called `psk_dhe_ke`, "pre-shared key with Diffie-Hellman",
  which browsers use). A stolen STEK then decrypts neither the old connection nor the resumed one.
  It does decrypt any **0-RTT early data**, requests sent before the handshake completes (a later
  section), because those are encrypted with the ticket's secret alone.

So the STEK is a long-term key in disguise. **Rotate** it, which means replacing it with a new one
on a schedule, every few hours to a day. Distribute it to the cluster as a secret, and keep old ones
only as long as tickets live (at most 7 days in TLS 1.3).

**Harvest now, decrypt later** is the same problem at a larger scale. A **quantum computer** is a
machine that uses quantum physics to run certain calculations no ordinary computer could finish. A
large enough one does not exist yet, but it could work back from an X25519 share to its private
number, so traffic recorded today could be opened then.

The fix is a hybrid key exchange, **X25519MLKEM768**, which runs X25519 and **ML-KEM** side by side.
ML-KEM agrees on a secret using a different kind of maths problem (lattice problems) that no known
quantum method solves. Both secrets go into key derivation, so an attacker must break both, and the
session is safe if either holds ([RFC 10024](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc10024/), August 2026).

The cost is size. The client's share grows from 32 to 1,216 bytes (1,184 for ML-KEM plus 32 for
X25519), so the ClientHello no longer fits in one network packet of about 1,500 bytes.
**Middleboxes**, devices on the path such as firewalls that inspect traffic, sometimes assumed it
would, and broke when browsers turned it on ([tldr.fail](https://tldr.fail/)). As of February 2026,
over 60% of clients reaching Cloudflare offered it, but only about 10% of **origin servers** (the
customers' own servers that Cloudflare connects to behind its network) supported it
([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-origin-pq-key-transparency-aspa/)).

### What an observer still sees

Encryption hides content, not shape. Here is what someone on the path learns from one visit, with
no keys at all. The DNS lookup is the question "what is the address of this name?" from the
[DNS page](/networking/dns/), and the handshake offers are the ClientHello's lists of ciphers,
groups and ALPN protocols:

<Figure caption="An on-path observer learns the addresses, usually the DNS lookup and the site name, the handshake offers, and the size and timing of every record. TLS 1.3 hides the certificate, the path, headers and body.">
  <Visible />
</Figure>

The SNI travels in the clear, as the handshake section showed. **Encrypted Client Hello**
(ECH, [RFC 9849](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9849.html), March 2026) uses public-key
encryption to hide it. The hosting provider publishes an ECH public key in its DNS records. The
client encrypts the real ClientHello, with the real name, to that key and wraps it inside an outer
ClientHello whose SNI names only the provider: the observer reads `ech.provider.example`, and only
the provider's private key opens the inner `clinic.example.in`.

ECH hides the site only when many sites share that provider's addresses. A site on its own IP
address gains nothing: the address already names it.

Sizes leak more than names, so the next figure looks at one record byte by byte. We look at bytes
here because the one thing encryption cannot hide is how many of them there are. Each record starts
with a 5-byte **header** in the clear: one byte for the **content type** (a code saying whether the
record carries application data, a handshake message or an error alert), two bytes for a version
number kept only for old middleboxes, and two bytes for the length of what follows. In the caption,
**plaintext** means your data before encryption:

<Figure caption="Every record starts with a 5-byte header in the clear, including its length. In TLS 1.3 with AES-GCM the length is the plaintext plus 17 bytes: one for the hidden content type and 16 for the tag.">
  <Record />
</Figure>

TLS 1.3 writes "application data" in every visible header and hides the real content type as one
extra byte inside the encryption. That byte and the 16-byte tag are the 17 bytes from step 7 of
Part 1.

The `observer` example puts a relay with no keys between a client and a server, the position of
every Wi-Fi router, and reads only the parts that are not encrypted:

Read the numbers. The response is exactly the page plus 17 bytes in TLS 1.3 and plus 24 in TLS 1.2
(the 16-byte tag, plus 8 bytes of nonce that TLS 1.2 sends with every record instead of deriving

is two characters longer than `/dental`, and its record is two bytes longer. TLS 1.2 also showed the

Real pages are many requests with sizes that vary a little per visit, and that turns out not to
help much. In 2014, researchers identified individual pages within ten large HTTPS sites,
including health and finance sites, with about 89% accuracy from traffic shape alone (the sizes and timing of
requests and responses); their best
defence cut it to 27% for 9% more traffic
([Miller et al., PETS 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.0297)). Here is the mechanism on a toy site
with twelve pages. The observer measured each page's typical size in advance and, for every visit,
names the page whose size is closest to what it saw.

The sim also lets you turn on the defence. **Padding** means adding filler bytes so that different
responses come out the same size. With 16 KB **buckets**, every response is padded up to the next
multiple of 16 KB: a 1,200-byte page and a 5,400-byte page both travel as 16,384 bytes, and the
observer cannot tell them apart.

<SizeLeakSim />

With the defaults the observer names the page in about 82% of visits, against 8% (1 in 12) for a blind
guess. 16 KB buckets drop it to 27%, at the cost of 23% more bytes;
32 KB buckets get to 21% for 52% more. Padding works by making pages share a size, so it only helps
when the buckets are wider than the gaps between real page sizes, and you pay for it in bandwidth
on every response. TLS 1.3 records can carry padding, but no common server adds it for you: it is
an application decision.

:::caution[Compression turns sizes into secrets]
**Compression** makes a response shorter by replacing text that repeats with a short reference to
its earlier copy. A **CSRF token** is a random secret a page puts in its forms, so the server can
tell its own forms from forged ones.

Size leaks get worse when a response compresses such a secret together with text the attacker
controls. If the attacker's guess matches part of a CSRF token on the page, the response compresses better
and comes out a few bytes shorter, so the token can be recovered one character at a time by
watching lengths. That is the BREACH attack (2013) on HTTP compression; TLS 1.3 removed TLS-level
compression for the same reason (CRIME, 2012). The usual defence is to mask such tokens with a fresh
random value on every response, so there is no stable string for guesses to match
([breachattack.com](https://breachattack.com/)).
:::

### 0-RTT: a request that can run twice

A full handshake costs one round trip, often written **RTT** (round-trip time), before the first
request can leave. **0-RTT** makes that zero on a resumed connection. The client already holds a
secret from its session ticket, so it sends its first request inside its very first flight, as
**early data**, encrypted with a key derived from that secret. For a user whose round trip to the server is 200 ms, the answer
arrives 200 ms sooner.

The price follows from the attack table above. A normal handshake is protected against **replay**,
an attacker sending a recorded copy again, because the *server's* fresh random value goes into the
keys, so yesterday's recording does not fit today's keys. Early data is sent before the server has
said anything, so its keys depend only on the client's flight. Anyone can copy that flight and send
it again on a new connection, and it decrypts correctly every time.

The specification says plainly that TLS gives early data no protection against replay *between
connections*, and lists what servers can do
([RFC 8446, section 8](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8446#section-8)). A server can
accept each ticket only once, or keep a **replay cache**: a memory of every ClientHello it has seen
recently, so a second copy is recognised and refused. Either way it adds a **freshness check** that
rejects a ClientHello whose **ticket age** (how long ago, by the client's clock, the ticket was
issued) is too far from what the server expects, on the order of ten
seconds, so the memory only has to cover those few seconds.

Both kinds of memory have to live *somewhere*, and in a cluster that is the whole problem. Watch an
attacker copy every `POST /transfer` four times and send the copies through the same load balancer.
The sim compares three defences: a replay cache inside each server, one cache shared by all
servers, and refusing `POST` in early data:

<ReplaySim />

With a replay cache inside each server, a copy is caught only if it reaches the server that saw the
original. With 8 servers and 4 copies per request, 570 of 800 copies ran in a five-minute run with
the default seed: about 71%. Counting gives nearly the same: the original and its 4 copies are five
random picks among 8 servers. Each server is missed by one pick with chance 7/8 and by all five
with chance (7/8)<sup>5</sup> ≈ 0.51, so the picks reach on average 8 × (1 − 0.51) ≈ 3.9 different
servers. One of those ran the original, so about 2.9 of the 4 copies run: 72%.

Per-server memory protects one server, and a cluster of one. A shared cache catches every copy, but
now every 0-RTT handshake waits on a lookup in a shared store, and when that store is unreachable
the only safe choice is to refuse early data. The cheapest complete answer is the third one: do not
accept anything in early data that is unsafe to run twice.

That is what production systems do. As of October 2026, Cloudflare's 0-RTT is off by default,
accepts only `GET`, `HEAD` and `OPTIONS` in early data, and adds an `Early-Data: 1` header so the
origin knows
([Cloudflare docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/speed/optimization/protocol/0-rtt-connection-resumption/)).
An origin that sees that header on a request it cannot replay answers `425 Too Early`, and the
client resends after the full handshake ([RFC 8470](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8470)).
Remember also that early data has no forward secrecy against a stolen STEK, from the previous
section.

:::danger[A GET is safe to replay only if it changes nothing]
"Accept only GET in early data" protects you exactly as well as your GET handlers are free of side
effects. `GET /unsubscribe?id=…`, `GET /api/redeem?code=…` and tracking pixels that count are all
replayable writes. Before turning 0-RTT on, list the endpoints that do something on GET, or give
every write an [idempotency key](/messaging/idempotency-keys/), a unique id the client sends with
the request, so the server can recognise a second copy and do nothing.
:::

### Chain building, and why "works in my browser" proves nothing

Part 1's certificate, opened up. It is a short structured record in the X.509 format, and each field
below is one of the checks a client makes, so the list is worth reading slowly:

- **Subject Alternative Name** (SAN): the names it is valid for, such as `bank.example.in` and
  `*.bank.example.in`, a **wildcard** that covers any one name under `bank.example.in`, such as
  `www.bank.example.in`.
- **Public key**: the key whose private half must sign CertificateVerify.
- **Issuer**: the CA that signed it.
- **Validity**: not before and not after, for example 1 October to 17 November 2026.
- **Serial number**: unique per CA, used to revoke it.
- **Extensions** that limit the key: `CA:TRUE` or `CA:FALSE` (may it sign other certificates?) and
  the allowed use, such as server authentication.
- **The issuer's signature** over a hash of all the fields above.

The last field makes the certificate a signed statement: the issuer vouches that this key belongs
to these names until that date. Add one name and the hash changes, so the signature fails. Checking
that signature needs the issuer's public key, which sits in the issuer's own certificate, which is
signed by *its* issuer. A root signs its own certificate, so its signature proves nothing; it is
trusted only because it is already in the trust store.

Why a chain and not the root signing everything? A root's private key is the most valuable key a CA
owns, so CAs keep it offline and use it rarely, to sign intermediate CAs. The intermediates sign
everyday certificates, and a leaked intermediate key can be replaced without touching every
device's trust store.

A server sends its own certificate (the **leaf**) and the **intermediate** CA certificates that
signed it. The client must build a path from the leaf to a **root** already in its trust store, and
then check every link.

<Figure caption="The server sends the leaf and the intermediate. The root is already on the device. The client checks each signature upwards and trusts the leaf only if the path ends at a root it holds.">
  <Chain />
</Figure>

For every certificate on the path the client checks the signature against the issuer's public key,
the validity dates against its own clock, that issuers are marked as CAs and the leaf is allowed
for server authentication, and finally that the leaf's Subject Alternative Names include the host
it asked for. The trust store at the end of the path is the list of about 120 roots from Part 1;
Mozilla's copy is also the one curl and most Linux distributions use.

The classic failure is a server that sends only its leaf. Clients then disagree, because each fills
the gap differently:

| Client | Missing intermediate |
|---|---|
| Chrome | Downloads it from the web address in the leaf's Authority Information Access (AIA) field, which says where the issuer's certificate can be fetched |
| Firefox | Already has it: it preloads every intermediate disclosed to the CA database browsers share ([Mozilla, 2020](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2020/11/13/preloading-intermediate-ca-certificates-into-firefox/)) |
| Any browser that visited another site with the same intermediate | Often has it cached |
| Python, Node, Go, Java, curl | Fail: "unable to get local issuer certificate" |

So the site opens in the browser, the developer's API call fails, and the bug is reported against
the wrong side. The `tls_checks` example in [Build it yourself](#build-it-yourself) reproduces it,
and shows that the error for a missing intermediate is *identical* to the error for an impostor
whose CA nobody trusts. That is why turning verification off to "fix" it is so dangerous: it fixes
the impostor case too.

Paths can also have more than one answer. CAs **cross-sign**: an intermediate may be signed by both
a new root and an old one, so old devices that lack the new root still find a path. When the old
root expires, a client that picks the expired path fails even though a valid one exists. That is
what happened to older versions of OpenSSL, the TLS library behind many Linux programs, when Let's Encrypt's old root expired in September 2021
([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/dst-root-ca-x3-expiration-september-2021/)). Check
chains with the clients you actually ship, not with a browser.

### Revocation, which mostly does not work

If a private key leaks, the CA can **revoke** the certificate: declare it cancelled before its
expiry date. Say the bank finds out on 3 October that its key leaked, and its certificate is valid
until 17 November. For those six weeks, every client must learn that the certificate is no longer
good. Both ways of asking have failed in practice:

- A **CRL** (certificate revocation list) is a signed list of revoked serial numbers. Lists get large,
  and clients download them rarely.
- **OCSP** (Online Certificate Status Protocol) asks the CA about one certificate at connection
  time. It adds a network round trip, tells the CA every site you visit, and fails often enough
  that browsers proceed when there is no answer. Carrying on as if the answer were "fine" is called
  **soft-fail**, and it is the fatal part:

**OCSP stapling** lets the server fetch a signed OCSP answer itself and attach ("staple") it to the
handshake, so the client learns the status without asking the CA: no extra round trip, and the CA
does not learn who visits. **Must-Staple** is a mark in a certificate that says it is unusable without a stapled answer.
Few sites used either. Let's Encrypt, the largest CA, stopped accepting Must-Staple requests in May
2025 and shut its OCSP service down on 6 August 2025, keeping only CRLs
([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2024/12/05/ending-ocsp/)).

Browsers moved the work to themselves. Chrome ships **CRLSets**, a curated list of revocations
pushed with browser updates, aimed at emergencies rather than every revoked certificate. Firefox
ships **CRLite**, a compressed encoding of every revocation of every certificate in the
Transparency logs, updated twice a day and checked locally; it is on for desktop users since
Firefox 137, and Firefox 142 stopped making OCSP queries for ordinary **domain-validated** certificates (the common
kind, for which the CA checked only that the requester controls the name)
([Mozilla, August 2025](https://hacks.mozilla.org/2025/08/crlite-fast-private-and-comprehensive-certificate-revocation-checking-in-firefox/)).
Your back-end code is in a worse position: Python's `ssl`, Node's `tls` and most HTTP clients check
no revocation at all by default.

So for most clients, the only revocation that always works is **expiry**. A stolen key is usable for
the rest of the certificate's life. With 398-day certificates that was up to 13 months; with 47-day
certificates it is under 7 weeks. That is the main reason lifetimes are being cut.

### Lifetimes falling to 47 days, and what breaks

In April 2025 the CA/Browser Forum, where CAs and browsers set the rules for public certificates,
passed a schedule that cuts the maximum lifetime in steps
([ballot SC-081v3](https://cabforum.org/2025/04/11/ballot-sc081v3-introduce-schedule-of-reducing-validity-and-data-reuse-periods/)):

<Figure caption="The maximum lifetime of a public certificate falls from 398 days to 47 by March 2029. Let's Encrypt issues 90 days by default and plans 45 by 2028.">
  <Lifetimes />
</Figure>

Before issuing, a CA checks that you control the name; that check is called **domain
validation**. The same ballot shortens how long a CA may reuse a past check to 10 days by 2029.

Automated renewal runs on **ACME** ([RFC 8555](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8555)), the
protocol that certbot (a popular renewal tool) and similar clients speak to a CA. The CA hands out
a random token, and the client proves control of the name by serving it at an HTTP path, in a DNS
record or in a TLS response; that proof is called a **challenge**. With 10-day reuse, nearly every
renewal must pass a fresh challenge, so that path must work *every* time, not once a year.

As of October 2026, Let's Encrypt issues 90-day
certificates by default, moves to 64 days on 10 February 2027 and 45 days in February 2028, and
recommends renewing at about two-thirds of the lifetime, ideally when the CA's **ARI** (ACME Renewal
Information) endpoint, an address the client can ask "when should I renew?", says so ([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2026/10/07/64-day-certs)).
It also stopped sending expiry reminder emails in June 2025.

Renewing at two-thirds means a third of the lifetime is left when renewal starts: for a 47-day
certificate, renewal starts after about 31 days, with about 16 days left. That third is not spare
time for the automation. It is **the time humans have to notice and fix broken automation**.
Automation breaks for dull reasons: a firewall change blocks the challenge, a DNS API token
expires, a server is rebuilt without the renewal job, the proxy is never reloaded. Here are six
certificates over two years, with those breakages:

<RenewalSim />

With the defaults, 47-day certificates, about one breakage per certificate per year, an alert at 14
days left and three days to fix, the run issues 139 certificates, the automation breaks 9 times,
and no certificate expires. Each break is a short amber band: renewal overdue, alert, fix, renewed
with days to spare. Set the alert to 0 and the same 9 breaks cause 27 days with an expired
certificate, because nothing tells a human until users do. Drag the lifetime to 6 days: the window
is 2 days, a 3-day fix arrives after expiry, and the run has 12 days of outage with the alert on;
it takes a 1-day fix to get back to zero. Short lifetimes shrink the damage of a stolen key, and
the price is that humans have to answer faster.

What breaks first when lifetimes fall is not ACME. It is every place a certificate was **copied by
hand**: uploaded to a CDN (a content delivery network, servers around the world that serve your site
close to users) or to a vendor's hardware box, pasted into a partner's allow-list, **pinned** in a
mobile app (built into the app so it accepts only that one certificate), or imported into a Java
keystore, the file Java programs read certificates from. At 398 days that was one chore a year per
place; at 47 days it is about eight (365 ÷ 47 ≈ 7.8). Find those places now, and make each one either automated or fetch its certificate
from somewhere that is.

:::caution[Monitor what is served, not what is stored]
On 22 February 2013, Windows Azure Storage served expired HTTPS certificates in every region for
about 11 and a half hours. The renewed certificates were already in the secret store, so expiry
alerts on the store had gone quiet; the release that would deploy them had been delayed
([InfoQ summary of Microsoft's post-mortem](https://www.infoq.com/news/2013/03/feb22_azure_outage)).
Check certificates by connecting to every endpoint as a client would, from outside your network,
and alert when one is past two-thirds of its lifetime.
:::

### Mutual TLS in a service mesh

In **mutual TLS** (mTLS) the client proves who it is too, with its own certificate and signature.
It exists for traffic between your own services: there the server needs to know *which* service is
calling, and a password or token in a header can be copied and replayed from anywhere, while a
signature over the transcript cannot.

Say the `orders` service calls the `payments` service to charge ₹100. In ordinary TLS, `payments`
proves itself and learns nothing about the caller. In mTLS, `payments` also asks for a
certificate, `orders` sends one naming itself, signs the transcript with its private key, and
`payments` now knows the charge came from `orders` and not from some other program on the network.

Technically, the server adds a CertificateRequest to its flight, and the client answers with its
own Certificate and CertificateVerify. Same mechanism, both directions. Programs that call each
other this way are often called **workloads**: a running service, or one copy of it.

Running mTLS by hand means a certificate for every workload, renewed constantly. A **service mesh**,
such as Istio or Linkerd, does that for you. It puts a small proxy, a **sidecar**, next to each
workload; the sidecars make the mTLS connections and renew the certificates, and the workload talks
plain HTTP to its own sidecar on the same machine.

The identity in each certificate is written as a URI, by the **SPIFFE** convention (a standard
naming scheme for workloads): `spiffe://cluster.local/ns/shop/sa/orders` means "the `orders`
service account (the identity a service runs as) in the `shop` namespace of this cluster", where a
namespace is a group of services in Kubernetes, the system that runs them. As of October 2026, Istio issues workload certificates
for 24 hours by default, from a key generated inside the **pod** (the unit Kubernetes runs a
workload in) ([Istio security](https://istio.io/latest/docs/concepts/security/)).

Four ways a mesh's mTLS is weaker than it looks:

1. **Authenticated is not authorized.** A valid certificate says who is calling. "Any workload with
   a certificate from our CA may call anything" is a policy, and usually the wrong one. The `mtls`
   example in Build it yourself refuses a perfectly authenticated caller.
2. **Permissive mode accepts plain text.** Istio's `PERMISSIVE` mode, the default, accepts both mTLS
   and unencrypted traffic so services can migrate one at a time. Until a namespace is `STRICT`,
   an attacker inside the network can skip mTLS entirely by speaking plain HTTP.
3. **The application trusts a header.** The sidecar ends mTLS and talks plain HTTP to the app on
   localhost (the same machine), passing the caller's identity in a header such as
   `X-Forwarded-Client-Cert`. If the app's port is reachable around the sidecar, anyone can send
   that header. Envoy, the proxy Istio uses as its sidecar, strips a client-supplied one by default
   ([Envoy docs](https://envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/configuration/http/http_conn_man/headers));
   make sure the app listens only on localhost.
4. **The mesh CA becomes a hard dependency.** With 24-hour certificates, a CA outage longer than the
   remaining lifetime is an outage of every service that cannot renew, at once. Monitor the CA like
   the database, not like a tool.

## Choosing where TLS ends

A request may cross a CDN, a load balancer, an **API gateway** (a proxy that checks and routes API
calls) and a sidecar before reaching your code, and each can decrypt it. Decrypting at a proxy is
**TLS termination**, and where it happens decides who can read your traffic. There are four common
choices:

- **Edge only** ends TLS at the first box, the **edge**, and sends plain HTTP inside.
- **Re-encrypt** ends it at the edge and opens a new TLS connection to the service.
- **Passthrough** forwards the encrypted bytes untouched, so TLS ends at the service itself.
- **Mesh mTLS** ends it at the edge and uses the sidecars' mutual TLS from there on.

<Figure caption="TLS can end at the edge, be re-encrypted to the service, pass through untouched, or be replaced by mutual TLS between services. Only the first leaves plain text inside your network.">

</Figure>

| | Edge only | Re-encrypt | Passthrough | Mesh mTLS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plain text inside the network | yes | only inside the proxy | no | no |
| Proxy can route on path and headers | yes | yes | no, only on SNI | yes |
| Private keys live on | the edge | edge and services | services only | edge, plus a short-lived key per workload |
| Callers identified by certificate | no | no | no | yes |
| Typical use | legacy | most web apps behind a managed balancer | databases, end-to-end rules | many services, zero-trust networks (no machine trusted for where it sits) |

"Re-encrypt" only helps if the proxy *verifies* the service's certificate; otherwise it is
`verify=False` on the back half. CDNs make this choice explicit. Cloudflare's *Flexible* mode is
HTTPS to the visitor and plain HTTP to your origin; *Full* encrypts to the origin without checking
its certificate; only *Full (strict)* verifies it
([Cloudflare SSL modes](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/origin-configuration/ssl-modes/)). A
Flexible-mode site whose origin redirects HTTP to HTTPS loops until the browser gives up, and teams
"fix" that by removing the redirect.

Cloud providers encrypt some links for you; AWS encrypts traffic between its zones (separate data
centres) and regions (geographic areas) and,
on some instance types, between instances. Its documentation still leaves TLS to you
([AWS EC2 data protection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/data-protection.html)),
because a cable that is encrypted does not stop a compromised machine on the same network, and it
identifies nobody.

:::note[From the trenches]
"It's an internal network" is the most common reason for plain HTTP between services, and it does
not survive how breaches unfold. Attackers rarely land on the service they want; they land on a
forgotten build server and move sideways, reading internal traffic as they go. The 2013 leak about
the private links between Google's data centres
([MUSCULAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUSCULAR)) showed a slide pointing at where encryption was
removed. I would start any new system with TLS on every hop and make plain text the exception that
needs a written reason.
:::

## Build it yourself

Two programs that run against a **private CA**: a root and an intermediate we create ourselves,
trusted only by our own test clients, so nothing here touches the real internet. `make-certs.sh`
builds it: a root, an intermediate, a good
server certificate, an expired one, one for the wrong name, an impostor's from a CA nobody trusts,
and two client certificates. All files are in

### Step 1: what a client checks, and what `verify=False` removes

### Step 2: mutual TLS with authorization by identity

A payments service that requires a client certificate, reads the caller's SPIFFE ID from it, and
checks what that caller may do:

```python title="mtls.py (excerpt)"

ALLOWED = {
    "spiffe://shop.internal/orders": {"POST /charge"},
    "spiffe://shop.internal/reports": {"GET /summary"},
}


def caller_identity(cert: dict) -> str | None:
    """The URI in the certificate's Subject Alternative Name, e.g. spiffe://shop.internal/orders."""
    return next((value for kind, value in cert.get("subjectAltName", ()) if kind == "URI"), None)


def payments_server() -> int:
    context = ssl.SSLContext(ssl.PROTOCOL_TLS_SERVER)
    context.load_cert_chain(CERTS / "server-fullchain.pem", CERTS / "server.key")
    context.verify_mode = ssl.CERT_REQUIRED  # this one line is what makes it *mutual* TLS
    context.load_verify_locations(CERTS / "root.pem")  # client certs must chain to our root
```

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[The certificate nobody renewed on the security tool]
In the 2017 Equifax breach, a device that inspected encrypted traffic for attacks had a certificate
that had expired about 10 months earlier, so it was not inspecting that traffic at all. Attackers
moved data out over encrypted connections for about 76 days; once the certificate was replaced on
29 July 2017, the suspicious traffic was noticed almost at once
([GAO-18-559](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-559)).
:::

An expired certificate on a monitoring or security tool alerts nobody, because nothing users see
breaks. It fails silently and **open**: traffic keeps flowing, only now unchecked. Put internal tooling on the same served-certificate monitor as
the public site.

- **Renewed on disk, old one in memory.** ACME clients write files; web servers and proxies
  such as nginx and HAProxy, Java services and many client libraries read certificates only at
  startup. Reload after renewal, and check what is served.
- **`verify=False` in a codebase is an incident, not a style problem.** It almost always arrived as a
  fix for a missing intermediate or a corporate proxy root. Fix the chain, or add that one root to
  that one client's **trust bundle**, the file of trusted roots it reads.
- **Containers carry their own trust stores.** A minimal container image without CA certificates, or with a stale
  bundle, fails every outbound call after a CA changes its chain. Treat the CA bundle as a
  dependency you update.
- **Infrastructure code picks old policies.** As of October 2026, an AWS Application Load Balancer
  created in the web console defaults to a **TLS policy** (a named list of allowed versions and
  ciphers) with TLS 1.2 and 1.3 and the post-quantum hybrid from earlier, while one created
  through the command line or CloudFormation (AWS's infrastructure-as-code tool) without a policy
  still gets
  `ELBSecurityPolicy-2016-08`, which allows TLS 1.0
  ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/describe-ssl-policies.html)).
  Always name the policy.
- **Identical expiry everywhere is one point of failure.** The Azure outage above hit every region
  at once because every region held the same certificate. Stagger renewals.

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**What TLS costs.** One round trip for a new connection in TLS 1.3 and little CPU: when Gmail moved
everyone to HTTPS in 2010 it cost under 1% of front-end CPU
([Langley, Modadugu and Chang](https://www.imperialviolet.org/2010/06/25/overclocking-ssl.html)).
The real cost is operations: keys to protect, certificates to renew, deploy and monitor, ticket keys
to rotate, and a class of outage, expiry, that plain text never has.

**What it does not solve.** The endpoints: after decryption, a compromised machine reads everything.
Data at rest, stored on disks and in databases. Authorization. Trustworthiness: phishing sites
have valid certificates. Metadata, the facts about the traffic rather than its content: addresses,
timing and sizes, and the site name unless ECH applies.

**When not to reach for it alone.** Inside one machine over a Unix socket (a connection between two
programs on the same machine) it adds little. To protect a whole network link, including protocols
that cannot speak TLS, a WireGuard or IPsec tunnel, which encrypts all traffic between two machines
or networks below the level of TLS, may be the better layer, alongside TLS rather than instead of
it. Never build your own replacement: every broken scheme on this page was once someone's careful
design.

## Interview corner

1. **"How does the client know it is talking to the real server?"** Chain to a trusted root, name
   match, dates, and above all the CertificateVerify signature over this handshake's transcript,
   which includes both key shares. Follow-up: *"So can a man in the middle never win?"* Yes, with a
   certificate from a CA the client trusts: a compromised CA or an installed corporate root.
   Certificate Transparency makes the first visible.
2. **"What does forward secrecy protect, and what breaks it?"** Recorded traffic, against later
   theft of the certificate key, thanks to ephemeral key exchange. Broken by RSA key exchange, by
   long-lived session ticket keys in TLS 1.2, and for 0-RTT data by ticket keys in TLS 1.3.
3. **"Would you turn on 0-RTT for this API?"** Only for requests safe to run twice, because early
   data can be replayed on new connections, and per-server anti-replay does not protect a cluster.
   Mention `425 Too Early` and idempotency keys.
4. **"Where would you terminate TLS?"** Edge versus re-encrypt versus passthrough versus mesh mTLS,
   trading the ability to route on paths against plain text inside, and who renews which
   certificates.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Mallory relays your ClientHello to the bank unchanged and relays the bank's whole reply back to you unchanged. What can she read?</summary>

Nothing. The key was agreed between your share and the bank's share, and she knows neither private
value. To read anything she must substitute a share, and then the bank's CertificateVerify covers a
transcript that does not match yours.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. An attacker records traffic to a TLS 1.2 server that uses ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (ECDHE, the elliptic-curve kind) and session tickets, and a year later steals the ticket key the server has used since it booted. What can they decrypt?</summary>

Every recorded connection whose session ticket they captured, including the full handshake that
issued the ticket, because a TLS 1.2 ticket contains the master secret encrypted under that key.
Ephemeral key exchange protected against theft of the certificate key, not the ticket key. Rotate
ticket keys often.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your Python service fails with "unable to get local issuer certificate" calling a partner API that opens fine in Chrome. What is the likely cause, and why is verify=False the wrong fix?</summary>

The partner's server sends only its leaf; Chrome fetches the intermediate through AIA or has it
cached, Python does not. Ask them to send the full chain, or add their intermediate to your trust
bundle for that host meanwhile. The same error appears for an impostor's certificate, so turning
verification off accepts the impostor too.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. You run 12 servers with a ClientHello replay cache in each, and accept POST in 0-RTT. An attacker sends 3 copies of a captured transfer. Roughly how many run?</summary>

The original plus three copies land on random servers. A copy is rejected only if it lands on a
server that already saw that ClientHello. With 12 servers, most copies land somewhere new: four
random picks reach about 3.5 distinct servers, so about 2.5 of the 3 copies run. The fix is a shared cache or, better, no POST in early data.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Your renewal automation breaks and is fixed within 5 days of an alert. With 47-day certificates renewed at two-thirds of their lifetime, what is the latest an alert can fire and still avoid an outage? What changes at 6 days?</summary>

Renewal starts with about 16 days left, so the alert must fire with more than 5 days left; 14 days
leaves plenty of slack. With 6-day certificates renewal starts with 2 days left, so a 5-day fix
cannot avoid expiry: the team must fix renewals within hours, or the lifetime is too short for them.

</details>

## Recap

- The server proves itself with **CertificateVerify**, a signature over the handshake transcript
  including both key shares. The certificate alone proves nothing; anyone can send it.
- TLS authenticates **whatever key a trusted CA vouched for**: compromised CAs and installed roots
  defeat it, and Certificate Transparency makes the first visible.
- **Forward secrecy** comes from ephemeral key exchange and is undone by RSA key exchange and by
  long-lived session ticket keys. Rotate them.
- Encrypted traffic still shows **addresses, the site name (without ECH), sizes and timing**; sizes
  alone can name pages unless responses are padded.
- **0-RTT early data can be replayed** on new connections; per-server caches do not protect a
  cluster. Accept only requests safe to repeat.
- **Revocation mostly does not work**; short lifetimes are the real fix, falling to 47 days by 2029.
  The renewal window is your human response time. Automate everything, alert on overdue renewals,
  monitor what is served.
- **Know where TLS ends.** Re-encrypt with verification, or use mTLS, and remember that mTLS
  authenticates; your code still authorizes.
