## A quick fix on a Friday night

Imagine you work at a small lending startup in Bengaluru. Your loan service calls a partner bank's
API to send money to borrowers. One Friday evening, every call starts failing with the same error:

```text
ssl.SSLCertVerificationError: [SSL: CERTIFICATE_VERIFY_FAILED] certificate verify failed:
unable to get local issuer certificate
```

The strange part is that the bank's developer portal opens perfectly in Chrome. Borrowers are
waiting for their money, so a teammate searches the error, finds a popular answer, and adds
`verify=False` to the HTTP call. The errors stop. Everyone goes home.

Two weeks later, before a funding round, a security engineer from the partner bank reviews your
system. She comes back with three findings:

1. `verify=False` is still in production, in the code that moves money.
2. The load balancer decrypts HTTPS and forwards plain HTTP to every service inside your cloud
   network. The design document says "internal traffic, private network, no TLS needed".
3. The certificate on your internal admin dashboard was bought by hand two years ago by someone who
   has since left. It expires in nine days, and nobody is getting the reminder emails.

Then she asks one question: "If someone joined your office Wi-Fi tonight, or got a shell on any one
machine in your cloud network, which of these connections could they read? Which could they
change? And which could they pretend to be?" The honest answer was: all three, nearly everywhere.

None of this needed a clever attacker or a new vulnerability. Each finding is a team not knowing
what TLS promises and what has to be true for those promises to hold. The foundations page,
[What happens when you type a URL](/foundations/how-the-web-works/), walked through a TLS handshake
as one step of a page load. This page answers the bigger question: **what does TLS actually
promise, what has to be checked for those promises to hold, and where in a real system does it
quietly stop?**

## Why this problem exists

When your phone talks to a bank, the bytes do not travel down a private wire. They cross the café's
Wi-Fi router, your mobile operator or broadband provider, a few networks in between, the bank's
cloud provider, a load balancer, and finally a server. Every one of those is a machine you do not
control, run by people you have never met. And as the [TCP vs UDP](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/) page
showed, TCP's checksum catches random noise, not deliberate changes: anyone who can touch the
packets can rewrite them and fix the checksum.

So anyone on that path can do three things to an unprotected connection:

<Figure caption="Anyone on the path can read, change or impersonate. Each threat needs its own defence: encryption for reading, an integrity tag for changing, and a certificate for pretending.">
  <Threats />
</Figure>

The three defences in the right-hand column are the three promises this whole page is about:

- **Confidentiality**: nobody on the path can read the data.
- **Integrity**: nobody can change, drop, reorder or replay the data without being detected.
- **Authentication**: you know who is on the other end, not only that someone is.

These are not theoretical. In October 2010 a browser add-on called
[Firesheep](https://lwn.net/Articles/412979/) let anyone on a café's open Wi-Fi click on another
customer's name and take over their Facebook or Twitter session, because those sites used HTTPS
only for the login page and sent the session cookie in plain text afterwards. In 2014 Comcast's
public Wi-Fi hotspots in the US were found
[injecting their own JavaScript](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2604422/comcasts-open-wi-fi-hotspots-inject-ads-into-your-browser.html)
into web pages as they passed through: a network operator changing content it was only meant to
carry. And in October 2013, documents leaked by Edward Snowden showed that the US National Security
Agency had tapped the private fibre links *between* Google's data centres
([MUSCULAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUSCULAR)). One slide had a hand-drawn note pointing at
the place where Google's front-end servers removed encryption: "SSL added and removed here". Google
announced within weeks that it was encrypting traffic between its data centres. Inside the
building turned out to be on the path too.

Who gets hurt? Users first: stolen sessions, stolen passwords, money sent to the wrong account.
Then the company: breach notifications, regulators, lost trust. And then engineers, because TLS
failures arrive in two flavours that are both painful. Missing or disabled TLS fails silently, for
months, until someone exploits it. Broken TLS, usually an expired certificate, fails loudly and
completely, often at 3 AM.

A few terms we will use throughout:

- **Plaintext** is readable data; **ciphertext** is the same data after encryption, which looks
  like random bytes. A **key** is the secret number that turns one into the other.
- **Symmetric** cryptography uses one shared key for both directions: fast, but both sides must
  already have the key. **Asymmetric** (public-key) cryptography uses a pair: a **public key**
  anyone may have and a **private key** only its owner has. It is slower, and it solves the problem
  of strangers.
- A **hash** is a fixed-size fingerprint of data, such as SHA-256's 32 bytes. A **MAC** (message
  authentication code) is a fingerprint that needs a key to compute, so only key holders can make a
  valid one. A **digital signature** is the asymmetric version: made with a private key, checked by
  anyone with the public key.
- A **man in the middle** (MITM) is an attacker who sits between two parties and relays, reads or
  alters what they send, while each side believes it is talking directly to the other.
- **Encryption in transit** protects data while it moves between machines. **Encryption at rest**
  protects data stored on disk. They defend against different attackers; this page is about the
  first, and the Encryption at rest page covers the second.

**TLS** (Transport Layer Security) is the standard protocol that provides all three promises for a
stream of bytes, usually a TCP connection. Its predecessor was called SSL, which is why people
still say "SSL certificate". HTTPS is HTTP sent inside TLS. So are secure connections to most
databases, message brokers and email servers, and QUIC builds TLS 1.3 into itself.

## The obvious fixes, and why they break

The fastest way to understand why TLS looks the way it does is to try building it yourself and
watch each attempt fail. Every attempt below is something real teams have shipped.

### Attempt 1: encrypt everything with a shared key

The first idea is to pick a secret key, put it in both the app and the server, and encrypt every
message with it. Two problems appear at once.

The first is **key distribution**. The key now lives inside every copy of your app. Anyone can
download your Android app and pull it apart; tools for that are free and well documented. One
extraction, and every user's traffic is readable, past and future. You cannot give each user their
own key without first having a secure way to deliver it, which is the problem you were trying to
solve.

The second problem is subtler, and it survives even a perfectly hidden key. **Encryption hides
data; it does not protect it from being changed.** Many ciphers work by mixing the plaintext with
a stream of key-derived bytes using XOR. Flip a bit in the ciphertext and exactly the same bit
flips in the decrypted plaintext. An attacker who knows the format of your messages, and API
formats are rarely secret, can edit them without ever learning the key:

A payment of ₹100 became ₹900, the message decrypted cleanly, and the bank had no way to notice.
The cipher in the demo is a toy built from SHA-256 so it runs with only the standard library, but
the attack works the same way on AES in its counter mode (CTR) and, with more effort, on AES in
CBC mode, whenever they are used without an integrity check. The second half of the output is the
repair: compute a MAC over the ciphertext with a second key and reject anything whose tag does not
match. The bit-flipped message is caught at once. So encryption needs a partner: **integrity**.

### Attempt 2: agree on a fresh key for every connection

With encryption plus a tag we have confidentiality and integrity, as long as both sides share keys.
To avoid baking a key into the app, we can use an idea from 1976 called **Diffie-Hellman key
exchange**: two parties who have never met each send one public number and each end up with the
same secret, while anyone who only *listens* learns nothing useful. We will see how it works in the
next section. It is genuinely one of the most surprising tricks in computing.

But "anyone who only listens" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Remember the person running
the fake café hotspot. She does not only listen; she sits in the middle and can answer on behalf
of either side. Step through what she does:

Notice the fingerprints. Alice's key and the bank's key are different. If the two of them could
compare fingerprints over a channel Mallory cannot touch, they would catch her at once. That is
exactly what SSH asks you to do the first time you connect to a server ("the authenticity of host
can't be established… key fingerprint is…"), and what messaging apps call a safety number. It works,
but it needs a human and a second channel, which is hopeless for a billion browsers and a million
websites.

### What we actually need

So the bank must *prove* that the key share came from the bank. It can do that by signing its share
(really, the whole conversation so far) with a private key that only the bank has. Then you need to
know that the matching public key belongs to the bank and not to Mallory, which needs someone you
already trust to vouch for it. That vouching document is a **certificate**. Put the pieces
together, encryption plus integrity, a fresh key exchange, and a signature that ties the exchange to
a name someone trustworthy has vouched for, and you have reinvented TLS.

Before we go inside, here are the neighbours TLS is most often confused with.

| These are often confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| **TLS** vs **SSL** | Same protocol family. SSL 2.0 and 3.0 are broken and retired; TLS 1.0 and 1.1 were formally deprecated in 2021. "SSL certificate" survives as a name for a TLS certificate. |
| **TLS** vs **HTTPS** | TLS protects a byte stream; HTTPS is HTTP carried inside it. Databases, email, gRPC and MQTT can all use TLS without any HTTP. |
| **Encryption** vs **integrity** vs **authentication** | Three separate promises. As the demos showed, any one without the others can be defeated. |
| **Certificate** vs **private key** | The certificate is public and contains the public key; anyone can download and present it. The private key is the secret. Presenting a certificate proves nothing; proving you hold its private key does. |
| **The padlock** vs **a safe site** | The padlock means the connection is private and the name in the address bar is verified. Phishing sites get valid certificates too. It says nothing about whether the site is honest. |
| **In transit** vs **at rest** | TLS protects data on the move. Once it arrives, it is plaintext again in memory, logs and databases unless something else protects it. |
| **TLS** vs **a VPN** | Both encrypt traffic in transit. TLS protects one application's connection, end to end. A VPN (WireGuard, IPsec) protects everything between two networks or devices, and only up to where the tunnel ends. |

## The core idea

Think of crossing a border. At the passport desk, an officer looks at your passport: a document
that ties a name to a face, issued by a government the officer's country recognises. The officer
checks three things: that the document was really issued by a recognised government and not
printed at home, that it has not expired, and that the face in front of them matches the photo.
Only then are you let in, and from then on everything you do is under that name.

TLS works the same way. The **certificate** is the passport: it ties a name, such as `example.com`,
to a public key. A **certificate authority** (CA) is the issuing government. Your device carries a
built-in list of CAs it recognises, the **trust store**. The face is the **private key**: the
server proves it is the real owner of the passport by signing something with it, live, during the
connection. And once the check passes, a fresh secret key is agreed that protects everything else.

The analogy breaks in places worth knowing:

- **Anyone can copy a certificate.** A photocopy of a passport is useless at the border because the
  photo will not match you. A certificate is public, and any server can send yours. What cannot be
  copied is the signature only your private key can make. The private key *is* the face.
- **Any recognised CA can issue for any name.** A real passport for an Indian citizen comes from one
  government. Your browser trusts over a hundred CAs, and any of them is technically able to issue a
  certificate for your bank's name. Certificate Transparency, below, exists because of that.
- **The check is mathematical, not human.** A tired officer might wave a bad passport through. A TLS
  client either verifies every signature or refuses to continue, unless someone turns the check off
  with `verify=False`, which is exactly the officer leaving the desk.

Put precisely: **TLS runs a handshake once per connection. The two sides use an ephemeral
Diffie-Hellman exchange to agree on fresh secret keys that never cross the network. The server sends
a certificate chain binding its name to a public key, and proves it holds the matching private key
by signing the whole handshake. After that, every message travels as a record encrypted and
authenticated with those keys, so it cannot be read, changed, reordered or replayed unnoticed. The
client's checks of name, dates, chain and signature are what make the other two promises mean
anything.**

The diagram shows the shape of a connection. Look at what happens once, and what happens on every
message.

<Figure caption="A TLS connection has two phases. The handshake agrees on keys and proves the server's identity, once. Then every message travels as an encrypted record with an integrity tag. Each of the three promises comes from a different part.">

</Figure>

The expensive, clever part is the handshake at the top, and it happens once. The records at the
bottom are where your data lives, and they use fast symmetric cryptography. This split, slow
asymmetric cryptography to set up a fast symmetric key, is the shape of nearly every secure
protocol you will meet.

## How it works, piece by piece

We will build TLS from the inside out: first the records that carry your data, then how the keys
for them are agreed, then how a certificate proves who is on the other end, then the full
handshake that ties it together. After that come the parts that matter in production: resumption
and 0-RTT, versions and the attacks that shaped them, mutual TLS between services, where TLS ends
inside your own system, and keeping certificates alive.

Everything measured on this page was measured in October 2026, from a network in India, with
Python 3.14 and Node 24, both of which use OpenSSL 3.5.

### Records: encryption and integrity in one step

Once keys are agreed, your data is cut into **records** of at most 16 KB each. Every record is
protected by an **AEAD** cipher (authenticated encryption with associated data): one algorithm that
encrypts the data *and* produces an integrity tag over it, so you cannot accidentally get one
without the other. It is attempt 1's two halves fused into one operation, done right. TLS 1.3 allows
only AEAD ciphers. In practice that means **AES-GCM**, which modern processors accelerate in
hardware, or **ChaCha20-Poly1305**, which is fast in software on phones without that hardware.

<Figure caption="A TLS 1.3 record is a short readable header, the encrypted payload and a 16-byte tag. The tag makes any change, reordering, replay or deletion detectable; what still leaks is the size and timing of the records.">
  <Record />
</Figure>

Two details on that diagram explain a lot of behaviour.

- **Every record has an invisible sequence number.** The nonce, a number that must never repeat
  with the same key, is derived from a counter both sides keep. It is never sent. If an attacker
  drops, reorders or replays a record, the receiver's counter no longer matches, the tag check
  fails, and the connection is torn down with an error. TLS does not try to repair; it refuses.
- **Size and timing still leak.** Encryption hides content, not shape. An observer can see that you
  downloaded 3.2 MB from a video site at 9 PM. TLS 1.3 lets senders add padding, but almost nobody
  uses it. When you design something sensitive, remember that "encrypted" is not "invisible".

How expensive is all this? Far less than people fear. When Gmail switched to HTTPS for everyone in
2010, Google's engineers reported that TLS used under 1% of their front-end servers' CPU, under 10 KB
of memory per connection and under 2% of network overhead, with no extra machines
([Langley, Modadugu and Chang, 2010](https://www.imperialviolet.org/2010/06/25/overclocking-ssl.html)).
Hardware has only got faster since. The record layer is not where TLS costs you.

### Key exchange: agreeing on a secret in public

Now the trick from attempt 2. **Diffie-Hellman** relies on a one-way operation: a calculation that
is quick to compute and practically impossible to undo. Here it is with numbers small enough to check on
a calculator.

<Figure caption="Each side keeps a secret, sends a public number derived from it, and combines the other's number with its own secret. Both reach 2. Someone watching the wire saw 23, 5, 8 and 19, and cannot get 2 without one of the secrets.">
  <KeyExchange />
</Figure>

**The worked example, step by step.** Everyone agrees on a prime p = 23 and a base g = 5; these are
public. You pick a secret a = 6 and send A = 5⁶ mod 23. Since 5⁶ = 15,625 and 15,625 = 23 × 679 + 8,
A = 8. The bank picks a secret b = 15 and sends B = 5¹⁵ mod 23 = 19. Now you compute B⁶ mod 23 =
19⁶ mod 23 = 2, and the bank computes A¹⁵ mod 23 = 8¹⁵ mod 23 = 2. Both arrive at 2 because both
calculated 5^(6×15) mod 23, only in a different order. An eavesdropper has 23, 5, 8 and 19, and to
get 2 needs to find 6 from "5 to the what is 8, mod 23". With numbers this small you can try all 22
possibilities by hand. With real sizes there is no known shortcut.

Real TLS uses the same idea on an elliptic curve called **X25519**, where each public share is 32
bytes and the secrets are 255-bit numbers. The shared result is never used directly; it is fed
through a key derivation function (HKDF) together with a hash of every handshake message, to produce
separate keys for each direction and each phase.

The word that matters most is **ephemeral**: both sides throw their secrets away after the
handshake. That gives TLS 1.3 **forward secrecy**: if someone records your encrypted traffic today
and steals the server's private key next year, they still cannot decrypt the recording, because the
key that protected it existed only in memory for the length of one connection. Older TLS versions
also allowed "RSA key exchange", where the client encrypted the secret with the server's long-term
public key. Steal that key, and every recorded session ever made with it opens up. TLS 1.3 removed
that option entirely.

**The post-quantum change.** A large enough quantum computer, which does not exist yet, could undo
X25519. That matters today because of "harvest now, decrypt later": anyone recording traffic now
could decrypt it once such a machine exists. So browsers and servers have started using a
**hybrid** key exchange called **X25519MLKEM768**, which combines X25519 with ML-KEM, a newer
algorithm designed to resist quantum computers. If either part holds, the key is safe. It was
standardised for TLS in [RFC 10024](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc10024/) in August 2026. The
price is size: the client's key share grows from 32 bytes to 1,216, which pushes the first handshake
message past a single 1,500-byte packet. Adoption has been fast: Cloudflare saw post-quantum support
grow from under 3% of clients at the start of 2024 to over 60% in February 2026
([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-origin-pq-key-transparency-aspa/)). When we connected
to `www.cloudflare.com` from Node 24, the TLS 1.3 connections used X25519MLKEM768 without any
configuration; when we forced TLS 1.2, it fell back to plain X25519.

### Certificates: binding a key to a name

Diffie-Hellman gives you a secret shared with *someone*. The certificate tells you who. Here is a
real one, the certificate `example.com` served on the day this page was written:

<Figure caption="A certificate binds names to a public key for a limited time, and an issuer signs it. The fields that clients check on every connection are in bold: the names, the dates, and the signature.">
  <Certificate />
</Figure>

The fields that matter most in practice:

- **Names.** Clients check the **Subject Alternative Name** (SAN) list, not the older "subject"
  field. `*.example.com` covers `api.example.com` but not `v2.api.example.com` and not
  `example.com` itself, which is why both are listed.
- **Validity dates.** A certificate is valid only between "not before" and "not after", checked
  against the client's own clock. This one lives 90 days, which is typical in 2026.
- **The public key.** ECDSA P-256 here; RSA 2048 is also common. The matching private key stays on
  the server and is never in the certificate.
- **Usage restrictions.** "May be used as a server, not as a CA" stops the owner of a website
  certificate from issuing certificates for other names.
- **The issuer's signature** over all of the above, which is what the next section checks.

During the handshake the server sends its certificate and then signs the transcript of the
handshake so far, including both key shares, with its private key. Mallory can download and send
`example.com`'s certificate like anyone else. What she cannot do is produce that signature, and
because the signature covers the key shares, she cannot swap them either. That is the precise
point where the man-in-the-middle attack from earlier dies.

### The chain of trust

Who signs the certificate, and why should your device believe them? Root CAs do not usually sign
website certificates directly. They sign **intermediate** CA certificates, kept online for daily
issuing, while the root's own private key stays offline in a vault. So a client receives a chain:

<Figure caption="The server sends its own certificate and the intermediate. The root is already in your device's trust store. The client checks each signature upwards and trusts the leaf only if the chain ends at a root it already holds.">
  <Chain />
</Figure>

How many roots does your device trust? We counted Mozilla's trust store, which Firefox, curl and
most Linux distributions use: as of September 2026 it held **121 root certificates** trusted for
websites ([curl's extract of Mozilla's list](https://curl.se/docs/caextract.html)). Apple,
Microsoft, Google and Java keep their own lists, broadly similar but not identical, which is one
source of "works on my laptop, fails on the server".

The client's checks, for every certificate in the chain, are:

1. The signature verifies against the issuer's public key.
2. Today's date is inside the validity period.
3. Each certificate is allowed to do what it is doing: intermediates must be marked as CAs; the leaf
   must be allowed for server authentication.
4. The chain ends at a certificate in the trust store.
5. The leaf's names include the host name you asked for.

The server must send the intermediates. If it sends only its own certificate, the client cannot
build the chain and fails with exactly the error in our story: "unable to get local issuer
certificate". Browsers often hide this mistake, because they cache intermediates they have seen
before and can download missing ones using a link inside the certificate. Most other clients,
including Python, Node, curl and Java, do not. A chain that "works in Chrome but fails in Python" is
almost always a missing intermediate on the server.

Chains are not always short. `example.com` actually sent four certificates: its own, an issuing
intermediate, a second intermediate, and a **cross-signed** root, the SSL.com root signed by an older
Comodo root. Cross-signing lets old devices that only trust the older root still build a path,
while newer devices stop at the newer root they already hold.

### When a CA gets it wrong: transparency and revocation

The weakest point of the system is the one the analogy flagged: any of those 121 roots, or any
intermediate under them, can issue a certificate for any name. In 2011 that stopped being
theoretical. Attackers broke into DigiNotar, a Dutch CA, and issued themselves more than 500
rogue certificates, including one for `*.google.com`. It was used to intercept Gmail
users, almost all of them in Iran; the investigators' interim report counted around 300,000
distinct IP addresses that had been shown the fake certificate
([Fox-IT interim report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1044777/000119312511241796/dex992.htm)).
Browsers removed DigiNotar from their trust stores, and the company went bankrupt within weeks.

Two mechanisms exist to limit the damage of a bad certificate.

**Certificate Transparency** (CT, [RFC 6962](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962)) makes
mis-issuance *visible*. CAs must submit every certificate to public, append-only logs before
browsers accept it. The log returns a signed timestamp (an SCT) that is embedded in the certificate;
the two "timestamps from public logs" on `example.com`'s certificate are exactly these. Chrome
requires SCTs from at least two different log operators
([Chrome CT policy](https://googlechrome.github.io/CertificateTransparency/ct_policy.html)). Anyone
can search the logs, so a company can watch for certificates issued for its own domains that it
did not ask for. Try your own domain at [crt.sh](https://crt.sh/); it is often surprising.

**Revocation** is meant to *cancel* a certificate before it expires, for example after its private
key leaks. In practice it has never worked well. The two mechanisms are CRLs, published lists of
revoked serial numbers, and OCSP, where the client asks the CA about each certificate as it
connects. OCSP leaks every site you visit to the CA, adds a network call to each new connection,
and browsers mostly ignored failures, so an attacker who could block the OCSP request could hide a
revocation. Let's Encrypt, the largest CA, stopped running OCSP altogether in August 2025 and now
publishes CRLs only ([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2024/12/05/ending-ocsp/)).

The industry's real answer to revocation is **short lifetimes**. A stolen key for a 47-day
certificate is useful for at most 47 days, whether or not anyone revokes it. That is the main reason
certificate lifetimes are falling, and the reason the last part of this section exists.

Three things in that sequence are worth knowing in production.

- **The guess.** The client sends key shares before it knows what the server supports. If it
  guessed wrong, the server replies with a **HelloRetryRequest** naming a group it accepts, and the
  handshake costs an extra round trip. A client offering only post-quantum shares to a server that
  does not support them pays this on every connection, which is why clients send a classic share
  alongside.
- **The disguise.** On the wire, a TLS 1.3 ClientHello claims to be TLS 1.2. The real version is
  hidden in an extension. Early versions of TLS 1.3 broke on networks where firewalls and inspection
  boxes rejected any version number they did not recognise; Cloudflare measured about 3% of
  connections failing in 2017, mostly because of such boxes
  ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/why-tls-1-3-isnt-in-browsers-yet/)). The protocol was
  changed to look like a TLS 1.2 resumption. This is the same ossification the TCP vs UDP page
  described for TCP.
- **Downgrade protection.** If a TLS 1.3 server is pushed into negotiating TLS 1.2 by an attacker
  who edits the ClientHello, it puts a special marker in its random value, and a TLS 1.3 client that
  sees the marker aborts. Together with the Finished MAC, that ends a decade of downgrade attacks.

### Resumption and 0-RTT

A full handshake costs one round trip plus some computation: building the key exchange, sending and
checking a certificate chain, verifying signatures. A client that comes back can skip much of it.

**Resumption** uses the ticket from the end of the last handshake. The client sends it in its next
ClientHello, the server recognises it, and both derive keys from the secret they already share,
usually mixed with a fresh Diffie-Hellman exchange to keep forward secrecy. No certificate is sent or
checked. It still takes one round trip, but less data and less work.

**0-RTT** ("zero round trip") goes further. Using keys derived from the ticket, the client sends its
first request *inside* the first flight, alongside the ClientHello, as **early data**. For a user 200
ms away, that request reaches the server 200 ms sooner.

<Figure caption="How many round trips TLS adds after TCP's own. TLS 1.3 halves TLS 1.2's cost; resumption keeps one round trip but skips the certificate; 0-RTT removes the wait entirely, at the price of replay.">
  <HandshakeRtts />
</Figure>

We measured this against `www.cloudflare.com`, about 13 ms away, in Step 5 of "Build it yourself":
a full TLS 1.2 handshake took about two round trips, a full TLS 1.3 handshake about one and a half,
and a resumed one about one. The extra half round trip is most likely computation on both ends,
building and checking the certificate chain and the larger post-quantum key exchange, and
resumption skips the certificate work.

0-RTT has a real price, and it is the reason it is off by default almost everywhere: **early data can
be replayed**. The keys for it come from the old ticket, not from a fresh exchange with this server,
so nothing stops an attacker from copying the first flight and sending it again.

The specification says plainly that there are no guarantees against replay between connections for
early data ([RFC 9846](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9846.html)). The practical defences:

- **Only accept safe requests early.** As of October 2026, Cloudflare's 0-RTT, when you turn it on, accepts only `GET`,
  `HEAD` and `OPTIONS` requests in early data and adds an `Early-Data: 1` header so your origin can
  tell ([Cloudflare docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/speed/optimization/protocol/0-rtt-connection-resumption/)).
- **Let the application refuse.** [RFC 8470](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8470) defines the
  `425 Too Early` status: a server that sees `Early-Data: 1` on a request it will not risk answers
  425, and the client sends it again once the handshake is complete.
- **Make the operation idempotent anyway.** A request with an idempotency key, the subject of a
  later page, is safe to replay whether the replay comes from 0-RTT, a retry or a user double-click.

### Versions and the attacks that shaped them

Every change in TLS has a broken thing behind it. You do not need to remember the details of each
attack, but the pattern is worth knowing, because it explains what TLS 1.3 removed and why your
configuration should not bring any of it back.

| Year | Attack | What it exploited | What we learned |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | BEAST | Predictable CBC initialisation vectors in TLS 1.0 | Old modes of encryption leak; use AEAD |
| 2012 | CRIME | Compression before encryption: a secret's presence changed the size | Compression of secrets leaks; TLS 1.3 removed it |
| 2014 | Heartbleed | An OpenSSL bug returned up to 64 KB of server memory, including private keys | Implementations fail too; patch fast and rotate keys |
| 2014 | POODLE | SSL 3.0's padding, reached by forcing a downgrade | Turn old versions off; prevent downgrades |
| 2015 | Logjam, FREAK | Downgrades to 1990s "export-grade" keys of 512 bits | Remove weak options; do not merely prefer strong ones |
| 2017 | ROBOT | RSA key exchange, a 1998 attack returning on modern servers | RSA key exchange removed in TLS 1.3 |

Sources: [RFC 7457](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7457) summarises the attacks up to 2015,
[heartbleed.com](https://heartbleed.com/) and [weakdh.org](https://weakdh.org/) the two famous ones,
and [robotattack.org](https://robotattack.org/) ROBOT. When Logjam was published, 8.4% of the top
million domains were vulnerable; ROBOT was found on 27 of the top 100 sites, including Facebook and
PayPal.

The lesson TLS 1.3's designers drew was to **remove options, not add them**. TLS 1.3 has no RSA key
exchange, no CBC, no RC4, no compression, no renegotiation and no SHA-1 in signatures. It has five
cipher suites, of which three are used in practice: `TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256`,
`TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384` and `TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256`. A suite now names only the AEAD cipher
and the hash; the key exchange and the signature algorithm are negotiated separately.

Where do the versions stand as of October 2026? SSL 2.0 and 3.0 are dead. TLS 1.0 and 1.1 were
deprecated by [RFC 8996](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8996) in March 2021, and browsers refuse
them. TLS 1.2 is still widely used and still acceptable when configured with only ECDHE key exchange
and AEAD ciphers; RFC 9846 retired its specification document and added requirements for TLS 1.2
implementations. TLS 1.3 is the default everywhere modern.

### Mutual TLS: when the server asks who you are

Everything so far authenticates the server. The client stays anonymous at the TLS level and proves
who it is later, with a password, a cookie or a token. Between your own services that is often not
good enough: a token can be stolen and replayed from anywhere, and a network location is not an
identity. In **mutual TLS** (mTLS) the server asks for a certificate too.

The identity inside a service certificate is usually a URI in the SAN field following a convention
called **SPIFFE**, such as `spiffe://shop.internal/orders`. Notice the split in the last steps,
because it is the most common mistake with mTLS: **a valid client certificate proves who is calling,
not what they may do**. "Any service with a certificate from our CA can call any endpoint" is a
policy, and usually the wrong one. Our Step 3 example below shows `reports` holding a perfectly valid
certificate and still getting `403 Forbidden` for an operation only `orders` may perform.

mTLS is rarely done by hand at scale, because every service needs a certificate, and they should be
short-lived. A **service mesh** such as Istio or Linkerd puts a small proxy next to each service, gives
each workload a certificate automatically, and rotates it constantly. Istio's control plane acts as
a CA: a local agent generates each workload's private key, which never leaves the pod, and gets it
signed. Istio's default workload certificate lives for 24 hours. Its documentation describes a
`PERMISSIVE` mode that accepts both plain text and mTLS during migration, and a `STRICT` mode that
accepts only mTLS ([Istio security concepts](https://istio.io/latest/docs/concepts/security/)).

### Where TLS ends inside your system

The security engineer's second finding was about a question that diagrams of TLS usually skip:
*where does the encryption stop?* A real request crosses a CDN, a load balancer, perhaps an API
gateway and a sidecar proxy before it reaches your code, and each of them may decrypt it. Decrypting
TLS at a proxy is called **TLS termination**. There are four common patterns.

<Figure caption="TLS can end at the edge, be re-encrypted to the service, pass through untouched, or be replaced by mutual TLS between services. Only the first leaves plain text inside your network.">
  <Termination />
</Figure>

- **Terminate at the edge.** The load balancer or CDN decrypts, and plain HTTP travels to your
  services. It is simple and was the default design for years. It is also exactly what the "SSL added
  and removed here" slide showed, and it means anyone who gets onto any machine or network segment
  inside can read and change everything, including passwords and tokens in headers.
- **Re-encrypt.** The proxy decrypts, does its job (routing by path, a web application firewall,
  adding headers), then opens a new TLS connection to the service. The proxy can read the traffic,
  but nothing else inside can. The connection to the service must be *verified*, or it is
  encryption to whoever answers.
- **Passthrough.** The load balancer forwards the encrypted bytes without decrypting them, routing
  on the TCP connection or the SNI name in the ClientHello. It cannot see paths or headers, so it
  cannot route on them, but the private key lives only on your service.
- **A mesh with mutual TLS.** Traffic is decrypted at the edge, then every hop between services is
  mutual TLS. This is where most large systems are heading: encrypted everywhere, and every caller
  identified.

Cloud providers add a layer of their own, and it is worth knowing what it does and does not cover.
AWS, for example, encrypts all traffic between its availability zones and regions at the physical
layer, and some instance types encrypt instance-to-instance traffic in hardware automatically, but
only within a VPC or peered VPCs and not through a load balancer. AWS's own documentation still says
it is your responsibility to use TLS for sensitive data in transit
([AWS EC2 data protection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/data-protection.html)).
Hardware encryption on a cable protects against someone tapping the cable. It does not protect
against a compromised machine on the same network, and it does not authenticate anyone.

:::note[From the trenches]
"It's an internal network" is the most common reason teams give for plain HTTP between services,
and the reasoning has not survived contact with how breaches actually unfold. Attackers rarely break
in at the service they want; they land on a forgotten build server or a developer laptop and move
sideways, reading internal traffic as they go. In practice I would start a new system with TLS on
every hop and make plain text the exception that needs a written reason. With a mesh or a managed
load balancer it costs very little to do from day one, and a lot to add later.
:::

**Lifetimes are falling fast.** In April 2025 the CA/Browser Forum, the body of CAs and browser makers
that sets the rules, voted to shrink the maximum lifetime of a public certificate step by step
([ballot SC-081v3](https://cabforum.org/2025/04/11/ballot-sc081v3-introduce-schedule-of-reducing-validity-and-data-reuse-periods/)).

As of October 2026, Let's Encrypt still issues 90-day certificates by default, offers 6-day ones, and
plans to move its default to 64 days in February 2027 and 45 days in February 2028. It recommends
renewing at about two-thirds of a certificate's lifetime, and using **ARI** (ACME Renewal
Information), which lets the CA tell your client when to renew, for example ahead of a mass
revocation ([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2025/12/02/from-90-to-45)). It also stopped
sending expiry reminder emails in June 2025
([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2025/06/26/expiration-notification-service-has-ended/)): if
your plan for expiry was "we'll get an email", you no longer have a plan.

Why renew at two-thirds rather than at the last moment? Watch what happens over two years to six
certificates renewed by hand. A person renews each one when a reminder arrives, a month before
expiry; now and then a reminder goes to someone who has left.

With the default 200-day certificates, most renewals happen quietly inside the amber band. The
failures are the problem: a missed reminder gives no warning at all. The certificate sits there,
green, until the day it turns red, and then someone has to find out who owned it, where its key is,
and how it was installed, while every client is failing. In our run, five of 22 renewals were missed
and the six certificates spent 10 days expired between them. Drag the lifetime down to 47 days and
the same team does 109 renewals by hand in two years, with 28 days of outage.

Now the same six certificates with automation that renews at two-thirds of the lifetime and retries
every day. Automation also breaks, for boring reasons: a firewall change blocks the HTTP challenge,
an API token for the DNS provider expires, a server is rebuilt without the renewal job. Each red
cross is one of those.

<CertLifecycleSim mode="automated" />

With the defaults, 90-day certificates, an alert at 14 days left and three days for a human to
fix things, eight breakages happened in two years and none of them caused an outage. A break shows
up as a short amber band: renewal is overdue, the alert fires, someone fixes it, and the certificate
renews with days to spare. That is the whole point of renewing early. **The renewal window is the
time you give humans to notice and fix broken automation.** Set the alert to 0 and every one of
those eight breakages becomes a three-day outage, 24 days in total. Press "CA unreachable for 3
days" and nothing happens at all: the renewals retry and succeed the day the CA comes back.

Then drag the lifetime down to 6 days. Over a thousand certificates are issued without anyone
noticing, which is the good news. The bad news is the window: a third of 6 days is 2 days, so a fix
that takes 3 days arrives after expiry, alert or no alert. Short lifetimes are wonderful for
limiting the damage of a stolen key, and they demand fast, reliable automation and humans who can
respond within the window. The comparison in the next section puts all three approaches on the same
bad luck.

## Choosing between them

Most TLS decisions in a system come down to two questions: where should encryption end, and how
strict should the configuration be?

**Where should TLS end?**

| | Edge only | Re-encrypt | Passthrough | Mesh mTLS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plain text inside the network | yes | only inside the proxy | no | no |
| Proxy can route on paths and headers | yes | yes | no, only on SNI | yes |
| Where private keys live | edge | edge and services | services only | edge, plus a short-lived key per service |
| Callers identified cryptographically | no | no | no | yes |
| Operational cost | lowest | moderate: certificates on every service | moderate | highest, unless a mesh or platform does it |
| Typical use | legacy systems, low-risk static sites | most web apps behind a managed load balancer | databases, mail, end-to-end requirements | microservices at scale, zero-trust networks |

**How strict should the configuration be?** Mozilla publishes server configuration profiles that
most tools follow ([Mozilla server-side TLS, guidelines version 6.0](https://ssl-config.mozilla.org/)):

| Profile | Versions | Oldest clients that can connect | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modern | TLS 1.3 only | Firefox 63, Chrome 70, Safari 12.1, Android 10, Java 11, OpenSSL 1.1.1 | You control the clients: internal services, mobile apps, APIs for modern SDKs |
| Intermediate | TLS 1.2 (ECDHE with AEAD only) and 1.3 | Android 4.4.2, IE 11 on Windows 10, Java 8u161, Safari 9 | Public websites and APIs: the right default for almost everyone |

**How should certificates be renewed?** Here are three strategies on exactly the same two years of
bad luck: the same automation breakages, the same days the CA is unreachable, the same missed
reminders.

<CertLifecycleSim mode="compare" />

In our run, the manual panel lost 9 days to missed reminders while someone renewed 23 certificates
by hand. The 90-day automated panel absorbed every breakage with no downtime, because a third of 90
days is a month of slack. The 6-day panel lost 18 days, roughly one per breakage, because a fix that
takes three days cannot fit into a two-day window. The exact numbers change with luck, so press
Reset with different sliders; the shape does not.

**How to choose.** In practice:

1. **Encrypt every hop by default**, including inside your network. Use re-encryption behind a
   managed load balancer for a typical web app, and a mesh with mTLS once you have many services.
   Passthrough when the backend must hold the only key.
2. **Use the intermediate profile for anything public**, modern for anything where you control
   every client.
3. **Automate every certificate**, renew at about two-thirds of its lifetime, and alert on "renewal
   overdue", not only on "expires soon". 90-day certificates with good alerting are a comfortable
   default in 2026; go shorter when your automation and on-call response are proven.
4. **Never turn off verification** to fix an error. Find out which of the five checks failed and fix
   that.

## Build it yourself

We have already broken encryption without integrity and key exchange without authentication. Now we
build the real thing with the standard libraries: a private certificate authority, a TLS server and
client that show each verification failure, mutual TLS with identity-based authorization, a
production-style certificate monitor, and a measurement of handshake costs on the internet. Every
file is in [examples/tls](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/tls),
and every output on this page came from running them in October 2026.

### Step 1: a private certificate authority

For services inside your own system you usually do not want public certificates; you want your own
CA, trusted only by your own services. Real setups use a tool for this, such as a mesh's built-in CA,
HashiCorp Vault or a cloud private CA, but the steps underneath are the same, and `openssl` shows
them plainly. This excerpt from `make-certs.sh` creates a root, an intermediate and a server
certificate for `localhost`:

```bash title="make-certs.sh (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/tls/make-certs.sh
# 1. The root CA: self-signed, the thing clients choose to trust.
key root
openssl req -new -x509 -key root.key -out root.pem -days 3650 \
  -subj "/O=Senior Dev Notes/CN=SDN Test Root CA" \
  -addext "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE" -addext "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign"

# 2. The intermediate CA, signed by the root. It signs everything below.
key intermediate
openssl req -new -key intermediate.key -subj "/O=Senior Dev Notes/CN=SDN Test Issuing CA 1" -out intermediate.csr
openssl x509 -req -in intermediate.csr -CA root.pem -CAkey root.key -CAcreateserial \
  -days 1825 -extfile ext.cnf -extensions intermediate -out intermediate.pem 2>/dev/null

# 3. Leaf certificates signed by the intermediate.
key server
openssl req -new -key server.key -subj "/CN=localhost" -out server.csr
sign server server 90
cat server.pem intermediate.pem > server-fullchain.pem   # what a server should send
```

The full script also makes an expired certificate, one for the wrong name, two client certificates
for mTLS, and an impostor's certificate for `localhost` signed by a CA nobody trusts. The
`server-fullchain.pem` line is the one that our story's bank forgot: a server must send its
certificate *and* the intermediate.

### Step 3: mutual TLS with identity-based authorization

Now a payments service that requires a client certificate, reads the caller's SPIFFE ID from it,
and checks what that caller may do:

```python title="mtls.py"

ALLOWED = {
    "spiffe://shop.internal/orders": {"POST /charge"},
    "spiffe://shop.internal/reports": {"GET /summary"},
}


def caller_identity(cert: dict) -> str | None:
    """The URI in the certificate's Subject Alternative Name, e.g. spiffe://shop.internal/orders."""
    return next((value for kind, value in cert.get("subjectAltName", ()) if kind == "URI"), None)


def payments_server() -> int:
    context = ssl.SSLContext(ssl.PROTOCOL_TLS_SERVER)
    context.load_cert_chain(CERTS / "server-fullchain.pem", CERTS / "server.key")
    context.verify_mode = ssl.CERT_REQUIRED  # this one line is what makes it *mutual* TLS
    context.load_verify_locations(CERTS / "root.pem")  # client certs must chain to our root
    listener = socket.create_server(("127.0.0.1", 0))

    def handle(conn: socket.socket) -> None:
        spare = conn.dup()  # wrap_socket() takes over conn; keep a handle for the failure path
        try:
            with context.wrap_socket(conn, server_side=True) as tls:
                who = caller_identity(tls.getpeercert())
                operation = tls.recv(1024).decode().strip()
                allowed = operation in ALLOWED.get(who, set())
                tls.sendall(f"{'200 OK' if allowed else '403 Forbidden'} for {who}".encode())
```

### Step 4: a certificate monitor that alerts in time

The most valuable TLS code most teams will ever write is not cryptography. It is a scheduled check
that connects to every endpoint exactly as a client would and complains early. The detail that
makes this one production-shaped is the alert rule: it warns when a certificate is past two-thirds
of its lifetime, which means renewal automation should already have replaced it and has not.

  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="cert_expiry.py"
# runnable version: examples/tls/python/cert_expiry.py
def check(host: str, port: int = 443) -> tuple[str, str]:
    context = ssl.create_default_context()  # the system trust store, like a real client
    try:
        with socket.create_connection((host, port), timeout=5) as raw:
            with context.wrap_socket(raw, server_hostname=host) as tls:
                cert = tls.getpeercert()
    except ssl.SSLCertVerificationError as err:
        return "CRIT", f"verification failed: {err.verify_message}"
    except (OSError, ssl.SSLError) as err:
        return "CRIT", f"cannot connect: {err}"

    start = ssl.cert_time_to_seconds(cert["notBefore"])
    end = ssl.cert_time_to_seconds(cert["notAfter"])
    lifetime = (end - start) / 86400
    left = (end - time.time()) / 86400
    issuer = dict(item[0] for item in cert["issuer"]).get("organizationName", "?")
    detail = f"{left:5.0f} of {lifetime:3.0f} days left, issuer {issuer}"

    if left < CRITICAL_DAYS:
        return "CRIT", detail
    if left < lifetime / 3:  # renewal should have happened by now
        return "WARN", detail + " (renewal overdue)"
    return "OK", detail
```

### Step 5: what a handshake costs on the real internet

Finally, a measurement. We connect to `www.cloudflare.com` seven times each way: a full TLS 1.2
handshake, a full TLS 1.3 handshake, and a TLS 1.3 handshake resuming the previous session. The
address is looked up once beforehand, so DNS does not pollute the timings:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">

```python title="handshake.py"
# runnable version: examples/tls/python/handshake.py
def handshake(version: ssl.TLSVersion, session: ssl.SSLSession | None = None):
    context = CONTEXTS[version]
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    raw = socket.create_connection(ADDRESS, timeout=5)
    t1 = time.perf_counter()
    tls = context.wrap_socket(raw, server_hostname=HOST, session=session)  # handshake happens here
    t2 = time.perf_counter()
    # TLS 1.3 sends resumption tickets after the handshake, so make one request to receive them.
    tls.sendall(f"HEAD / HTTP/1.1\r\nHost: {HOST}\r\nConnection: close\r\n\r\n".encode())
    while tls.recv(65536):
        pass
    saved, reused = tls.session, tls.session_reused
    tls.close()
    return (t1 - t0) * 1000, (t2 - t1) * 1000, saved, reused
```

```js title="handshake.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/tls/js/handshake.mjs
function handshake(version, session) {
  return new Promise((resolve, reject) => {
    const result = {};
    let t1;
    const t0 = performance.now();
    const socket = tls.connect({ host: ADDRESS, port: 443, servername: HOST, minVersion: version, maxVersion: version, session }, () => {
      const t2 = performance.now();
      Object.assign(result, { tcp: t1 - t0, tls: t2 - t1, reused: socket.isSessionReused(), group: socket.getEphemeralKeyInfo()?.name });
      // TLS 1.3 sends resumption tickets after the handshake, so make one request to receive them.
      socket.write(`HEAD / HTTP/1.1\r\nHost: ${HOST}\r\nConnection: close\r\n\r\n`); // the server closes
      socket.resume();
      socket.on('close', () => setImmediate(resolve, result)); // let a late 'session' event land first
    });
    socket.once('connect', () => { t1 = performance.now(); }); // TCP is up; the TLS handshake starts now
    socket.on('session', (s) => { result.session = s; }); // listen early: it can fire before the callback
    socket.on('error', reject);
  });
}
```

measure the rate.

## How real systems do it

The standards move slowly, but the defaults around them change every year. Facts in this section can
change, so each is marked with when we checked.

**Let's Encrypt made automation normal.** It launched in 2015 with free 90-day certificates issued
only through ACME, and by late 2025 it was frequently issuing ten million certificates a day. Its
lifetime plan (64 days in February 2027, 45 days in February 2028) and its end of OCSP and expiry
emails are pushing every other CA and every hosting platform towards automatic renewal, as of October
2026 ([Let's Encrypt lifetimes](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/cert-lifetimes/)).

**CDNs make the termination choice explicit.** Cloudflare's SSL modes are a good map of the patterns
in this page: *Off*; *Flexible*, HTTPS to the visitor but plain HTTP to your origin; *Full*, HTTPS to the
origin without checking its certificate; and *Full (strict)*, HTTPS to the origin with the certificate
verified ([Cloudflare SSL modes](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/origin-configuration/ssl-modes/)).
Only the last one gives the promises this page is about end to end. Flexible gives the visitor a
padlock while the second half of the journey travels in plain text, and Full is our `verify=False`
on the back half.

**Cloud load balancers pick your TLS policy, and the default depends on how you create them.** As of
October 2026, an AWS Application Load Balancer created in the console defaults to a TLS 1.2 and 1.3
policy with post-quantum key exchange (`ELBSecurityPolicy-TLS13-1-2-Res-PQ-2025-09`), while one
created through the CLI or CloudFormation without a policy still gets the legacy
`ELBSecurityPolicy-2016-08`, which allows TLS 1.0
([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/describe-ssl-policies.html)).
Infrastructure code should always name the policy.

**Service meshes rotate certificates daily.** As of October 2026, Istio issues every workload a
certificate that lives 24 hours by default and rotates it automatically, with the private key generated inside the workload.
Short lifetimes stop being scary when nobody has to do anything to renew them.

**Browsers moved to post-quantum key exchange quietly.** Chrome switched to the standard
X25519MLKEM768 in version 131 in November 2024, and Cloudflare saw over 60% of clients supporting
post-quantum key agreement by February 2026, while only about 10% of the origin servers behind
Cloudflare supported it. The client side moved first because a handful of browsers and libraries
cover most users; servers are millions of separate configurations.

**Big sites send long chains for old devices.** `example.com`'s four-certificate chain, with a
cross-signed root, is a deliberate compatibility choice: devices that have not had a trust-store
update in years can still find a path. The cost is a few extra kilobytes in every full handshake.

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A real breach: the certificate nobody renewed on the security tool]
In 2017, attackers stole personal data on 145.5 million Americans from Equifax. The US Government
Accountability Office's report found that Equifax had a device inspecting encrypted traffic for
signs of attack, but the certificate it needed had expired about 10 months before the breach, so
encrypted traffic was not being inspected at all. The attackers ran commands and took data out over
encrypted connections for about 76 days. When the certificate was finally replaced on 29 July 2017,
the administrator saw the suspicious traffic almost immediately
([GAO-18-559](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-559)).
:::

The lesson is broader than "renew certificates". An expired certificate on a monitoring or security
tool does not page anyone, because nothing user-facing breaks. It fails silent and open. Monitor the
certificates of your internal tooling as carefully as your public site.

:::danger[A real outage: one expiry date, every region]
On 22 February 2013, Windows Azure Storage stopped serving HTTPS in every region for about 11 and a
half hours: its certificates expired. The renewed certificates had been created weeks earlier and
stored in the secret store, but the release that would deploy them was not flagged as containing
certificate updates and was delayed behind other work. Because the secret store already held the new
certificates, the expiry alerts went quiet
([InfoQ summary of Microsoft's post-mortem](https://www.infoq.com/news/2013/03/feb22_azure_outage)).
:::

Two lessons hide in that one. Monitor the certificate *being served*, not the one in your secret
store. And identical certificates with identical expiry dates across every region are a single point
of failure that no amount of redundancy protects against.

**Expired certificates take down whole networks, not only websites.** On 6 December 2018, an expired
certificate in two versions of Ericsson's core network software took down mobile data for O2 in the
UK, including the networks that rent O2's capacity, for most of a day, and affected SoftBank in Japan
and operators in other countries
([Ericsson](https://www.ericsson.com/en/press-releases/2018/12/update-on-software-issue-impacting-certain-customers)).
Certificates hide inside appliances and vendor software too. Ask your vendors when theirs expire.

:::caution[The Friday fix, explained]
Back to our story. The bank had renewed its certificate that Friday and configured its server to
send only the new leaf, without the intermediate. Chrome filled the gap from its cache, so the portal
worked; Python, like most non-browser clients, did not. The right fix was a one-line change on the
bank's server, or, while waiting for them, adding the bank's intermediate to your client's trust
bundle for that one host. `verify=False` "fixed" it by making your service willing to send borrowers'
money instructions to anyone who could intercept the connection. If you ever find it in a codebase,
treat it as a security incident, not a code smell.
:::

**Renewed on disk, old one served.** ACME clients renew files. Web servers, proxies and Java
applications often read certificates only at startup. The certificate on disk is fresh, the one in
memory expires, and the dashboard that checks the file says everything is fine. Reload after renewal,
and monitor what is served, as in Step 4.

**A wrong clock breaks every certificate.** Clients compare validity dates with their own clock. A
device whose clock is months off, a virtual machine restored from an old snapshot, or an embedded
board with no battery-backed clock will reject valid certificates as "not yet valid" or "expired".
The foundations page has more on this.

**Certificate pinning trades one risk for another.** Some mobile apps "pin" a server's certificate or
public key, refusing anything else even if a trusted CA signed it. It defeats rogue CAs, and it turns
every certificate rotation into a risk of locking out every user with an old app version. Browsers
abandoned their pinning mechanism years ago for exactly this reason. If you pin, pin to several keys,
including a backup that is not yet in use, and have a way to update the pins remotely.

**Corporate inspection proxies break clients.** Many companies decrypt employees' traffic with a
proxy that issues its own certificates from a company root installed on managed laptops. Browsers
trust it; Python, Node, Java and Docker images do not, because they carry their own trust stores.
"Certificate verify failed" on a work laptop and not at home is usually this. The fix is to add the
company root to the tool's trust bundle, not to disable verification.

:::caution[Flexible TLS and redirect loops]
A CDN set to Cloudflare's Flexible mode talks plain HTTP to your origin. If your origin redirects all
HTTP requests to HTTPS, as it should, every request bounces between them until the browser gives up
with "too many redirects". Teams often "fix" it by removing the redirect. The real fix is to give the
origin a certificate and switch the CDN to Full (strict).
:::

**SNI tells the network where you are going.** The site name in the ClientHello is readable by
everyone on the path, which is how some networks block sites even over HTTPS.
**Encrypted Client Hello** ([RFC 9849](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc9849)) hides it, but only when
both the browser and the server, usually a large CDN, support it.

**Heartbleed's real cost was the keys.** When the bug was disclosed in April 2014, around 17% of
trusted HTTPS servers were believed vulnerable, and patching was only half the job. Any private key
that had been in the memory of a vulnerable server had to be treated as stolen, so certificates had
to be replaced and revoked, and that took far longer. Plan for key rotation before you need it.

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**What TLS costs.** One round trip for a new connection (TLS 1.3), plus a few milliseconds of
computation. A little CPU and memory, small enough to be invisible on modern hardware. And the real
cost: operations. Certificates to issue, renew, deploy, reload and monitor; private keys to protect;
CAs to choose; trust stores to keep updated; and a whole class of outages, expired certificates,
that plain text never has.

**What TLS does not solve.**

- **The endpoints.** TLS protects data between two machines. If either one is compromised, the
  attacker reads everything in plain text, after decryption.
- **Data at rest.** The moment data arrives, it is plaintext again, in memory, in logs and in the
  database.
- **Authorization.** Even mutual TLS only tells you who is calling. What they may do is your code.
- **Trustworthiness.** A valid certificate proves control of a domain name, nothing else. Phishing
  sites have them.
- **Metadata.** Who talks to whom, when and how much is still visible.
- **Replay of early data** and anything else the application does twice.

**When not to use it, or not alone.** There are few places where TLS should be absent, but there are
places where it is not the right tool. Communication inside one machine, over a Unix domain socket
between two processes, gains little from it. When an entire network link must be protected, including
protocols that cannot speak TLS, a VPN or WireGuard tunnel may be the better layer, as a complement
rather than a replacement. And never build your own replacement: every attempt on this page was a
real design someone once trusted, and each failed in a way its authors did not foresee. Use the
standard library's TLS, a well-known proxy, or your platform's mesh.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their TLS posture in one of two ways: a certificate expires on a Saturday, or
an auditor asks where encryption stops. Both are cheaper to answer on a quiet Tuesday. Keep an
inventory: every endpoint, who owns its certificate, how it renews, and where its TLS terminates.
The certificate monitor from Step 4 pointed at that list is an afternoon of work, and it is the most
outage-preventing afternoon in this whole topic.
:::

## Interview corner

TLS comes up in two ways: as a networking fundamentals question ("what happens in a TLS handshake?")
and inside system design, when the interviewer asks how services talk securely or where to terminate
HTTPS. A weak answer lists "symmetric and asymmetric encryption". A strong one explains which
promise each part provides and what goes wrong without it.

1. **"Walk me through a TLS handshake."** ClientHello with key shares, SNI and ALPN; ServerHello with
   the server's share; from there everything is encrypted; certificate chain, CertificateVerify
   signature over the transcript, Finished; the client verifies chain, name, dates and signature;
   client Finished plus the first request. One round trip in TLS 1.3, two in 1.2. Bonus: forward
   secrecy from ephemeral keys, and session tickets for resumption.
2. **"How does the client know it's talking to the real server?"** The certificate binds the name to
   a public key, the chain ends at a root in the trust store, and the CertificateVerify signature
   proves the server holds the private key *now*, over this handshake's key shares, which is what
   stops a man in the middle. Mention Certificate Transparency for mis-issued certificates.
3. **"Why do we need both asymmetric and symmetric cryptography?"** Asymmetric solves agreeing on a
   key and proving identity between strangers but is slow; symmetric AEAD is fast and protects bulk
   data. The handshake uses the first to set up the second.
4. **"Where would you terminate TLS in this design?"** Discuss edge termination versus re-encryption
   versus passthrough versus mesh mTLS, the trade-off between routing ability and plain text inside,
   and that internal traffic should be encrypted too. Strong answers mention certificate automation
   and who owns renewal.
5. **"What is 0-RTT and why is it dangerous?"** Early data sent with the ClientHello using keys from a
   previous session; saves a round trip; can be replayed, so accept only idempotent requests, keep
   anti-replay state, or use `425 Too Early`.

In system design rounds, a one-sentence mention earns credit: "TLS terminates at the load balancer,
which re-encrypts to services, and services talk over mTLS issued by the mesh, with certificates
rotated automatically."

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A service encrypts messages with AES in counter mode using a key nobody else has, but adds no MAC. What can an attacker on the network do, and what is the fix?</summary>

They cannot read the messages, but they can change them. Counter mode XORs the plaintext with a
key-derived stream, so flipping a bit in the ciphertext flips the same bit in the plaintext. If they
know or can guess the format, they can change an amount or a recipient precisely. The fix is
authenticated encryption, such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305, which rejects any modified message;
in practice, use TLS rather than designing the message protection yourself.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. An attacker records all traffic to your server today. Next year they steal the server's private key. Which of these sessions can they decrypt: TLS 1.3, TLS 1.2 with ECDHE, TLS 1.2 with RSA key exchange?</summary>

Only the TLS 1.2 sessions that used RSA key exchange. There, the session secret was encrypted with the
server's long-term key, so the stolen key decrypts the recording. TLS 1.3 and TLS 1.2 with ECDHE use
ephemeral Diffie-Hellman keys that were discarded after each handshake, so the long-term key cannot
recover them. That property is forward secrecy. The stolen key does let them impersonate the server
from now on, until the certificate is revoked or expires.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your Python service fails with "unable to get local issuer certificate" when calling a partner's API, which opens fine in Chrome. What is the most likely cause, and what should you not do?</summary>

Most likely the partner's server sends its certificate without the intermediate. Chrome fills the
gap from its cache or by downloading it; Python does not. Check with
`openssl s_client -showcerts`. Ask the partner to send the full chain, and meanwhile you can add their
intermediate to your trust bundle for that host. Do not set `verify=False`: it would also accept an
attacker's certificate, as Step 2 showed. The same message also appears for a genuine impostor,
which is why you investigate rather than suppress it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your automation renews 90-day certificates when 30 days are left and retries daily. An alert fires when an overdue certificate has 14 days left, and fixing broken automation takes your team 3 days. If you moved to 6-day certificates with the same renew-at-two-thirds rule, what changes?</summary>

The renewal window shrinks from 30 days to 2 days. With 90-day certificates a breakage has at least
14 days between the alert and expiry, comfortably more than the 3-day fix. With 6-day certificates,
the certificate expires 2 days after renewal starts failing, so a 3-day fix arrives a day too late:
every breakage becomes an outage unless you can fix things within hours. Short lifetimes need faster
detection and response, or more automation that repairs itself. The compare simulation shows exactly
this.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A load balancer terminates TLS and forwards plain HTTP to services. A colleague says that is fine because the services are in a private subnet. Give two concrete risks.</summary>

Anyone who compromises any machine in that network, or gets access to it through a misconfigured
peering or VPN, can read passwords, tokens and personal data in every request, and change requests in
flight. And nothing authenticates the callers: any process that can reach a service can call it while
pretending to be the load balancer. Compliance rules for card and health data also commonly require
encryption on internal networks. Re-encrypt from the load balancer, or use mTLS between services.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. You turn on 0-RTT at your CDN. Which of these endpoints are safe to serve from early data: GET /products, POST /orders, GET /logout?</summary>

Only `GET /products`, which is safe to repeat. `POST /orders` could be replayed to place the same order
twice. `GET /logout` uses GET but changes state; a replay would log the user out again, harmless here
but a reminder that the rule is "safe to run twice", not "uses GET". This is why Cloudflare allows only
GET, HEAD and OPTIONS in early data and marks such requests with `Early-Data: 1`, and why
state-changing GET endpoints are a bad idea anyway.

</details>

## Recap

- **TLS makes three promises**: confidentiality (encryption), integrity (a tag on every record) and
  authentication (a certificate plus a live signature). Any one without the others can be defeated.
- **Encryption without integrity can be edited; key exchange without authentication can be
  intercepted.** Both failures are silent.
- **The handshake** uses ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (X25519, now often hybrid post-quantum
  X25519MLKEM768) for fresh keys with forward secrecy, and a CertificateVerify signature over the
  transcript to prove the server's identity. One round trip in TLS 1.3, two in 1.2.
- **A certificate binds names to a public key** and is public; the private key is the secret. Clients
  check the chain to a root in their trust store, the names, the dates and the signature. Servers must
  send the intermediates.
- **Certificate Transparency** makes mis-issuance visible; revocation barely works; **short lifetimes**
  are the real answer, falling to 47 days by 2029.
- **0-RTT saves a round trip but can be replayed**: only for requests safe to run twice.
- **mTLS gives services identities**; authorization is still your job.
- **Know where TLS ends** in your system and encrypt every hop. Automate every certificate, renew at
  about two-thirds of its lifetime, monitor what is actually served, and never ship `verify=False`.
