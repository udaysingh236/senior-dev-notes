## A quick fix on a Friday night

Imagine you work at a small lending startup in Bengaluru. Your loan service calls a partner bank's
API to send money to borrowers. One Friday evening, every call starts failing with the same error:

```text
ssl.SSLCertVerificationError: [SSL: CERTIFICATE_VERIFY_FAILED] certificate verify failed:
unable to get local issuer certificate
```

The strange part is that the bank's developer portal opens perfectly in Chrome. Borrowers are
waiting for their money, so a teammate searches the error, finds a popular answer, and adds
`verify=False` to the HTTP call. The errors stop. Everyone goes home.

Two weeks later, before a funding round, a security engineer from the partner bank reviews your
system. She comes back with three findings:

1. `verify=False` is still in production, in the code that moves money.
2. The load balancer decrypts HTTPS and forwards plain HTTP to every service inside your cloud
   network. The design document says "internal traffic, private network, no TLS needed".
3. The certificate on your internal admin dashboard was bought by hand two years ago by someone who
   has since left. It expires in nine days, and nobody is getting the reminder emails.

Then she asks one question: "If someone joined your office Wi-Fi tonight, or got a shell on any one
machine in your cloud network, which of these connections could they read? Which could they
change? And which could they pretend to be?" The honest answer was: all three, nearly everywhere.

None of this needed a clever attacker. Each finding is a team not knowing what TLS promises and
what has to be true for those promises to hold. The foundations page,
[What happens when you type a URL](/foundations/how-the-web-works/), walked through a TLS handshake
as one step of a page load. This page answers the bigger question: **what does TLS actually
promise, what has to be checked for those promises to hold, and where in a real system does it
quietly stop?**

## Why this problem exists

When your phone talks to a bank, the bytes cross the café's Wi-Fi router, your mobile operator, a
few networks in between, the bank's cloud provider, a load balancer, and finally a server. Every
one of those is a machine you do not control. And as the [TCP vs UDP](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/)
page showed, TCP's checksum catches random noise, not deliberate changes. So anyone on that path
can do three things to an unprotected connection:

<Figure caption="Anyone on the path can read, change or impersonate. Each threat needs its own defence: encryption for reading, an integrity tag for changing, and a certificate for pretending.">
  <Threats />
</Figure>

The three defences in the right-hand column are the three promises this whole page is about:

- **Confidentiality**: nobody on the path can read the data.
- **Integrity**: nobody can change, drop, reorder or replay the data without being detected.
- **Authentication**: you know who is on the other end, not only that someone is.

These are not theoretical. In 2010 a browser add-on called
[Firesheep](https://lwn.net/Articles/412979/) let anyone on a café's open Wi-Fi take over other
customers' Facebook and Twitter sessions with one click, because those sites sent the session
cookie in plain text after login. In 2013, leaked documents showed that the US National Security Agency had tapped
the private fibre links *between* Google's data centres
([MUSCULAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUSCULAR)), with a slide pointing at where encryption was
removed: "SSL added and removed here". Inside the building turned out to be on the path too.

TLS failures come in two flavours, and both hurt. Missing or disabled TLS fails silently, for
months, until someone exploits it. Broken TLS, usually an expired certificate, fails loudly and
completely, often at 3 AM.

A few terms we will use throughout:

- **Plaintext** is readable data; **ciphertext** is the same data after encryption. A **key** is
  the secret number that turns one into the other.
- **Symmetric** cryptography uses one shared key: fast, but both sides must already have it.
  **Asymmetric** (public-key) cryptography uses a pair: a **public key** anyone may have and a
  **private key** only its owner has. It is slower, and it solves the problem of strangers.
- A **MAC** (message authentication code) is a fingerprint of data that needs a key to compute, so
  only key holders can make a valid one. A **digital signature** is the asymmetric version: made
  with a private key, checked by anyone with the public key.
- A **man in the middle** (MITM) is an attacker who sits between two parties and relays, reads or
  alters what they send, while each side believes it is talking directly to the other.
- **Encryption in transit** protects data while it moves between machines; **encryption at rest**
  protects data stored on disk. This page is about the first.

**TLS** (Transport Layer Security) is the standard protocol that provides all three promises for a
stream of bytes, usually a TCP connection. Its predecessor was called SSL, which is why people
still say "SSL certificate". HTTPS is HTTP sent inside TLS; so are secure connections to most
databases, message brokers and email servers.

## The obvious fixes, and why they break

The fastest way to understand why TLS looks the way it does is to try building it yourself and
watch each attempt fail.

### Attempt 1: encrypt everything with a shared key

The first idea is to put one secret key in both the app and the server, and encrypt every message
with it. The first problem is **key distribution**: the key now lives inside every copy of your
app, and anyone can pull an Android app apart with free tools. One extraction, and every user's
traffic is readable, past and future.

The second problem survives even a perfectly hidden key. **Encryption hides data; it does not
protect it from being changed.** Many ciphers mix the plaintext with a stream of key-derived bytes
using XOR, so flipping a bit in the ciphertext flips the same bit in the decrypted plaintext. An
attacker who knows your message format can edit it without the key:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="bit_flip.py (excerpt)"
# runnable version: examples/tls/python/bit_flip.py
def attacker_rewrite(ciphertext: bytes, offset: int, known: bytes, wanted: bytes) -> bytes:
    """No key needed. ciphertext = plaintext XOR stream, so XOR in (known XOR wanted)."""
    patch = xor(known, wanted)
    edited = bytearray(ciphertext)
    for i, p in enumerate(patch):
        edited[offset + i] ^= p
    return bytes(edited)
```

```js title="bit-flip.mjs (excerpt)"
/** No key needed. ciphertext = plaintext XOR stream, so XOR in (known XOR wanted). */
function attackerRewrite(ciphertext, offset, known, wanted) {
```

```text title="bit_flip.py output (node bit-flip.mjs prints the same)"

   original: {"to": "ravi@okbank", "amount_inr": 100}
```

A payment of ₹100 became ₹900, and the bank had no way to notice. The demo's cipher is a toy built
from SHA-256, but the same attack works on AES in counter mode (CTR) and, with more effort, in CBC
mode, whenever they are used without an integrity check. The second half of the output is the
repair: a MAC over the ciphertext with a second key catches the edit at once. So encryption needs
a partner: **integrity**.

### Attempt 2: agree on a fresh key for every connection

To avoid baking a key into the app, we can use an idea from 1976 called **Diffie-Hellman key
exchange**: two parties who have never met each send one public number and each end up with the
same secret, while anyone who only *listens* learns nothing useful. We will see how in the next
section.

But "anyone who only listens" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The person running the fake
café hotspot does not only listen; she sits in the middle and can answer for either side:

Alice's key and the bank's key are different. If they could compare fingerprints over a channel
Mallory cannot touch, they would catch her at once. That is what SSH asks you to do the first time
you connect to a server, and what messaging apps call a safety number. It needs a human and a second
channel, which is hopeless for a billion browsers and a million websites.

### What we actually need

So the bank must *prove* that the key share came from the bank, by signing it (really, the whole
conversation so far) with a private key only the bank has. Then you need to know that the matching
public key belongs to the bank and not to Mallory, which needs someone you already trust to vouch
for it. That vouching document is a **certificate**. Encryption plus integrity, a fresh key
exchange, and a signature tied to a vouched-for name: put them together and you have reinvented
TLS.

Here are the neighbours TLS is most often confused with.

| These are often confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| **TLS** vs **HTTPS** | TLS protects a byte stream; HTTPS is HTTP carried inside it. Databases, gRPC and MQTT can all use TLS without any HTTP. |
| **Encryption** vs **integrity** vs **authentication** | Three separate promises. As the demos showed, any one without the others can be defeated. |
| **Certificate** vs **private key** | The certificate is public; anyone can download and present it. Proving you hold its private key is what counts. |
| **The padlock** vs **a safe site** | The padlock means the connection is private and the name is verified. Phishing sites get valid certificates too. |
| **TLS** vs **a VPN** | TLS protects one application's connection end to end. A VPN (WireGuard, IPsec) protects everything between two networks or devices, only up to where the tunnel ends. |

## The core idea

Think of crossing a border. The officer at the passport desk checks three things: that the
passport was issued by a government their country recognises, that it has not expired, and that
the face in front of them matches the photo. Only then are you let in, and from then on everything
you do is under that name.

TLS works the same way. The **certificate** is the passport: it ties a name, such as `example.com`,
to a public key. A **certificate authority** (CA) is the issuing government. Your device carries a
built-in list of CAs it recognises, the **trust store**. The face is the **private key**: the
server proves it owns the passport by signing something with it, live, during the connection. Once
the check passes, a fresh secret key is agreed that protects everything else.

The analogy breaks in places worth knowing:

- **Anyone can copy a certificate.** A certificate is public, and any server can send yours. What
  cannot be copied is the signature only your private key can make. The private key *is* the face.
- **Any recognised CA can issue for any name.** Your browser trusts over a hundred CAs, and any of
  them can technically issue a certificate for your bank's name. Certificate Transparency, below,
  exists because of that.
- **The check is mathematical, not human.** A TLS client either verifies every signature or refuses
  to continue, unless someone turns the check off with `verify=False`: the officer leaving the desk.

Put precisely: **TLS runs a handshake once per connection. The two sides use an ephemeral
Diffie-Hellman exchange to agree on fresh secret keys that never cross the network. The server sends
a certificate chain binding its name to a public key, and proves it holds the matching private key
by signing the whole handshake. After that, every message travels as a record encrypted and
authenticated with those keys, so it cannot be read, changed, reordered or replayed unnoticed.**

Look at what happens once, and what happens on every message.

<Figure caption="A TLS connection has two phases. The handshake agrees on keys and proves the server's identity, once. Then every message travels as an encrypted record with an integrity tag. Each of the three promises comes from a different part.">
  <Overview />
</Figure>

The expensive, clever part is the handshake, and it happens once. The records, where your data
lives, use fast symmetric cryptography. Slow asymmetric cryptography to set up a fast symmetric key
is the shape of nearly every secure protocol you will meet.

## How it works, piece by piece

We build TLS from the inside out: records, key exchange, certificates and the handshake, then the
production parts. Everything here was measured in October 2026, from a network in India, with
Python 3.14 and Node 24 (both on OpenSSL 3.5).

### Records: encryption and integrity in one step

Once keys are agreed, your data is cut into **records** of at most 16 KB. Each is protected by an
**AEAD** cipher (authenticated encryption with associated data): one algorithm that encrypts *and*
produces an integrity tag, so you cannot get one without the other. It is attempt 1's two halves
fused into one operation. TLS 1.3 allows only AEAD ciphers: in practice **AES-GCM**, which modern
processors accelerate in hardware, or **ChaCha20-Poly1305**, fast in software on phones without it.

<Figure caption="A TLS 1.3 record is a short readable header, the encrypted payload and a 16-byte tag. The tag makes any change, reordering, replay or deletion detectable; what still leaks is the size and timing of the records.">
  <Record />
</Figure>

Two details on that diagram matter.

- **Every record has an invisible sequence number.** The nonce, a number that must never repeat with
  the same key, comes from a counter both sides keep and never send. If an attacker drops, reorders
  or replays a record, the counters no longer match, the tag check fails, and the connection is torn
  down. TLS does not try to repair; it refuses.
- **Size and timing still leak.** "Encrypted" is not "invisible": an observer still sees you
  downloaded 3.2 MB at 9 PM.

The record layer is cheap: when Gmail moved everyone to HTTPS in 2010, it cost under 1% of
front-end CPU and no extra machines ([Langley, Modadugu and Chang, 2010](https://www.imperialviolet.org/2010/06/25/overclocking-ssl.html)).

### Key exchange: agreeing on a secret in public

**Diffie-Hellman** relies on a one-way operation: quick to compute and practically impossible to
undo. Here it is with numbers small enough to check on a calculator.

<Figure caption="Each side keeps a secret, sends a public number derived from it, and combines the other's number with its own secret. Both reach 2. Someone watching the wire saw 23, 5, 8 and 19, and cannot get 2 without one of the secrets.">
  <KeyExchange />
</Figure>

**The worked example.** Everyone agrees on a public prime p = 23 and base g = 5. You pick a secret
a = 6 and send A = 5⁶ mod 23. Since 5⁶ = 15,625 = 23 × 679 + 8, A = 8. The bank picks b = 15 and
sends B = 5¹⁵ mod 23 = 19. You compute 19⁶ mod 23 = 2; the bank computes 8¹⁵ mod 23 = 2. Both
calculated 5^(6×15) mod 23, in a different order. An eavesdropper has 23, 5, 8 and 19, and must find
6 from "5 to the what is 8, mod 23". Here you can try all 22 possibilities by hand; at real sizes
there is no known shortcut.

Real TLS uses the same idea on an elliptic curve called **X25519**, with 32-byte public shares. The
shared result is fed through a key derivation function (HKDF) with a hash of every handshake
message, producing separate keys for each direction and phase.

The word that matters most is **ephemeral**: both sides throw their secrets away after the
handshake. That gives TLS 1.3 **forward secrecy**: if someone records your traffic today and steals
the server's private key next year, they still cannot decrypt the recording. Older versions also
allowed "RSA key exchange", where the client encrypted the secret with the server's long-term key;
steal that key and every recorded session opens up. TLS 1.3 removed that option.

**The post-quantum change.** A large enough quantum computer, which does not exist yet, could undo
X25519, and anyone recording traffic now could decrypt it later. So browsers and servers have
started using a **hybrid** key exchange, **X25519MLKEM768**, which combines X25519 with ML-KEM, an
algorithm designed to resist quantum computers. If either part holds, the key is safe. It was
standardised in [RFC 10024](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc10024/) in August 2026, and its
client key share is 1,216 bytes instead of 32. Over 60% of clients reaching Cloudflare supported it
by February 2026, but only about 10% of origin servers
([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-origin-pq-key-transparency-aspa/)); our TLS 1.3
connections from Node 24 used it with no configuration.

### Certificates: binding a key to a name

Diffie-Hellman gives you a secret shared with *someone*. The certificate tells you who. Here is the
one `example.com` served on the day this page was written:

<Figure caption="A certificate binds names to a public key for a limited time, and an issuer signs it. The fields that clients check on every connection are in bold: the names, the dates, and the signature.">
  <Certificate />
</Figure>

- **Names.** Clients check the **Subject Alternative Name** (SAN) list. `*.example.com` covers
  `api.example.com` but not `v2.api.example.com` and not `example.com` itself, which is why both are
  listed.
- **Validity dates**, checked against the client's own clock. This one lives 90 days, typical in 2026.
- **The public key.** ECDSA P-256 here; RSA 2048 is also common. The private key never appears.
- **Usage restrictions.** "May be used as a server, not as a CA" stops a website's owner from
  issuing certificates for other names.
- **The issuer's signature** over all of the above.

During the handshake the server sends its certificate and then signs the transcript so far,
including both key shares, with its private key. Mallory can send `example.com`'s certificate like
anyone else, but she cannot produce that signature, and because it covers the key shares, she
cannot swap them either. That is the point where the man-in-the-middle attack dies.

### The chain of trust

Root CAs do not usually sign website certificates directly. They sign **intermediate** CA
certificates, kept online for daily issuing, while the root's private key stays offline. So a client
receives a chain:

<Figure caption="The server sends its own certificate and the intermediate. The root is already in your device's trust store. The client checks each signature upwards and trusts the leaf only if the chain ends at a root it already holds.">
  <Chain />
</Figure>

As of September 2026, Mozilla's trust store, which Firefox, curl and most Linux distributions use,
held **121 root certificates** trusted for websites
([curl's extract of Mozilla's list](https://curl.se/docs/caextract.html)). Apple, Microsoft, Google
and Java keep their own, broadly similar lists. For every certificate in the chain, the client checks:

1. The signature verifies against the issuer's public key.
2. Today's date is inside the validity period.
3. Each certificate is allowed to do what it is doing: intermediates must be marked as CAs; the leaf
   must be allowed for server authentication.
4. The chain ends at a certificate in the trust store.
5. The leaf's names include the host name you asked for.

The server must send the intermediates. If it sends only its own certificate, the client cannot
build the chain and fails with exactly the error in our story. Browsers often hide this mistake,
because they cache intermediates and can download missing ones; Python, Node, curl and Java do not.
A chain that "works in Chrome but fails in Python" is almost always a missing intermediate.

### When a CA gets it wrong: transparency and revocation

The weakest point is the one the analogy flagged: any of those 121 roots, or any intermediate under
them, can issue for any name. In 2011 attackers broke into DigiNotar, a Dutch CA, and issued more
than 500 rogue certificates, including one for `*.google.com` that was used to intercept Gmail
users, almost all in Iran: around 300,000 distinct IP addresses
([Fox-IT interim report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1044777/000119312511241796/dex992.htm)).
Browsers removed DigiNotar from their trust stores, and it went bankrupt within weeks.

**Certificate Transparency** (CT, [RFC 6962](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962)) makes
mis-issuance *visible*. CAs must submit every certificate to public, append-only logs, which return
signed timestamps (SCTs) embedded in the certificate; Chrome requires SCTs from at least two log
operators ([Chrome CT policy](https://googlechrome.github.io/CertificateTransparency/ct_policy.html)).
Anyone can search the logs for certificates issued for their own domains. Try yours at
[crt.sh](https://crt.sh/).

**Revocation**, cancelling a certificate before it expires, has never worked well. OCSP, where the
client asks the CA about each certificate, leaks every site you visit, adds a network call, and
browsers mostly ignored its failures. Let's Encrypt, the largest CA, stopped running OCSP in August
2025 ([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2024/12/05/ending-ocsp/)). The industry's real answer
is **short lifetimes**: a stolen key for a 47-day certificate is useful for at most 47 days.

Two things in that sequence matter in production.

- **The guess.** The client sends key shares before it knows what the server supports. If it
  guessed wrong, the server replies with a **HelloRetryRequest** and the handshake costs an extra
  round trip. That is why clients send a classic X25519 share alongside the post-quantum one.
- **Downgrade protection.** An attacker who edits the ClientHello to force an older version is
  caught twice: the Finished MAC covers every message, and a TLS 1.3 server pushed into TLS 1.2
  plants a marker in its random value that a TLS 1.3 client checks. (A TLS 1.3 ClientHello also
  claims to be TLS 1.2 on the wire, to get past middleboxes; the [TCP vs UDP](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/)
  page explains that kind of ossification.)

### Resumption and 0-RTT

A returning client can skip much of the work. **Resumption** sends the ticket from the last
handshake in the next ClientHello; both sides derive keys from the secret they already share,
usually mixed with a fresh Diffie-Hellman exchange to keep forward secrecy. No certificate is sent
or checked. It still takes one round trip, with less data and work.

**0-RTT** ("zero round trip") goes further: using keys from the ticket, the client sends its first
request *inside* the first flight, as **early data**. For a user 200 ms away, the request arrives
200 ms sooner.

<Figure caption="How many round trips TLS adds after TCP's own. TLS 1.3 halves TLS 1.2's cost; resumption keeps one round trip but skips the certificate; 0-RTT removes the wait entirely, at the price of replay.">
  <HandshakeRtts />
</Figure>

We measured this against `www.cloudflare.com`, one 14.5 ms round trip away, with `handshake.py` and
`handshake.mjs` from the examples folder (median of 7 rounds). A full TLS 1.2 handshake took
31.2 ms (2.1 round trips), a full TLS 1.3 handshake 21.7 ms (1.5) and a resumed one 13.0 ms (0.9).
The extra half round trip in the full TLS 1.3 case is most likely computation: the certificate chain
and the larger post-quantum key exchange, both of which resumption skips.

0-RTT has a real price, and it is why it is off by default almost everywhere: **early data can be
replayed**. Its keys come from the old ticket, not a fresh exchange, so nothing stops an attacker
copying the first flight and sending it again.

The practical defences:

- **Only accept safe requests early.** As of October 2026, Cloudflare's 0-RTT accepts only `GET`,
  `HEAD` and `OPTIONS` in early data and adds an `Early-Data: 1` header for your origin
  ([Cloudflare docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/speed/optimization/protocol/0-rtt-connection-resumption/)).
- **Let the application refuse.** [RFC 8470](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8470) defines
  `425 Too Early`: the client sends the request again once the handshake is complete.
- **Make the operation idempotent anyway**, with an idempotency key, so a replay from 0-RTT, a retry
  or a double-click is harmless.

### Versions and the attacks that shaped them

Every change in TLS has a broken thing behind it: BEAST (2011) broke old CBC encryption, CRIME
(2012) leaked secrets through compression, POODLE (2014) and Logjam (2015) forced downgrades to weak
options, and ROBOT (2017) revived a 1998 attack on RSA key exchange
([RFC 7457](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7457), [weakdh.org](https://weakdh.org/),
[robotattack.org](https://robotattack.org/)). Heartbleed (2014), an OpenSSL bug that leaked server
memory and private keys, showed that implementations fail too ([heartbleed.com](https://heartbleed.com/)).

TLS 1.3's designers drew one lesson: **remove options, not add them**. It has no RSA key exchange,
no CBC, no compression and no renegotiation, and three cipher suites in practical use, all AEAD. As of
October 2026, SSL 2.0 and 3.0 are dead, TLS 1.0 and 1.1 were deprecated by
[RFC 8996](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8996) in 2021, TLS 1.2 is still acceptable with only
ECDHE key exchange and AEAD ciphers, and TLS 1.3 is the default everywhere modern.

### Mutual TLS: when the server asks who you are

So far only the server is authenticated; the client proves who it is later, with a password, cookie
or token. Between your own services that is often not enough: a token can be stolen and replayed
from anywhere, and a network location is not an identity. In **mutual TLS** (mTLS) the server asks
for a certificate too.

The identity is usually a URI in the SAN field following a convention called **SPIFFE**, such as
`spiffe://shop.internal/orders`. The most common mistake with mTLS: **a valid client certificate
proves who is calling, not what they may do**. "Any service with a certificate from our CA can call
any endpoint" is a policy, and usually the wrong one.

At scale, a **service mesh** such as Istio or Linkerd puts a small proxy next to each service, gives
each workload a certificate automatically and rotates it constantly. As of October 2026, Istio's
default workload certificate lives 24 hours, its private key generated inside the pod; a `PERMISSIVE`
mode accepts plain text and mTLS during migration, and `STRICT` only mTLS
([Istio security concepts](https://istio.io/latest/docs/concepts/security/)).

### Where TLS ends inside your system

The security engineer's second finding was about a question most TLS diagrams skip: *where does the
encryption stop?* A request may cross a CDN, a load balancer, an API gateway and a sidecar before
it reaches your code, and each may decrypt it. Decrypting at a proxy is called **TLS termination**.

<Figure caption="TLS can end at the edge, be re-encrypted to the service, pass through untouched, or be replaced by mutual TLS between services. Only the first leaves plain text inside your network.">
  <Termination />
</Figure>

- **Terminate at the edge.** Plain HTTP travels to your services. Simple, and exactly the "SSL added
  and removed here" design: anyone on any machine inside can read and change everything, including
  passwords and tokens in headers.
- **Re-encrypt.** The proxy decrypts, does its job (path routing, a web application firewall,
  headers), then opens a new, *verified* TLS connection to the service.
- **Passthrough.** The load balancer forwards encrypted bytes, routing on the TCP connection or the
  SNI name. It cannot route on paths, but the private key lives only on your service.
- **A mesh with mutual TLS.** Decrypted at the edge, then every hop between services is mTLS:
  encrypted everywhere, every caller identified.

Cloud providers encrypt some traffic for you: AWS encrypts links between its zones and regions, and
some instance types encrypt instance-to-instance traffic in hardware. Its documentation still makes
TLS your responsibility
([AWS EC2 data protection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/data-protection.html)),
because encryption on a cable does not stop a compromised machine on the same network, and it
authenticates nobody.

:::note[From the trenches]
"It's an internal network" is the most common reason teams give for plain HTTP between services,
and it does not survive how breaches unfold. Attackers rarely break in at the service they want;
they land on a forgotten build server or a developer laptop and move sideways, reading internal
traffic as they go. In practice I would start a new system with TLS on every hop and make plain text
the exception that needs a written reason.
:::

As of October 2026, Let's Encrypt issues 90-day certificates by default, offers 6-day ones, and
moves its default to 64 days on 10 February 2027 and 45 days in February 2028
([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2026/10/07/64-day-certs)). It recommends renewing at about
two-thirds of the lifetime, ideally via **ARI** (ACME Renewal Information), which lets the CA tell
your client when to renew. It also stopped sending expiry reminder emails in June 2025
([Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/2025/06/26/expiration-notification-service-has-ended/)):
if your plan was "we'll get an email", you no longer have a plan.

Why renew at two-thirds rather than at the last moment? Watch six certificates over two years,
renewed by hand when a reminder arrives a month before expiry; now and then a reminder goes to
someone who has left.

<CertLifecycleSim mode="manual" />

Most renewals happen quietly inside the amber band. A missed reminder gives no warning at all: the
certificate stays green until the day it turns red, with every client failing. In our run, five of 22 renewals were
missed, for 10 days of outage. Drag the lifetime down to 47 days and the same team does 109 renewals
by hand, with 28 days of outage.

Now the same certificates with automation that renews at two-thirds of the lifetime and retries
daily. Automation breaks too, for boring reasons: a firewall change blocks the challenge, a DNS API
token expires, a server is rebuilt without the renewal job. Each red cross is one of those.

<CertLifecycleSim mode="automated" />

With 90-day certificates, an alert at 14 days left and three days for a human to fix things, eight
breakages happened in two years and none caused an outage. Each shows as a short amber band: renewal
overdue, alert, fix, renewed with days to spare. **The renewal window is the time you give humans to
notice and fix broken automation.** Set the alert to 0 and those eight breakages become 24 days of
outage. Drag the lifetime to 6 days and the window shrinks to 2 days, so a 3-day fix arrives after
expiry, alert or no alert. Short lifetimes limit the damage of a stolen key, and they demand fast,
reliable automation and fast humans.

## Choosing between them

Most TLS decisions come down to three questions: where should encryption end, how strict should the
configuration be, and how should certificates be renewed?

| | Edge only | Re-encrypt | Passthrough | Mesh mTLS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plain text inside the network | yes | only inside the proxy | no | no |
| Proxy can route on paths and headers | yes | yes | no, only on SNI | yes |
| Where private keys live | edge | edge and services | services only | edge, plus a short-lived key per service |
| Callers identified cryptographically | no | no | no | yes |
| Operational cost | lowest | moderate | moderate | highest, unless a mesh or platform does it |
| Typical use | legacy systems | most web apps behind a managed load balancer | databases, end-to-end requirements | microservices at scale, zero-trust networks |

For configuration, Mozilla publishes profiles most tools follow
([Mozilla server-side TLS, guidelines version 6.0](https://ssl-config.mozilla.org/)): **Modern** is
TLS 1.3 only, for when you control the clients; **Intermediate** adds TLS 1.2 with ECDHE and AEAD
only, and is the right default for public websites and APIs.

For renewal, here are three strategies on exactly the same two years of bad luck:

<CertLifecycleSim mode="compare" />

In our run, the manual panel lost 9 days while someone renewed 23 certificates by hand. The 90-day
automated panel absorbed every breakage, because a third of 90 days is a month of slack. The 6-day
panel lost 18 days, because a three-day fix cannot fit into a two-day window. Press Reset with
different sliders; the numbers change, the shape does not.

**How to choose.** In practice:

1. **Encrypt every hop by default.** Re-encrypt behind a managed load balancer for a typical web app;
   move to a mesh with mTLS once you have many services.
2. **Use the intermediate profile for anything public**, modern where you control every client.
3. **Automate every certificate**, renew at two-thirds of its lifetime, and alert on "renewal
   overdue", not only "expires soon". 90-day certificates with good alerting are a comfortable
   default in 2026.
4. **Never turn off verification** to fix an error. Find which of the five checks failed and fix that.

### Step 2: mutual TLS with identity-based authorization

A payments service that requires a client certificate, reads the caller's SPIFFE ID from it, and

```python title="mtls.py (excerpt)"
ALLOWED = {
    "spiffe://shop.internal/orders": {"POST /charge"},
    "spiffe://shop.internal/reports": {"GET /summary"},

def caller_identity(cert: dict) -> str | None:
    """The URI in the certificate's Subject Alternative Name, e.g. spiffe://shop.internal/orders."""
    return next((value for kind, value in cert.get("subjectAltName", ()) if kind == "URI"), None)
```

## How real systems do it

**Let's Encrypt made automation normal.** It launched in 2015 with free 90-day certificates issued
only through ACME, and by late 2025 it was often issuing ten million a day. Its move to 64 and then
45 days, and its end of OCSP and expiry emails, are pushing every CA and hosting platform towards
automatic renewal, as of October 2026
([Let's Encrypt lifetimes](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/cert-lifetimes/)).

**CDNs make the termination choice explicit.** Cloudflare's SSL modes map onto this page's patterns:
*Flexible* is HTTPS to the visitor but plain HTTP to your origin; *Full* is HTTPS to the origin
without checking its certificate; *Full (strict)* verifies it
([Cloudflare SSL modes](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/origin-configuration/ssl-modes/)). Only
strict keeps the promises end to end. Flexible gives the visitor a padlock while the second half
travels in plain text, and Full is our `verify=False` on the back half.

**Cloud load balancers pick your TLS policy, and the default depends on how you create them.** As of
October 2026, an AWS Application Load Balancer created in the console defaults to a TLS 1.2 and 1.3
policy with post-quantum key exchange (`ELBSecurityPolicy-TLS13-1-2-Res-PQ-2025-09`), while one
created through the CLI or CloudFormation without a policy still gets the legacy
`ELBSecurityPolicy-2016-08`, which allows TLS 1.0
([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/describe-ssl-policies.html)).
Infrastructure code should always name the policy.

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A real breach: the certificate nobody renewed on the security tool]
In 2017, attackers stole personal data on 145.5 million Americans from Equifax. The US Government
Accountability Office found that a device inspecting encrypted traffic for attacks had an expired
certificate, about 10 months out of date, so encrypted traffic was not being inspected at all. The
attackers took data out over encrypted connections for about 76 days. When the certificate was
replaced on 29 July 2017, the suspicious traffic was seen almost immediately
([GAO-18-559](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-559)).
:::

An expired certificate on a monitoring or security tool pages nobody, because nothing user-facing
breaks. It fails silent and open. Monitor your internal tooling's certificates as carefully as your
public site's.

:::danger[A real outage: one expiry date, every region]
On 22 February 2013, Windows Azure Storage stopped serving HTTPS in every region for about 11 and a
half hours because its certificates expired. The renewed certificates were already in the secret
store, so the expiry alerts went quiet, but the release that would deploy them had been delayed
behind other work
([InfoQ summary of Microsoft's post-mortem](https://www.infoq.com/news/2013/03/feb22_azure_outage)).
:::

Monitor the certificate *being served*, not the one in your secret store. And identical
certificates with identical expiry dates in every region are a single point of failure that no
redundancy protects against.

:::caution[The Friday fix, explained]
Back to our story. The bank had renewed its certificate that Friday and configured its server to
send only the new leaf, without the intermediate. Chrome filled the gap from its cache; Python did
not. The right fix was a one-line change on the bank's server or, while waiting, adding the bank's
intermediate to your client's trust bundle for that one host. `verify=False` "fixed" it by making
your service willing to send money instructions to anyone who could intercept the connection. If you
find it in a codebase, treat it as a security incident, not a code smell.
:::

**Renewed on disk, old one served.** ACME clients renew files, but web servers, proxies and Java
applications often read certificates only at startup. The file is fresh, the certificate in memory
expires. Reload after renewal, and monitor what is served, as in Step 3.

**Corporate inspection proxies break clients.** Many companies decrypt employees' traffic with a
proxy that issues certificates from a company root installed on managed laptops. Browsers trust it;
Python, Node, Java and Docker images carry their own trust stores and do not. "Certificate verify
failed" at work and not at home is usually this. Add the company root to the tool's trust bundle;
do not disable verification.

:::caution[Flexible TLS and redirect loops]
A CDN in Cloudflare's Flexible mode talks plain HTTP to your origin. If your origin redirects HTTP to
HTTPS, as it should, every request bounces until the browser gives up with "too many redirects".
Teams often "fix" it by removing the redirect. The real fix is a certificate on the origin and Full
(strict) at the CDN.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**What TLS costs.** One round trip for a new connection in TLS 1.3, a few milliseconds of
computation, and CPU small enough to be invisible. The real cost is operations: certificates to
issue, renew, deploy, reload and monitor; private keys to protect; trust stores to keep updated; and
a whole class of outages, expired certificates, that plain text never has.

**What TLS does not solve.**

- **The endpoints.** If either machine is compromised, the attacker reads everything after
  decryption.
- **Data at rest.** Once data arrives, it is plaintext again, in memory, logs and the database.
- **Authorization.** Even mutual TLS only tells you who is calling.
- **Trustworthiness.** A valid certificate proves control of a domain name. Phishing sites have them.
- **Metadata.** Who talks to whom, when and how much is still visible, and so is the site name in
  the ClientHello unless both sides support Encrypted Client Hello.

**When not to use it, or not alone.** Communication inside one machine over a Unix domain socket
gains little from it. When a whole network link must be protected, including protocols that cannot
speak TLS, a VPN or WireGuard tunnel may be the better layer, as a complement. And never build your
own replacement: every attempt on this page was a real design someone once trusted. Use the standard
library's TLS, a well-known proxy, or your platform's mesh.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their TLS posture in one of two ways: a certificate expires on a Saturday, or
an auditor asks where encryption stops. Both are cheaper to answer on a quiet Tuesday. Keep an
inventory of every endpoint, who owns its certificate, how it renews and where its TLS terminates,
and point the Step 3 monitor at it. That is an afternoon of work, and the most outage-preventing
afternoon in this whole topic.
:::

## Interview corner

TLS comes up as a fundamentals question and inside system design, when the interviewer asks where
to terminate HTTPS. A strong answer says which promise each part provides and what goes wrong
without it.

1. **"Walk me through a TLS handshake."** ClientHello with key shares, SNI and ALPN; ServerHello with
   the server's share; then everything is encrypted: certificate chain, CertificateVerify signature
   over the transcript, Finished; the client verifies chain, name, dates and signature; client
   Finished plus the first request. One round trip in TLS 1.3, two in 1.2. Bonus: forward secrecy
   and session tickets.
2. **"How does the client know it's talking to the real server?"** The chain ends at a trusted root,
   the name matches, and the CertificateVerify signature over this handshake's key shares proves
   the server holds the private key now. Mention Certificate Transparency.
3. **"Where would you terminate TLS in this design?"** Edge versus re-encryption versus passthrough
   versus mesh mTLS, routing ability versus plain text inside, and who owns certificate renewal.
4. **"What is 0-RTT and why is it dangerous?"** Early data sent with the ClientHello using keys from
   a previous session; it saves a round trip but can be replayed, so accept only requests safe to
   run twice, or answer `425 Too Early`.

In system design rounds, one sentence earns credit: "TLS terminates at the load balancer, which
re-encrypts to services, and services talk over mTLS issued by the mesh, rotated automatically."

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A service encrypts messages with AES in counter mode using a key nobody else has, but adds no MAC. What can an attacker on the network do, and what is the fix?</summary>

They cannot read the messages, but they can change them: flipping a bit in the ciphertext flips the
same bit in the plaintext, so with a known format they can change an amount or a recipient. The fix
is authenticated encryption, such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305; in practice, use TLS rather than
designing message protection yourself.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. An attacker records all traffic to your server today. Next year they steal the server's private key. Which sessions can they decrypt: TLS 1.3, TLS 1.2 with ECDHE, TLS 1.2 with RSA key exchange?</summary>

Only the TLS 1.2 sessions with RSA key exchange, where the session secret was encrypted with the
server's long-term key. The others used ephemeral Diffie-Hellman keys discarded after each
handshake: forward secrecy. The stolen key does let them impersonate the server from now on.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your Python service fails with "unable to get local issuer certificate" when calling a partner's API, which opens fine in Chrome. What is the most likely cause, and what should you not do?</summary>

The partner's server most likely sends its certificate without the intermediate; Chrome fills the
gap, Python does not. Check with `openssl s_client -showcerts`, ask the partner to send the full
chain, and meanwhile add their intermediate to your trust bundle for that host. Do not set
`verify=False`: the same message appears for a genuine impostor, as Step 1 showed.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your automation renews 90-day certificates with 30 days left and retries daily; an alert fires at 14 days left, and fixing broken automation takes 3 days. What changes with 6-day certificates and the same renew-at-two-thirds rule?</summary>

The renewal window shrinks from 30 days to 2. With 90-day certificates a breakage leaves 14 days
between alert and expiry, plenty for a 3-day fix. With 6-day certificates the fix arrives a day after
expiry, so every breakage becomes an outage unless you can respond within hours.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A load balancer terminates TLS and forwards plain HTTP to services. A colleague says that is fine because the services are in a private subnet. Give two concrete risks.</summary>

Anyone who compromises any machine in that network can read passwords, tokens and personal data in
every request, and change requests in flight. And nothing authenticates callers: any process that
can reach a service can call it while pretending to be the load balancer. Re-encrypt from the load
balancer, or use mTLS between services.

</details>

## Recap

- **TLS makes three promises**: confidentiality (encryption), integrity (a tag on every record) and
  authentication (a certificate plus a live signature). Any one without the others can be defeated.
- **The handshake** uses ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (now often hybrid post-quantum X25519MLKEM768) for
  fresh keys with forward secrecy, and a CertificateVerify signature over the transcript to prove the
  server's identity. One round trip in TLS 1.3, two in 1.2.
- **A certificate is public; the private key is the secret.** Clients check the chain to a trusted
  root, the names, the dates and the signature. Servers must send the intermediates.
- **Certificate Transparency** makes mis-issuance visible; revocation barely works; **short
  lifetimes** are the real answer, falling to 47 days by 2029.
- **0-RTT saves a round trip but can be replayed**; **mTLS gives services identities**, and
  authorization is still your job.
- **Know where TLS ends** and encrypt every hop. Automate every certificate, renew at two-thirds of
  its lifetime, monitor what is served, and never ship `verify=False`.
