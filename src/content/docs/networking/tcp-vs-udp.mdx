## The question

A client sends each request to a server 0.1 ms away as two writes, a header and a body, and every
request takes 44 ms. A 1 MB download on a fresh connection to a server 100 ms away takes about 700 ms on a 50 Mbit/s
line and about 700 ms on a 1 Gbit/s line. A voice call over TCP on a link that drops 4% of packets
misses its deadline on 17% of its audio frames. And a service that opens 500 new connections a
second to one backend starts failing with "cannot assign requested address" after about a minute.

None of these is a bug. Each is one of TCP's guarantees doing its job, and every guarantee is
enforced the same way: **somebody waits**, for a round trip, a timer or a missing packet. This page
finds each of those waits, puts a number on it, and works out what you take on when you choose UDP
to avoid them.

## What each one actually promises

**UDP** ([RFC 768](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc768)) adds three things to IP: **ports**, a
16-bit **checksum**, and **message boundaries**. A datagram arrives whole, at most once, or not at
all, in any order. Nothing in UDP knows how fast the receiver reads or how full the network is, and
`sendto()` returning tells you only that your own kernel took the bytes.

**TCP** ([RFC 9293](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293)) promises one thing, and the exact
wording matters: a **reliable, ordered stream of bytes between two kernels, or an error**. Read it
slowly and four consequences fall out, each of which this page measures:

1. **A stream of bytes, not messages.** Your writes are not preserved as units. Where one message
   ends is your problem.
2. **Ordered means a hole blocks everything behind it.** Bytes that arrived are held until the
   missing ones are resent.
3. **Reliable means retried, by timers.** Those timers are measured in hundreds of milliseconds,
   and TCP keeps retrying for minutes before it reports the error.
4. **The sender is throttled** by two windows: the receiver's free buffer (**flow control**) and
   the sender's estimate of what the network can take (**congestion control**). On a new connection
   that estimate starts small.

"Between two kernels" is the part people miss. When the peer's kernel acknowledges your bytes,
your side is done; if the peer's process crashes before it reads them, they are gone and you were
told "success". Only an application-level reply can confirm the application acted, which is why
payment APIs add their own acknowledgements and idempotency keys on top of TCP.

## What people assume first, and how it breaks

Two beliefs cause most transport bugs. The first is that **one send on one side is one receive on
the other.** Here is a server that parses each read on a TCP socket as one JSON message. The client
sends five small score updates and one 220 KB leaderboard, while the server is busy for 50 ms:

The second belief is that **UDP is TCP minus the overhead.** `udp_loss.py` sends 5,000 numbered
1,000-byte datagrams from one process to another on the same machine, no network involved, to a
receiver that spends 0.2 ms on each:


98% vanished, with no error on either side: the receiving socket's buffer filled and the kernel
dropped the rest. TCP would have slowed the sender with its receive window. UDP did not remove that
overhead; it handed you the job.

## The core idea

Every TCP guarantee is a wait with a known size:

| Guarantee | Enforced by waiting for | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Both sides agree to talk | the handshake | 1 round trip per new connection |
| Don't flood the network | the congestion window to grow | 1 round trip per doubling, from 14.6 KB |
| Nothing lost | a duplicate ACK or a timer | 1 round trip, or 200 ms to seconds |
| In order | the missing segment | everything behind a loss waits too |
| Fewer tiny packets | an ACK (Nagle) that the peer delays | up to 40 ms on Linux, 200 ms elsewhere |
| No stale packets in a new connection | TIME-WAIT | the port, for 60 s on Linux |

UDP waits for none of these and guarantees none of them. So the real choice is never "reliable or
fast". It is: **which of these waits can your data afford, and which guarantees are you willing to
rebuild yourself?**

away, on a 50 Mbit/s link.

## How it works, piece by piece

### A byte stream, so you frame your own messages

TCP's specification says outright that there is no link between how the application writes and
how segments are cut ([RFC 9293 §3.7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293)). The sender's kernel
may merge small writes into one segment and must split a large write into segments of about 1,460
bytes (the **MSS**, maximum segment size: a 1,500-byte Ethernet packet minus 40 bytes of IP and TCP
headers). The receiver's kernel appends every arriving byte to one buffer, and a read returns
whatever is there.

<Figure caption="The application writes three messages. TCP delivers the same bytes in whatever chunks happen to be ready, so a read can hold one and a half messages. UDP keeps each datagram whole. With a length field before each message, a TCP reader can find the boundaries again.">
  <Framing />
</Figure>

Every protocol on TCP marks its own boundaries, in one of three ways:

- **A length prefix**: DNS over TCP uses 2 bytes, HTTP/2 a 24-bit length on every frame
  ([RFC 9113](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113)). The most robust choice; we build it below.
- **A delimiter**: a byte that cannot appear inside a message, such as the newline in
  newline-delimited JSON or the blank line that ends HTTP/1.1 headers.
- **A library that does it**: WebSocket, gRPC and database drivers frame for you. The bug appears
  when someone opens a raw socket beside them.

A length prefix needs one more defence: the length comes from the network. Without a maximum, one
corrupt or hostile header claiming 4 GB makes your server try to allocate 4 GB.

UDP has the opposite problem. A datagram keeps its boundary, but it has a size limit: 65,507 bytes
of payload over IPv4, and in practice much less, because anything larger than the path's **MTU**
(1,500 bytes on most links) is split into IP fragments, and losing any fragment loses the whole
datagram. Many firewalls drop fragments outright. The largest unfragmented payload on Ethernet is
1,500 − 20 − 8 = 1,472 bytes, and QUIC stays at or below about 1,200, the size it assumes every
path carries.

### Loss repair: fast retransmit, timers, and the one-second spike

TCP numbers every byte, and each ACK names the next byte the receiver expects: "ACK 5001" means
"I have everything up to 5,000". When a segment in the middle of a busy stream is lost, the segments
after it still arrive, and each one triggers the same ACK again. Three of these **duplicate ACKs**
tell the sender "lost, not late", and it resends at once: **fast retransmit**, roughly one round
trip after the loss. With **SACK** (selective acknowledgement, on by default everywhere) the ACKs
also say exactly which later ranges arrived, so only the hole is resent.

Fast retransmit needs later segments to produce those duplicate ACKs. When the lost segment is the
last of a burst, or the connection is quiet, nothing comes back, and only the **retransmission
timeout** (RTO) is left. [RFC 6298](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298) sets it from a smoothed
round-trip time and its variation:

> RTO = SRTT + 4 × RTTVAR

Three rules surround it. Before any measurement, the RTO is **1 second**. It never drops below a
floor: the RFC asks for 1 s, Linux uses **200 ms**. And it **doubles after every expiry**, so a dead
path is not flooded. A retransmitted segment is never used to measure the round trip, because you
cannot tell which copy the ACK was for (Karn's rule).

**A worked example.** A steady 100 ms path has almost no variation, so Linux's RTO is about
100 + 200 = **300 ms**. The last segment of a response is lost: the sender waits 300 ms and
resends. If that copy is lost too, it waits 600 ms, then 1.2 s. One unlucky segment has cost over
two seconds on a path where a round trip is a tenth of a second. Linux softens the first case with
a **tail loss probe** that resends after about two round trips instead of the full RTO
([RFC 8985](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8985)), but repeated losses still back off.

A lost **SYN** is worse, because there is no measurement yet: the client waits the initial **1
second**. On older Linux kernels the SYN retries then double (1, 2, 4 s), which is why latency
graphs so often show a cluster of requests at almost exactly 1 s and 3 s above normal. Since 2023
(Linux 6.5), the first four SYN retries are spaced 1 s apart instead
(`net.ipv4.tcp_syn_linear_timeouts = 4`, as of October 2026,
[kernel docs](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/ip-sysctl.html)), so the clusters sit at 1, 2, 3 and
4 s. Either way, a latency that is a suspiciously round number of seconds is almost always a timer.

TCP is also patient past the point of usefulness. Linux keeps retrying a `connect()` for about
2 minutes and data on an open connection for about 15 minutes (`tcp_retries2 = 15`) before it
reports an error. Your application needs its own, much shorter timeouts; `TCP_USER_TIMEOUT` caps
the kernel's patience per socket.

### Head-of-line blocking: what "in order" costs

When a segment is lost, the segments behind it arrive and wait in the receiver's buffer. The
application cannot have byte 2,001 until it has byte 2,000. This is **head-of-line blocking**, and
it turns a single loss into a stall for everything sent in the next round trip or so.

For a file download that stall is a rounding error. For anything with a deadline it is the whole
problem. The simulation sends 50 small messages a second, one every 20 ms like voice frames, over a
link with a 100 ms round trip and 4% loss. Each bar is one message's delay from send to the
application; the dashed line is 150 ms, roughly the most one-way delay a conversation tolerates
before it feels awkward ([ITU-T G.114](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-G.114)). Press "Lose the
next packet" to make a loss happen on demand.

<TransportSim mode="tcp" />

Every loss looks the same: one tall bar for the lost message, then a descending staircase of bars
for messages that had already arrived and were held behind the gap, all released at the same
instant. That staircase is the "freeze, then fast-forward" of a voice call over TCP.

**The staircase in numbers.** Message *n* is lost at time 0. Messages *n*+1, *n*+2 and *n*+3 are
sent at 20, 40 and 60 ms and arrive 50 ms later; the third duplicate ACK reaches the sender at
60 + 100 = **160 ms**, and the resend arrives at **210 ms**. Messages *n*+1 to *n*+7 arrived
between 70 and 190 ms and are all released at 210 ms. So *n* is 210 ms late, *n*+1 190 ms,
*n*+2 170 ms and *n*+3 exactly 150 ms: one loss pushes three messages past the deadline and drags
four more towards it. Over a long run at 4% loss, the model shows **17%** of messages late, four
times the loss rate, because now and then a resend is lost too and only the timer recovers it. At
a 200 ms round trip it is 42%.

UDP on the same link has no staircase: every message that arrives is 50 ms old, and 4% never
arrive. For voice that is the better trade, because a codec can hide a missing 20 ms frame and
nobody can hide a 200 ms freeze.

**HTTP/2 inherits this.** HTTP/2 multiplexes many requests as streams over one TCP connection,
which fixed HTTP/1.1's queueing at the HTTP layer and moved it down a level. The streams are
independent to HTTP, but they share one TCP byte sequence, so one lost segment stalls every stream
on the connection, including ones whose data all arrived. On a lossy mobile link, a browser's six
HTTP/1.1 connections can beat one HTTP/2 connection, because a loss on one of six stalls only a
sixth of the traffic. That is the problem QUIC was built to remove, and we come back to it with
the same simulation below.

### Flow control: the receive window and the bandwidth-delay product

Every ACK also carries the **receive window**: how many more bytes the receiver's buffer can take.
The sender may have at most that many bytes sent but not yet acknowledged. If the receiving
application stops reading, the window falls to zero and the sender stops without losing a byte.
That is TCP's built-in **backpressure**, and it is why the UDP demo above lost 98% while a TCP
version would have lost nothing.

The window also caps throughput at **one window per round trip**, however fast the link. To keep a
100 Mbit/s path 100 ms long busy, you need 100,000,000 ÷ 8 × 0.1 = **1.25 MB** in flight at all
times. That product of bandwidth and round-trip time is the **bandwidth-delay product** (BDP),
the size of the "pipe". The original 16-bit window field allows 65,535 bytes, which caps that path
at 65,535 × 8 ÷ 0.1 ≈ **5.2 Mbit/s**. The window scale option
([RFC 7323](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323)) lifts the limit to about 1 GB, and Linux grows
socket buffers automatically up to `tcp_rmem`'s maximum. When a transfer between two distant data
centres is slow on a fast link and nothing is lost, a buffer that an application or a sysctl
capped is the first suspect.

### Congestion control: slow start in numbers

The receive window protects the receiver. Nothing in it protects the routers in between, and in
October 1986 that nearly stopped the internet: throughput between two Berkeley sites fell by a
factor of about a thousand because the network was full of retransmissions of packets dropped
because the network was full ([Jacobson and Karels, 1988](https://ee.lbl.gov/papers/congavoid.pdf)).
Their fix is still the shape of every TCP.

The sender keeps a second limit, the **congestion window** (cwnd): its own guess at how much the
path can hold. It may have in flight only the smaller of cwnd and the receive window.

- **Slow start.** A new connection starts at 10 segments, about 14.6 KB
  ([RFC 6928](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6928)), and doubles cwnd every round trip in which
  everything is acknowledged. A response of *S* bytes on a new connection therefore needs about
  log₂(*S* ÷ 14.6 KB + 1) round trips: 3 for 100 KB, 7 for 1 MB, 10 for 10 MB, **whatever the
  bandwidth**.
- **A loss means congestion.** The sender assumes a queue overflowed and cuts cwnd: to 70% under
  **CUBIC** ([RFC 9438](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438)), the default in Linux, Windows and
  Apple's stacks, or to 50% under the older Reno.
- **Then it probes again.** CUBIC climbs back along a cubic curve: fast at first, flat near the
  window where the last loss happened, then faster again to look for more.
- **Idle resets it.** If a connection sits idle for longer than one RTO, Linux shrinks cwnd back
  towards 10 segments before sending again, because what it learned about the path may be stale
  (`tcp_slow_start_after_idle`, on by default, from [RFC 2861](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2861)).

Here is one connection sending the same response again and again. Each bar is one round trip: how
many segments the sender had in flight. The dashed line is the pipe, the BDP; the bottleneck can
queue one more pipe's worth, and anything beyond that is dropped.

<CwndSim />

The defaults are a 1 MB response, 100 ms away, on 50 Mbit/s. The first response climbs 10, 20, 40,
80, 160, 320 and finishes in the seventh round trip, **713 ms**. The link alone needs 260 ms (one
round trip plus 164 ms of bytes), which is what the second response, on the now warm connection,
takes. Slide the bandwidth to 1,000 Mbit/s and the first response takes 701 ms: twenty times the
bandwidth bought 12 ms. On a new connection, round-trip time is the only number that matters.

Then slide the idle gap to 1,000 ms, longer than the 300 ms RTO. The window halves three times
during the gap, falls from 640 segments to 80, and every warm response takes 429 ms instead of 260.
This is the trap of "we reuse connections, so slow start is not our problem": a connection that
idles for a second between bursts pays part of slow start again on every burst. Servers that hold
long-lived connections (HTTP/2, gRPC, database proxies) often turn `tcp_slow_start_after_idle` off
for exactly this reason.

**Loss caps throughput, not bandwidth.** Set the response to 5,000 KB and random loss to 1%. The
window never gets near the pipe: each loss cuts it, and it climbs back by only a few segments per
round trip. The sim settles at about **1.6 Mbit/s on a 50 Mbit/s link**. The classic model of this
is the Mathis formula: a loss-based TCP's long-run rate is about (MSS ÷ RTT) × 1.22 ÷ √*p*, where *p*
is the loss rate ([Mathis et al., 1997](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/fall24/compsci514/readings/mathis-tcpmodel-ccr97.pdf)).
At 1% loss and 100 ms, that is 1,460 × 8 ÷ 0.1 × 1.22 ÷ 0.1 ≈ **1.4 Mbit/s** for Reno, whatever the
link. Halve the round trip and the rate doubles; cut the loss to 0.1% and it rises about three
times. This is why a long-distance transfer over a slightly lossy link is slow even on a huge pipe.

Two refinements matter in practice. Linux's CUBIC uses **HyStart** to leave slow start before the
first loss, by watching round-trip times rise as the queue fills, so it rarely overshoots as hard as
the sim's 5 MB run does ([netdev 0x1A, 2026](https://netdevconf.info/0x1A/sessions/talk/validation-and-evaluation-of-hystart-for-linux.html)).
And Google's **BBR** stops treating loss as the signal at all: it measures the bottleneck bandwidth
and the minimum round trip and paces packets to match, which holds up far better on links with
random loss ([ACM Queue, 2016](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3022184)). BBR's third version
is still an IETF draft as of October 2026
([draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr/)).

UDP has none of this, which is fine for one DNS question and harmful for a program that sends
megabytes as fast as the socket accepts them. [RFC 8085](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8085),
the IETF's rules for UDP applications, says anything sending more than a little over UDP must
control its rate in a way comparable to TCP.

### Nagle and delayed ACK: the 44 ms request

Back to the first puzzle. **Nagle's algorithm** ([RFC 896](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc896)),
on by default, holds back small writes while any sent data is unacknowledged. **Delayed ACK** makes
the receiver wait before acknowledging, hoping to piggyback the ACK on its reply: at least 40 ms on
Linux, up to 200 ms on Windows. A client writes the header, which goes out at once. It writes the body, which Nagle holds because
the header is unacknowledged. The server has the header but cannot reply without the body, so it
has nothing to piggyback the ACK on and waits for its delayed-ACK timer. Each side waits for the
other until the timer fires. `nagle.py` and `nagle.mjs` time 50 such requests on loopback:

```text title="python nagle.py (Linux; node nagle.mjs gave the same)"
nagle on, two writes     median  44.01 ms   worst  48.20 ms
TCP_NODELAY, two writes  median   0.06 ms   worst   0.16 ms
nagle on, one write      median   0.06 ms   worst   0.10 ms
```

Seven hundred times slower, from one extra write. The better fix is the last line: build the whole
message in memory and write it once, as the framing code below does. `TCP_NODELAY` turns Nagle off
for the socket. Most HTTP and database libraries set it; Node's `http.createServer` does by default
since version 18, but a plain `net.Socket` does not ([Node docs](https://nodejs.org/api/http.html)).
Windows' loopback path does not show the stall, so measure on the operating system you deploy to.

### Closing: TIME-WAIT, CLOSE_WAIT and running out of ports

A TCP connection is named by four numbers: source address, source port, destination address,
destination port. The side that closes first does not free its four-tuple at once; it enters
**TIME-WAIT** for twice the maximum segment lifetime. That wait does two jobs: if the final ACK is
lost, the peer resends its FIN and still gets an answer, and a delayed duplicate segment from this
connection cannot be mistaken for data in a new connection with the same four numbers. The RFC's
value is 4 minutes; Linux uses a fixed **60 seconds**.

Which side holds it is decided by who calls `close()` first.
[`time_wait.py`](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/tcp-vs-udp/python/time_wait.py)
makes 500 requests three ways and counts the TIME-WAIT sockets left behind:

```text title="python time_wait.py (Linux; node time-wait.mjs gave the same)"
client closes first, 500 connections     TIME_WAIT {'client side': 500, 'server side': 0}
server closes first, 500 connections     TIME_WAIT {'client side': 0, 'server side': 500}
one connection reused for 500 requests   TIME_WAIT {'client side': 1, 'server side': 0}

ephemeral ports 32768-60999: 28232. Linux keeps TIME_WAIT for 60 s, so one client
can open at most about 470 new connections a second to one server address and port.
```

TIME-WAIT on a server costs a little memory and nothing else, because the server's port is fixed
and each four-tuple differs by the client's address and port. On a client it is a real limit. Each
outgoing connection takes an **ephemeral port** from 32,768–60,999 on Linux by default (28,232
ports, as of October 2026), and a port stays taken for 60 s after the client closes. Per
destination address and port:

> maximum new connections per second ≈ 28,232 ÷ (connection lifetime + 60 s) ≈ 470

So the service from the opening, at 500 new connections a second, needs 500 × 60 = 30,000 ports,
runs out after about 56 seconds, and `connect()` fails with `EADDRNOTAVAIL`, "cannot assign
requested address", from code that never chose a port. The typical culprit is an HTTP client
created per request, or a proxy calling one backend without keep-alive.

The fixes, best first:

1. **Reuse connections.** A pool with keep-alive turns 500 connections a second into a handful of
   long-lived ones; the third line of the output is the whole argument.
2. **Spread over more four-tuples**: more backend addresses or ports, or more source addresses
   (Cloudflare describes the socket options for this:
   [Cloudflare, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-to-stop-running-out-of-ephemeral-ports-and-start-to-love-long-lived-connections/)).
3. **Widen `ip_local_port_range`** or enable **`tcp_tw_reuse`** (default 2: loopback only), which
   lets a new outgoing connection take a port in TIME-WAIT, relying on TCP timestamps to reject old
   segments. Its cousin `tcp_tw_recycle` broke clients behind NAT and was removed in Linux 4.12.

Do not "fix" TIME-WAIT by closing with `SO_LINGER` set to 0: that sends an RST instead of a FIN,
skips the safety TIME-WAIT exists for, and can throw away data the peer has not read yet.

**CLOSE_WAIT is the opposite state, and it is always your bug.** It means the peer closed and your
application never called `close()`, usually on an error path. The kernel will wait for ever, and a
growing count in `ss -tan` is a file-descriptor leak in progress.

### Idle connections: the middleboxes that forget them

Between your two kernels sit boxes that keep a table entry for every flow they pass: NAT routers,
cloud NAT gateways, load balancers, firewalls, Linux's own connection tracking. Each entry has an
idle timer, and when it expires the entry is deleted without telling either end. The next packet
on that connection is dropped silently, or answered with an RST. TCP's own **keep-alive**, which
could keep the entry fresh, is off unless you enable it, and once enabled Linux waits **2 hours**
before its first probe. Every one of these timeouts is shorter (as of October 2026):

| Box | Idle timeout | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| AWS NAT gateway | 350 s | RST on the next packet ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/vpc/latest/userguide/nat-gateway-troubleshooting.html)) |
| AWS Network Load Balancer | TCP 350 s (60–6,000), UDP 120 s | silent drop ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/networking-and-content-delivery/introducing-nlb-tcp-configurable-idle-timeout/)) |
| Azure Load Balancer | 4 min (up to 100) | silent drop unless "TCP reset on idle" is on ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/load-balancer/load-balancer-tcp-reset)) |
| Google Cloud NAT | TCP 20 min, UDP 30 s | port mapping freed ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/nat/docs/tune-nat-configuration)) |
| Linux conntrack | TCP 5 days, UDP 30 s (120 s once two-way) | entry deleted ([kernel docs](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/nf_conntrack-sysctl.html)) |

The classic symptom: a service keeps a pool of database connections through a NAT gateway; traffic
is near zero overnight; the first queries every morning fail with "connection reset", then
everything works. Make the pool discard connections idle for longer than the shortest timeout on
the path, or send keep-alives below it (`TCP_KEEPIDLE`, or an application ping). Retry idempotent
requests once on a reset on a pooled connection, because that is the common case of a stale entry.
The mirror image, a backend that closes idle connections before its load balancer does and causes
502s, is on the [proxy and load balancer page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/#what-every-extra-hop-costs).

UDP has no FIN, so these boxes can only guess when a UDP flow is over, and they guess short:
[RFC 4787](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4787) asks NATs to keep a UDP mapping at least 2
minutes, but QUIC's designers found that something every 30 seconds is needed in practice
([RFC 9000 §10.1.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000)).

That is why voice and game clients send a tiny packet every 15 to 30 seconds even when nobody
speaks; RFC 8085 asks for no more than one every 15 seconds.

### Why QUIC moved to UDP

If TCP's waits hurt, why not change TCP? Because TCP lives in two places that are very hard to
change. It is in the operating system kernel, so a new feature reaches users only as fast as phones
and servers update their kernels. And it is inspected by millions of firewalls, NATs and load
balancers that parse its headers and drop what they do not recognise. **TCP Fast Open**, which
lets data ride on the SYN ([RFC 7413](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7413)), is the cautionary
tale: boxes that drop SYNs carrying data kept it from wide use. The network has frozen around what
TCP looked like; engineers call this **ossification**.

UDP is the one other transport every network passes. **QUIC**
([RFC 9000](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000), 2021) runs inside the application on top of
UDP, rebuilds TCP's machinery there, and changes the parts that hurt:

- **Independent streams.** One connection carries many streams, each ordered on its own. A loss
  stalls only its own stream: head-of-line blocking moves from the connection to the stream.
- **Encryption built in.** TLS 1.3 is part of the handshake, so transport and encryption are set
  up in one round trip; a returning client can send data in its very first packet (**0-RTT**).
- **Connection IDs.** A connection is not named by the four-tuple, so it survives a phone moving
  from Wi-Fi to mobile data, and a NAT rebinding its port.
- **Nearly every header byte encrypted**, so middleboxes cannot learn to depend on it and freeze it
  again.

At the defaults, TCP settles at about 17% of messages late, QUIC with four streams at about 6%,
and UDP loses exactly the 4% the network dropped. Now set QUIC streams to 1: its chart becomes
identical to TCP's, 17%. QUIC's gain comes entirely from splitting independent data across
streams. A single ordered stream over QUIC (one video, one file, one voice channel sent as a
reliable stream) blocks behind a loss exactly as TCP does; voice over QUIC uses unreliable
datagrams for that reason.

QUIC has real costs. It runs in user space, without the decades of kernel and network-card
offloads TCP has, so it uses more CPU per byte: Google's 2017 paper put it at about 3.5 times TLS
over TCP at the time ([Google, SIGCOMM 2017](https://research.google/pubs/the-quic-transport-protocol-design-and-internet-scale-deployment/)).
Some corporate networks block UDP, so every HTTP/3 client must be able to fall back to TCP. And
0-RTT data can be replayed by an attacker, so it is only safe for idempotent requests. Even so,
HTTP/3 carried about 21% of the requests Cloudflare saw in 2025
([Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/year-in-review/2025)).

### What you must rebuild if you choose UDP

Choosing UDP directly, for a game server, a media path or a metrics pipe, means taking on the
duties TCP would have done. RFC 8085 is the checklist; here are the parts that bite:

- **Size**: stay at or under about 1,200 bytes per datagram unless you discover the path MTU.
- **Rate**: pace your sends and back off when you see loss. The loopback demo above needed nothing
  more than pacing to go from 98% loss to none.
- **Only the reliability you need**: sequence numbers to detect loss and reordering, and
  acknowledgements with timers only for data that must arrive. A game resends inputs but never old
  position snapshots, because the next snapshot replaces them.
- **Duplicates**: a retransmission whose original was only late arrives twice; make applying a
  message twice harmless.
- **NAT keep-alives** every 15 to 30 seconds, as above.
- **Spoofing and amplification.** With no handshake, the source address on a datagram is a claim,
  not a fact. If your reply is much larger than the request, anyone can aim your server at a victim
  by forging the victim's address. QUIC's rule: before an address is verified, send at most three
  times the bytes received from it.

:::danger[A real outage: 1.35 Tbps from a UDP amplifier]
On 28 February 2018, GitHub received what was then the largest attack publicly reported, 1.35 Tbps
at its peak. Attackers sent small requests to tens of thousands of memcached servers left open on
UDP port 11211, forging GitHub's address as the source, and each server sent its much larger reply
to GitHub: amplification of up to 51,000 times. GitHub was unreachable for about five minutes and
intermittently for nine, until its traffic moved behind DDoS protection
([GitHub incident report](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/ddos-incident-report/)).
:::

One more rule: **never carry TCP inside a TCP tunnel.** When a packet is lost, the inner and outer
connections both retransmit and both back off, with timers that fight each other. This is why
WireGuard runs only over UDP ([WireGuard](https://www.wireguard.com/known-limitations/)), and why
VPNs that fall back to TCP feel so much worse on a lossy network.

## Choosing between them

Read the table as "what you get without writing code".

| | TCP | UDP | QUIC (on UDP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setup before data | 1 round trip; 2 with TLS 1.3 | none | 1 with encryption; 0 when resuming |
| A loss delays | everything behind it | nothing | only its own stream |
| Message boundaries | none: a byte stream | kept, up to ~1,200 bytes in practice | none within a stream |
| Rate control | in the kernel | yours to build | in the library |
| Survives a network change | no | if your protocol does | yes, with connection IDs |
| State in middleboxes | until close, or idle timeout | idle timeout only, often 30 s | idle timeout only |
| Typical uses | APIs, databases, HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, SSH | DNS, voice and video media, games, VPN tunnels, metrics | HTTP/3, mobile apps on lossy networks |

In practice you rarely pick TCP or UDP directly; you pick a protocol that sits on one of them.

1. **Every byte must arrive, in order:** payments, API calls, queries. TCP, through HTTP, gRPC or
   your driver, over pooled connections. This is the default for almost everything.
2. **Late data is worthless:** voice, video, game state, live sensor readings. UDP, through a
   library that adds what you need (WebRTC for media, a game networking library).
3. **One tiny question, one tiny answer:** DNS, time sync. UDP, with a retry, and TCP for answers
   too big for one datagram.
4. **TCP's guarantees, but lossy networks, many parallel streams, or roaming clients:** HTTP/3 or
   QUIC through a library or your CDN. Do not write QUIC yourself.

## Build it yourself

Two pieces: framing on a TCP stream, and reliability on UDP. Standard library only; every file is in
[examples/tcp-vs-udp](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/tcp-vs-udp),
and every output on this page came from running them in October 2026.

### Step 1: a length prefix on a TCP stream

Four bytes of length, then the message. The reader reads exactly 4 bytes, then exactly that many
more, however TCP happens to chunk them:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="framing.py"
# runnable version: examples/tcp-vs-udp/python/framing.py
HEADER = struct.Struct(">I")  # 4-byte unsigned length, network byte order
MAX_MESSAGE = 1 << 20  # refuse anything over 1 MB: a bad length must not make us allocate 4 GB


def send_message(sock: socket.socket, message: dict) -> None:
    body = json.dumps(message).encode()
    sock.sendall(HEADER.pack(len(body)) + body)  # one buffer: header and body leave together


def recv_exactly(sock: socket.socket, n: int) -> bytes:
    """Read exactly n bytes. recv() may return fewer than asked for, so loop."""
    buf = bytearray()
    while len(buf) < n:
        chunk = sock.recv(min(n - len(buf), 65536))
        if not chunk:  # the peer closed: clean only if we were between messages
            raise ConnectionClosed("peer closed mid-message" if buf else "peer closed")
        buf += chunk
    return bytes(buf)


def recv_message(sock: socket.socket) -> dict:
    (length,) = HEADER.unpack(recv_exactly(sock, HEADER.size))
    if length > MAX_MESSAGE:
        raise ValueError(f"message of {length} bytes is over the {MAX_MESSAGE}-byte limit")
    return json.loads(recv_exactly(sock, length))
```

```js title="framing.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/tcp-vs-udp/js/framing.mjs
const MAX_MESSAGE = 1 << 20; // refuse anything over 1 MB: a bad length must not make us buffer 4 GB

function encode(message) {
  const body = Buffer.from(JSON.stringify(message));
  const header = Buffer.alloc(4);
  header.writeUInt32BE(body.length);
  return Buffer.concat([header, body]); // one buffer: header and body leave together
}

/** Turns a stream of chunks into whole messages. Call push() with each chunk. */
class MessageReader {
  buffer = Buffer.alloc(0);

  constructor(onMessage) {
    this.onMessage = onMessage;
  }

  push(chunk) {
    this.buffer = Buffer.concat([this.buffer, chunk]);
    while (this.buffer.length >= 4) {
      const length = this.buffer.readUInt32BE(0);
      if (length > MAX_MESSAGE) throw new Error(`message of ${length} bytes is over the ${MAX_MESSAGE}-byte limit`);
      if (this.buffer.length < 4 + length) return; // not all here yet: wait for more chunks
      this.onMessage(JSON.parse(this.buffer.subarray(4, 4 + length)));
      this.buffer = this.buffer.subarray(4 + length);
    }
  }
}
```

### Step 2: reliability on top of UDP

Now the other direction: enough of TCP's machinery on UDP to deliver 100 messages in order over a
bad link. A wrapper drops 10% of datagrams each way and delays the rest by 20 to 30 ms, so they
also reorder. The receiver buffers early arrivals and delivers in order; the sender keeps a timer
per message and doubles it on each resend. The full file also sets the timeout with RFC 6298's
formula and Karn's rule.

```python title="reliable_udp.py"
        while self.expected in self.buffer:  # deliver in order; a gap holds everything behind it
            self.waits.append(time.perf_counter() - self.buffer.pop(self.expected))
            self.delivered.append(self.expected)
            self.expected += 1

# Sender:
```

The same code runs three ways: plain UDP, **stop-and-wait** (a window of 1: send one message, wait
for its ACK), and a **sliding window** of 16:

```text title="python reliable_udp.py (Linux)"
link: 10% loss each way, 20-30 ms delay each way

1. plain UDP, no acks
    91/100 arrived, 9 lost, 50 arrived after a later one
2. stop-and-wait (window of 1)
    100/100 delivered, in order: True, in 6.71 s
    18 retransmissions; final RTO 66 ms
3. sliding window of 16
    100/100 delivered, in order: True, in 0.89 s
    28 retransmissions; final RTO 65 ms
    76 messages arrived but waited for an earlier one; longest wait 206 ms
```

Plain UDP lost 9 messages and delivered half out of order. Stop-and-wait paid one round trip per
message, so 100 messages took almost 7 seconds: throughput is one window per round trip, with a
window of one. The sliding window was seven times faster. The last line is head-of-line blocking
again, now in our own code: 76 of 100 messages arrived and waited for an earlier one. This is
roughly TCP without congestion control or decades of tuning, which makes it a good way to
understand TCP and a bad thing to ship.

## How real systems do it

**Voice and games ride on UDP, with only the reliability they need.** Discord described in 2018 how
its native clients send voice over UDP to its own servers, with periodic pings to keep NATs and
firewalls open, serving 2.6 million concurrent voice users at the time
([Discord engineering](https://discord.com/blog/how-discord-handles-two-and-half-million-concurrent-voice-users-using-webrtc)).
Fast multiplayer games send frequent snapshots of the world and never resend an old one, because
the next snapshot replaces it ([Gaffer on Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/udp_vs_tcp/)).

**HTTP/3 pays off where loss and distance are high.** Google's 2017 QUIC paper reported 15–18%
fewer YouTube rebuffers and Search latency down 3.6–8%, with the biggest gains on slow, distant
paths, exactly where head-of-line blocking and handshake round trips cost most
([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-quic-transport-protocol-design-and-internet-scale-deployment/)).
Uber moved its rider and driver apps to QUIC in 2019 and reported tail latency down 10–30% on
mobile networks ([Uber engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/employing-quic-protocol/)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::caution[The five-second DNS lookups in Kubernetes]
Around 2017 to 2019, many Kubernetes clusters saw DNS lookups that occasionally took exactly 5
seconds. The C library sends the A and AAAA queries in parallel from one UDP socket, and a race in
Linux's connection tracking sometimes dropped one of the pair. Nothing retries a lost UDP packet
except the application, so the client waited out the resolver's 5-second timeout. The kernel races
were fixed in Linux 4.19 and 5.0
([kubernetes/kubernetes#56903](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/issues/56903)).
:::

**TCP's own machinery is attack surface.** In June 2019 Netflix disclosed "SACK Panic"
(CVE-2019-11477): a peer that announced a tiny MSS of 48 bytes and then sent crafted SACKs made
Linux split its retransmission queue into so many pieces that a 16-bit counter overflowed and the
kernel panicked, on every version since 2.6.29. The mitigations, before patched kernels shipped,
were to disable SACK or drop connections with a very small MSS
([Red Hat](https://access.redhat.com/security/vulnerabilities/tcpsack)). Every feature in the
kernel's TCP is code an untrusted peer can drive.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their transport behaviour during an incident, not a design review. Three
numbers worth knowing for every service you own: how many new connections it opens per second to
each dependency, the shortest idle timeout on each path, and the RTO it would face on its longest
path. Each one predicts an outage described on this page.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**TCP costs** round trips before and during every new connection, a stall behind every loss,
kernel timers your application must cut short, and a port held for a minute after every close.
Avoid it when late data is useless (live voice and video, game state), when the whole exchange is
one tiny question, or when you need broadcast or multicast on a local network.

**UDP costs** every guarantee you need, rebuilt by you, plus a TCP fallback for networks that block
it. Avoid it when all your data must arrive in order: you would end up rebuilding TCP, and the
first home-made TCP is always worse than the real one. If you need TCP's guarantees without its
head-of-line blocking or handshakes, use QUIC through a well-tested library.

## Interview corner

A weak answer recites "reliable versus fast". A strong one names the mechanism behind each
guarantee and what it costs when packets are lost.

1. **"TCP or UDP for a voice chat app?"** UDP for audio, because head-of-line blocking turns each
   loss into a stall of at least one round trip and late audio is useless; then list what you must
   add (sequence numbers, a jitter buffer, rate control, NAT keep-alives, encryption) and say you
   would use WebRTC. Chat and settings stay on TCP.
2. **"A 1 MB response is slow for distant users though bandwidth is fine. Why?"** Slow start:
   about seven round trips on a new connection, plus two handshakes. Reuse and warm connections,
   serve closer to the user.
3. **"Why does HTTP/3 run on UDP?"** TCP is ossified by kernels and middleboxes; QUIC rebuilds
   reliability and congestion control in user space with independent streams (no cross-stream
   head-of-line blocking), a combined transport and TLS handshake, and connection IDs for
   migration. Bonus: on a single stream it blocks exactly like TCP.
4. **"Your service logs `EADDRNOTAVAIL` under load."** Ephemeral ports held in client-side
   TIME-WAIT; about 470 new connections a second per destination. Pool connections first.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A 300 KB response, a new HTTPS connection with TLS 1.3, 100 ms round trip, a 1 Gbit/s link, no loss. Roughly how long until the last byte arrives?</summary>

300 KB is about 206 segments. Slow start sends 10, 20, 40, 80 (150 so far) and the remaining 56 in
the fifth round trip, so five round trips of data. Add one for TCP and one for TLS: about seven
round trips, 700 ms. The 1 Gbit/s link needs about 2.4 ms for the bytes; it barely matters.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A voice app sends 50 packets a second over TCP, the round trip is 120 ms, and one packet in the middle of the stream is lost. How late is the lost packet, and how many packets miss a 150 ms deadline?</summary>

The three packets after it are sent at 20, 40 and 60 ms and arrive 60 ms later; the third duplicate
ACK reaches the sender at 60 + 120 = 180 ms, and the resend arrives at 240 ms. Packets sent at 20,
40, 60 and 80 ms wait until then too, with delays of 220, 200, 180 and 160 ms. Five packets miss the
deadline from one loss; UDP would have lost one and delivered the rest on time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A gRPC service holds one HTTP/2 connection to a backend 80 ms away. Requests come in bursts every 2 seconds and each burst is slow, though the connection is never closed. Why, and what would you change?</summary>

Between bursts the connection is idle for longer than the RTO (about 280 ms here), so Linux's
`tcp_slow_start_after_idle` shrinks the congestion window back towards 10 segments, and each burst
pays part of slow start again. Turn `tcp_slow_start_after_idle` off on that host, or keep a little
traffic flowing; also check that the receive window is not the limit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A client makes 800 requests a second to one backend address and port, opening a new connection for each and closing it first. When does it start failing, and with what?</summary>

Each closed connection holds its ephemeral port in TIME-WAIT for 60 s, so the client needs about
800 × 60 = 48,000 ports and has 28,232. It runs out after about 28,232 ÷ 800 ≈ 35 seconds, and
`connect()` fails with `EADDRNOTAVAIL`. Pool and reuse connections; spreading over more backend
addresses or enabling `tcp_tw_reuse` only raises the ceiling.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Every morning the first few queries from your service fail with "connection reset", then everything works. Its database connections go through an AWS NAT gateway and traffic is near zero overnight. What is happening?</summary>

The pooled connections sat idle for longer than the NAT gateway's 350-second idle timeout, so it
deleted their entries without telling either end, and answers the next packet on each with an RST.
Make the pool discard connections idle for under 5 minutes, or enable keep-alives with an idle time
below 350 s, and retry idempotent queries once on a reset.

</details>

## Recap

- **UDP** adds ports, a checksum and message boundaries to IP. Rate, order and loss are yours.
- **TCP** is a reliable, ordered **byte stream between kernels, or an error**. Frame your messages,
  and confirm important work at the application level.
- **Every guarantee is a wait**: handshake round trips, slow start round trips, a stall behind
  every loss, RTO timers from 200 ms (1 s for a SYN), 40 ms Nagle stalls, 60 s of TIME-WAIT.
- **On a new connection, round-trip time decides speed**, not bandwidth: about log₂(size ÷ 14.6 KB)
  round trips of slow start. Under loss, rate ≈ (MSS ÷ RTT) × 1.22 ÷ √p.
- **Reuse connections**, keep them warm, and expire them before the shortest idle timeout on the
  path; a new connection per request runs out of ports at about 470 a second per destination.
- **QUIC** moves TCP's machinery onto UDP with independent streams, a one-round-trip encrypted
  handshake and connection IDs. Its gain is from streams; one stream blocks like TCP.
