## A quiz app that froze on the train

Imagine you have recently joined a small team in Pune that builds a live quiz app for cricket fans.
During a match, friends join a room, answer questions about the next over together, and talk to
each other over voice chat. The first version sends everything over one WebSocket: voice in small
chunks every 20 milliseconds, and score updates as JSON. In the office, on good Wi-Fi, it is
perfect.

Then comes the first big match. The reviews are not kind. On mobile data, voice freezes for half a
second, then plays back too fast to catch up, and the delay between friends slowly grows to
several seconds. Someone senior says, "Voice should never go over TCP. Use UDP, it's faster." So a
teammate moves everything, voice *and* score updates, to UDP.

Voice gets better at once. But new bugs arrive. Some players' scores never update. Sometimes the
"question closed" message arrives before "question opened" and the app shows a closed question that
never opened. The end-of-match leaderboard, about 200 KB of JSON, never arrives at all; the server
logs say "Message too long". And in the old WebSocket server, someone finally finds the cause of a
rare crash: when the server is busy, the JSON parser fails with "Extra data", because two messages
arrived glued together in one read.

Nothing here is a network failure. Every one of these bugs is TCP or UDP doing exactly what it
promises, and the team not knowing what that was. The foundations page,
[What happens when you type a URL](/foundations/how-the-web-works/), showed TCP's handshake as one
step of a page load, and the [DNS](/networking/dns/) page showed DNS using UDP first and TCP when an
answer is too big. This page answers one question: **what do TCP and UDP actually promise, what does
each promise cost, and how do you choose, or build on top of, the right one?**

## Why this problem exists

The internet moves data in **packets**: chunks of at most about 1,500 bytes on most links, each
carrying a source address and a destination address. The layer that moves them is **IP**, the
Internet Protocol, and it makes exactly one promise: it will try. A packet can be dropped when a
router's queue is full, corrupted by a noisy radio link, duplicated, or overtaken by a later packet
that took a faster path. IP does not tell anyone when any of this happens. This is called
**best-effort delivery**.

Best effort is a deliberate design. Routers stay simple and fast because they never remember
anything about the conversations passing through them. Everything that needs memory, such as
"which packets have arrived?" or "is the other side overwhelmed?", is pushed to the two computers at
the ends. That leaves two jobs for the layer above IP, the **transport layer**:

1. **Find the right program.** An address gets a packet to a machine, but a machine runs a web
   server, a database and a hundred other programs. A 16-bit **port** number says which one.
2. **Repair what IP breaks, if you want it repaired.** Put things back in order, resend what was
   lost, drop duplicates, and send no faster than the receiver and the network can take.

The two classic answers split those jobs differently. **UDP**, the User Datagram Protocol
([RFC 768](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc768), 1980), does only the first job, in a
specification three pages long. **TCP**, the Transmission Control Protocol (first standardised in
1981; today [RFC 9293](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293), 2022), does both, and its
specification runs to about a hundred pages. Nearly everything you use daily sits on one of them:
HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, databases, SSH and email on TCP; DNS, voice and video calls, online games, VPN
tunnels and HTTP/3 on UDP.

Who gets hurt when engineers pick the wrong one, or use the right one wrongly? Users first, as
freezes, timeouts and missing data, and almost always on the networks where they can least afford
it: crowded Wi-Fi, mobile data on the move, a village tower. Then engineers, because these bugs
hide in testing. Your laptop and the office network rarely lose packets, so lab tests rarely show
them. They appear only in production, under load, on bad networks, and in the slowest one percent
of requests.

A few terms we will use throughout:

- A **segment** is one TCP packet's worth of data; a **datagram** is one UDP message. Both travel
  inside IP packets.
- A **socket** is the handle your program uses to send and receive, bound to an address and port.
- The **round-trip time** (RTT) is how long a packet takes to reach the other side and an answer to
  come back. From India to a server in Mumbai it may be 10 ms; to Frankfurt about 150 ms.
- The **loss rate** is the share of packets that never arrive.
- The **MTU** (maximum transmission unit) is the largest packet a link can carry, 1,500 bytes on
  Ethernet and most of the internet.

## The obvious picture, and why it breaks

The picture most of us start with fits in one line: **TCP is reliable but slow; UDP is fast but
unreliable.** It appears in every comparison table, and it is not exactly wrong. It is
dangerously incomplete, and three beliefs that grow from it are behind every bug in our story.

**Belief one: a send on one side is a receive on the other.** Here is a server that receives
JSON messages over TCP and parses each read as one message. It is the code most people write
first. The client sends five small score updates and one large leaderboard; the server happens to be
busy for 50 ms before it starts reading:

Six messages went in, four reads came out, and not one parsed. The first read holds all five small
messages *and* the start of the leaderboard; the other three cut the leaderboard wherever they
happened to. TCP delivered every byte, in order, exactly as promised. What it never promised was to
keep your messages apart.

**Belief two: UDP is TCP minus the overhead.** If UDP is "TCP but faster", sending data with it
should be the same, only quicker. Our second demo sends 5,000 numbered datagrams of 1,000 bytes from
one process to another *on the same machine*, with no network involved at all. The receiver does
0.2 ms of work per datagram, so it can handle 5,000 a second:

```text title="udp_loss.py output (Windows 11)"
full speed:
sender:   5000 datagrams sent in 127 ms, 0 send errors
receiver: 645 received, 4355 lost (87%), 561 gaps, 0 out of order

paced to 3,000 per second:
sender:   5000 datagrams sent in 1667 ms, 0 send errors
receiver: 5000 received, 0 lost (0%), 0 gaps, 0 out of order
```

At full speed, 87% of the datagrams vanished, and neither side got a single error. The receiver's
socket buffer filled up and the operating system dropped the rest, as UDP allows it to. On Linux
we saw 93–96% lost. Only when the sender slowed itself down did everything arrive. TCP would have
slowed the sender for us; UDP never slows anyone down. That is not overhead removed. It is a job
handed to you.

**Belief three: "reliable" means "delivered".** When `send()` returns on a TCP socket, your bytes
have been copied into a buffer in your own operating system's kernel. Nothing more. They may not
have left the machine yet. If the other side's process crashes after its kernel acknowledged the
bytes but before the application read them, they are gone, and your side was already told
"success". TCP's promise is narrower than people think: **every byte arrives, in order, or the
connection reports an error**, between two kernels. Whether the other *application* acted on your
request is something only an application-level reply can tell you, which is why payment APIs use
their own acknowledgements and idempotency keys, a later page on this site, on top of TCP.

Before we go inside, here are the neighbours TCP and UDP are most often confused with.

| These are often confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| **TCP** vs **HTTP** | TCP moves bytes between two programs. HTTP is a language of requests and responses that, up to HTTP/2, travels *inside* a TCP connection. HTTP/3 travels inside QUIC instead. |
| **TCP** vs **TLS** | TCP makes bytes arrive reliably; anyone on the path can still read and change them. TLS, layered on top, encrypts and authenticates them. "TCP is secure" is false. |
| **UDP** vs **QUIC** | UDP is the thin envelope. QUIC is a full transport, with reliability, streams, congestion control and built-in encryption, that happens to be carried inside UDP datagrams. |
| **Connectionless** vs **stateless** | UDP has no handshake, but firewalls, NAT routers and Linux's connection tracking still keep state for every UDP flow, with a timer. "No connection" does not mean "nobody remembers you". |
| **Port** vs **connection** | A port names a program on a machine. A TCP connection is named by four numbers together: both addresses and both ports. One server port can hold thousands of connections. TCP port 53 and UDP port 53 are two different sockets. |
| **Unreliable** vs **bad** | UDP is not a worse TCP. It is a smaller promise, and for some data, a voice frame due in 20 ms for example, a smaller promise is exactly the right one. |

## The core idea

Think of the post office. TCP is like a courier service that delivers a long book page by page,
the way a careful office does it. Before starting, the courier phones you to agree that you are
ready. Every page gets a number. You sign for what arrives, and anything unsigned for is sent again.
Pages are handed to you strictly in order: if page 7 is missing, page 8 waits on the counter,
even though it is right there, until page 7 turns up. And if the roads are jammed or your desk is
full, the courier slows down.

UDP is a stack of postcards. No phone call first, no numbers unless you write them, no receipts.
Each postcard is read on its own the moment it arrives. Most arrive. Some arrive in a different
order from the one you posted them in. Some never arrive, and the post office will not tell you.

The analogy breaks in three places, and each one matters.

- **TCP does not keep pages.** It delivers a continuous stream of characters with no page breaks
  at all. Where one of your messages ends is invisible to it, which was belief one.
- **Postcards have no manners.** The courier slows down for traffic; a person posting postcards
  can post ten thousand a second and jam the sorting office for everyone else. Slowing down is
  the sender's job with UDP, which was belief two.
- **The post office does remember postcards, for a while.** Home routers and firewalls note every
  UDP conversation and forget it after a short silence, sometimes as little as 30 seconds.

Put precisely: **UDP adds ports and a checksum to IP and nothing else. Each datagram is delivered
whole, at most once, or not at all, with no order and no pacing. TCP builds a connection with a
handshake and turns IP's packets into a reliable, ordered stream of bytes, using sequence numbers,
acknowledgements and retransmissions, while flow control protects the receiver and congestion
control protects the network. TCP's guarantees are paid for in round trips before data moves and
in waiting whenever something is lost; UDP's freedom is paid for by doing yourself whatever you
need of the rest.**

## How it works, piece by piece

We will build TCP up piece by piece and keep UDP alongside for contrast: what the two share, what
UDP adds, how a TCP connection opens and closes, how lost data is repaired, why a stream has no
message boundaries, how TCP avoids drowning the receiver and the network, what all that order
costs when packets are lost, how QUIC rebuilds the same ideas on top of UDP, and what you owe the
network if you build on UDP yourself.

Where we measured something, we say so. The demos in "Build it yourself" ran on Windows 11 and on
Linux (Ubuntu in WSL 2) in October 2026, and the internet measurements from a network in India.

### What both share: IP, ports and checksums

Both protocols sit inside IP packets and start with the same two fields, a source port and a
destination port. That is where the similarity ends: UDP's whole header is 8 bytes, TCP's is at
least 20.

<Figure caption="UDP's header is two ports, a length and a checksum: 8 bytes. TCP starts with the same ports, then adds the sequence and acknowledgment numbers, flags and a window, where all of its reliability and pacing lives.">
  <Headers />
</Figure>

The tinted fields are TCP's machinery, and the rest of this page is about what they do. A few
things about the shared parts are worth knowing:

- **Ports are 16 bits**, 0 to 65,535. Servers listen on well-known ports (443 for HTTPS, 53 for DNS,
  5432 for PostgreSQL). Clients get a temporary **ephemeral port** from a range the operating system
  chooses: 32,768 to 60,999 on Linux by default as of October 2026
  ([kernel docs](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/ip-sysctl.html)), which is 28,232 ports. Keep
  that number in mind; it comes back in the war stories.
- **A conversation is named by five things**: the protocol, both addresses and both ports. This is
  why one laptop can hold hundreds of connections to the same server: each uses a different
  ephemeral port.
- **The checksum is weak.** It is a 16-bit sum that catches most random corruption, and a packet
  that fails it is dropped silently. It is mandatory for TCP, and for UDP over IPv6. It does not
  protect against anyone deliberately changing your data; that is what TLS is for.

### UDP: a thin layer over IP

What UDP gives you is short enough to list in full:

- **Ports**, so the datagram reaches the right program.
- **A checksum**, so a corrupted datagram is dropped rather than delivered.
- **Message boundaries.** One send is one receive. A datagram arrives whole or not at all, never
  merged with another and never split.

And what it does not give you is everything else: no handshake, no acknowledgement, no
retransmission, no ordering, no duplicate removal, no flow control and no congestion control. A
`sendto()` call returns as soon as the datagram is handed to the kernel. You learn nothing about
what happens next.

Size matters more than people expect. A datagram's payload can be at most 65,507 bytes over IPv4,
and in practice must be much smaller. When a datagram is bigger than the path's MTU, IP cuts it into
**fragments**, and if any one fragment is lost the whole datagram is lost; many firewalls drop
fragments on sight. So real protocols stay small:

- On Ethernet the largest unfragmented UDP payload is 1,500 − 20 (IPv4 header) − 8 (UDP header) =
  **1,472 bytes**.
- The UDP usage guidelines, [RFC 8085](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8085), say to stay under
  the path MTU and, when it is unknown, to fall back to 576 bytes for IPv4 or 1,280 for IPv6.
- DNS defaults to answers of at most **1,232 bytes**, and QUIC requires its first datagrams to be
  at least **1,200 bytes** and assumes paths carry at least that.

That explains our story's leaderboard. A 219,813-byte JSON document cannot be one datagram at all.
We tried: sending 65,507 bytes worked, and 65,508 failed at once with "Message too long" on Linux
(`EMSGSIZE`) and error 10040 on Windows. Anything bigger has to be split into numbered pieces by
your own code, and then you need to notice and resend lost pieces, which is the start of rebuilding
TCP.

Why use something that promises so little? Because sometimes the promise you need is exactly
that small, and anything more gets in the way:

- **Small, independent questions**, where a handshake would double the cost. A DNS question and its
  answer each fit in one datagram; if the answer does not come, the client asks again.
- **Real-time data, where late is the same as lost.** A voice frame for the 20 ms that already
  played, or a game's position update that a newer one has replaced, is worth nothing late.
- **One sender, many receivers.** Broadcast and multicast on a local network, such as a printer
  announcing itself, exist only for UDP.
- **Building a new transport.** QUIC and WireGuard are built on UDP precisely because it adds
  almost nothing and gets through almost every network.

Three practical consequences come straight out of this sequence:

- **New connections cost a round trip, before any encryption.** At 150 ms to Frankfurt, that is
  150 ms of nothing for every new connection. With TLS 1.3 on top, the first request byte reaches
  the server after two round trips. This is why every serious client reuses connections, the
  subject of the Connection pooling page.
- **The side that closes first carries TIME_WAIT.** A client that opens and closes thousands of
  short connections a second to one server piles up thousands of sockets in TIME_WAIT, each holding
  an ephemeral port for 60 seconds. At 28,232 ports per destination, that is about 470 new
  connections a second before the client runs out.
- **An abrupt end is a reset.** If one side sends **RST** instead of FIN, because the process died
  or a port is closed, the other side's next read or write fails with "connection reset by peer".

### Reliability: numbers, acknowledgements and retransmission

TCP numbers every **byte**, not every packet. Each segment carries the number of its first byte,
and each acknowledgement carries the number of the next byte the receiver expects: "ACK 5001"
means "I have everything up to byte 5,000; send 5,001 next". That single number lets the sender
know exactly what is safe and what may need sending again.

There are two ways a sender discovers a loss. The fast one needs no timer at all: watch the
acknowledgements.

The slow way is the **retransmission timeout** (RTO). For every segment the sender starts a timer;
if no acknowledgement arrives in time, it resends. How long to wait is the hard part: too short and
you resend data that was only late, too long and you sit idle. [RFC 6298](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298)
keeps a smoothed average of measured round trips (SRTT) and of how much they vary (RTTVAR), and sets

> RTO = SRTT + 4 × RTTVAR

with three rules around it: before any measurement, start at **1 second**; never go below a
minimum, which the RFC sets at 1 second and Linux sets at **200 ms**; and **double the timeout after
each expiry**, so a dead path is not flooded with retries. Retransmitted segments are never used to
measure the round trip, because you cannot tell which copy the ACK was for (Karn's rule).

**A worked example.** A connection with a steady 100 ms round trip has an RTTVAR close to zero, so
on Linux its RTO is about 100 + 200 = **300 ms**. A segment at the end of a burst is lost, with
nothing behind it to trigger duplicate ACKs. The sender waits 300 ms and resends. If that copy is
lost too, it waits 600 ms, then 1.2 s. One unlucky segment has now cost over two seconds. And the
handshake is worse: a lost SYN has no measurement yet, so the client waits the initial **1 second**
before trying again.

That last fact explains a pattern every on-call engineer eventually sees: a latency graph where the
slowest one percent of requests cluster at almost exactly 1 second or 3 seconds above normal. Those
are lost SYNs, retried at 1 s and then 1 + 2 s. Linux tries 6 times before reporting that a connect
failed, about 131 seconds in total, and keeps retrying data on an established connection for at
least 924.6 seconds, over 15 minutes, before declaring it dead
([kernel docs](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/ip-sysctl.html), `tcp_syn_retries` and
`tcp_retries2`). That is why your application needs its own timeouts, the subject of the Timeouts
page: TCP's patience is measured in minutes.

### A byte stream, not messages

Our first naive demo showed the consequence; here is the reason. TCP's specification says plainly
that there is no correlation between how the application writes and how the segments are cut
([RFC 9293 §3.7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293)). The sender's kernel may combine several
small writes into one segment, and must split a large write into segments of about 1,460 bytes.
The receiver's kernel puts all arriving bytes into one buffer, and a read returns whatever is there,
up to the size you asked for.

<Figure caption="The application writes three messages. TCP delivers the same bytes in whatever chunks happen to be ready, so a read can hold one and a half messages. UDP keeps each datagram whole. With a length field before each message, a TCP reader can find the boundaries again.">
  <Framing />
</Figure>

So every protocol on top of TCP must mark where its messages end. There are three common ways,
and you have used all of them without noticing:

- **A length prefix.** Write the length first, then the bytes. DNS over TCP uses a 2-byte length;
  HTTP/2 starts every frame with a 9-byte header holding a 24-bit length
  ([RFC 9113](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113)); gRPC puts a 1-byte flag and a 4-byte length
  before every message. This is the most robust choice and the one we build below.
- **A delimiter.** End each message with a byte that cannot appear inside it. Newline-delimited JSON
  works this way, and HTTP/1.1 ends its headers with an empty line.
- **A higher-level protocol that does it for you.** WebSocket, HTTP and gRPC libraries frame
  messages so your code never sees the raw stream. Our quiz team's WebSocket server was fine; the
  bug was in a raw TCP side channel someone had added later.

### Flow control: not drowning the receiver

A fast sender can easily outrun a slow receiver; our UDP demo lost 87% of its datagrams exactly
that way. TCP prevents this with the **receive window**: every ACK carries a number saying how many
more bytes the receiver has room for. The sender may have at most that many bytes sent but not yet
acknowledged. If the receiver's application stops reading, the window shrinks to zero and the
sender stops, without losing a byte.

<Figure caption="The window is the number of bytes that may be sent but not yet acknowledged. Every ACK moves its left edge forward, which lets the sender send more at the right edge: the window slides.">
  <SlidingWindow />
</Figure>

This is TCP's built-in **backpressure**: a slow consumer automatically slows the producer, all the
way back through the network. It is also a source of a classic limit. The window field is 16 bits,
so without extensions the most a sender can have in flight is 65,535 bytes. That caps throughput at
one window per round trip:

**A worked example.** To fill a 100 Mbit/s link to a server 100 ms away, you need 100,000,000 ÷ 8 ×
0.1 = **1.25 MB** in flight at all times. This amount, bandwidth × round trip, is called the
**bandwidth-delay product**. A 64 KB window allows only 65,535 × 8 ÷ 0.1 ≈ **5.2 Mbit/s**, however
fast the link is. The **window scale** option ([RFC 7323](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323))
multiplies the field, up to about 1 GB, and modern kernels grow buffers automatically. When a bulk
transfer between two distant data centres is mysteriously slow on a fast link, a small window or
buffer somewhere is the first suspect.

### Congestion control: not drowning the network

The receive window protects the receiver. Nothing in it protects the routers in between, and that
nearly killed the early internet. In October 1986, throughput between Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory
and UC Berkeley, 400 yards apart, fell from 32 kbit/s to 40 bit/s, about 800 times slower. The
network was full of retransmissions of packets that were being dropped because the network was full
of retransmissions. Van Jacobson and Michael Karels' fix, published in 1988
([Congestion Avoidance and Control](https://ee.lbl.gov/papers/congavoid.pdf)), is still the shape
of every TCP today.

The sender keeps a second limit, the **congestion window** (cwnd), its own estimate of how much the
network can take. It may have in flight only the smaller of the receive window and the congestion
window, so its sending rate is roughly min(cwnd, receive window) ÷ RTT.

- **Slow start.** A new connection does not know the path, so it starts small, 10 segments, about
  14.6 KB ([RFC 6928](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6928)), and doubles the window every round
  trip while everything is acknowledged. "Slow" is a historical joke: the growth is exponential.
- **Loss means congestion.** When a loss is detected, the sender assumes a queue overflowed and cuts
  its window. Classic TCP (Reno) halves it.
- **Then it probes again.** It grows the window slowly until the next loss, giving the famous
  sawtooth.

<Figure caption="Slow start doubles the window every round trip until the first loss. CUBIC cuts the window to 70%, climbs back quickly, slows down near the old peak where the last loss happened, then speeds up again to probe for more. Numbers are illustrative.">
  <CongestionWindow />
</Figure>

The algorithm in the drawing is **CUBIC** ([RFC 9438](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438), 2023),
which its RFC notes is the default in the Linux, Windows and Apple TCP stacks. It cuts the window to
70% instead of 50% and grows it along a cubic curve shaped around the window size where the last
loss happened. Google's **BBR** takes a different approach entirely: instead of treating loss as the
signal, it measures the bottleneck's bandwidth and the minimum round trip and paces packets to match
([ACM Queue, 2016](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3022184)); its third version is an IETF draft
as of October 2026.

Two consequences matter in everyday work. A **new connection is slow at first**: a 100 KB response
takes several round trips to arrive in full on a fresh connection, and one on a warm connection
whose window has already grown does not. And by default Linux **restarts slow start after an idle
period** (`tcp_slow_start_after_idle`), so a connection that has been quiet for a while may start
slow again.

UDP has none of this. That is fine for a DNS question, and a disaster for a program that sends
megabytes as fast as the socket will take them. RFC 8085 is blunt: applications that send more than
a little over UDP must control their rate in a way comparable to TCP, and those that cannot should
have a circuit breaker that stops them.

### Head-of-line blocking: the price of order

Here is where the two protocols really part ways. TCP's promise of order means that when one
segment is lost, **every byte behind it waits** until the retransmission arrives, even though
those bytes are already sitting in the receiver's memory. This is **head-of-line blocking**, named
after a queue where the person at the front holds up everyone behind.

For a file download this hardly matters; you want the whole file anyway. For our quiz app's voice
chat it is the whole problem. The simulation sends 50 small messages a second, one every 20 ms, like
voice frames, over a link with a 100 ms round trip and 4% loss. Each bar is one message's delay
from sending to reaching the application; the dashed line is 150 ms, roughly the most delay a
conversation can take before it starts to feel awkward
([ITU-T G.114](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-G.114)).

<TransportSim mode="tcp" />

Every loss in TCP looks the same: one tall amber bar for the lost message, then a staircase of
shorter amber bars descending from it, all messages that had already arrived and were waiting
behind the gap. Then a burst: they reach the app at the same moment. That is exactly the "freeze,
then fast-forward" the quiz app's users heard.

**A worked example with the simulation's numbers.** Message *n* is lost at time 0. The next three
messages are sent at 20, 40 and 60 ms, and each arrives 50 ms later. The duplicate ACK triggered by
the third one gets back to the sender at 60 + 100 = **160 ms**; the resend leaves then and arrives
at **210 ms**. Meanwhile messages *n*+1 to *n*+7 arrived between 70 and 190 ms, and all of them were
held until 210 ms too. So *n* reaches the application 210 ms after it was sent, *n*+1 after 190 ms,
*n*+2 after 170 ms and *n*+3 after exactly 150 ms: one loss pushed three messages past the
deadline and dragged four more towards it. At 4% loss and 50 messages a second, the simulation's
model ends up with about **14%** of messages late, more than three times the loss rate, because now
and then a resend is lost too and only the slower timeout recovers it.

No bar is ever tall: every message that arrives goes to the application at once, 50 ms after it was
sent. The price is visible too: about 4% of messages never arrive. For voice, that is the
better trade by far. A good voice codec hides a missing 20 ms frame by stretching its neighbours, and
the listener barely notices. Nobody can hide a 200 ms freeze.

Raise the round-trip time to 200 ms in the TCP simulation and watch the staircases grow: the
repair takes longer, so more messages pile up behind each loss. This is why TCP feels worst for
real-time use exactly where users are far away and on lossy networks.

### QUIC: TCP's ideas, rebuilt on top of UDP

If TCP's ideas are good and its head-of-line blocking hurts, why not fix TCP? Because TCP lives in
operating system kernels and inside millions of routers, firewalls and load balancers that inspect
its headers. A new TCP feature takes years to reach phones and is often broken by some middlebox on
the path that does not recognise it. TCP Fast Open, which lets data ride on the SYN
([RFC 7413](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7413), 2014), is the cautionary tale: middleboxes that
drop SYNs carrying data kept it from wide use in browsers. Engineers call this **ossification**: the
network has frozen around what it already knows.

**QUIC** ([RFC 9000](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000), 2021) avoids all of that by running
in the application's own process, on top of UDP, which every network already passes. Inside, it
rebuilds TCP's ideas and changes several:

- **Streams.** One QUIC connection carries many independent streams. A loss holds up only the
  stream it belongs to, so a lost packet of one image does not stall the others.
- **Encryption built in.** QUIC always uses TLS 1.3 and sets up transport and encryption in one
  round trip instead of two; a client returning to a server it knows can send data in its very first
  packet (**0-RTT**).
- **Connection IDs.** A connection is not named by addresses and ports, so it survives your phone
  moving from Wi-Fi to mobile data.
- **Better loss detection.** Packet numbers are never reused, so the sender always knows which copy
  an ACK refers to ([RFC 9002](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9002)). It still uses TCP's ideas:
  a packet counts as lost when three later packets are acknowledged.
- **Nearly everything encrypted**, headers included, so middleboxes cannot learn to depend on it
  and ossify it.

<Figure caption="Round trips spent before the first byte of a request can reach the server, at 100 ms each. QUIC sets up transport and encryption together, and a returning client can send data in its first packet.">
  <SetupCost />
</Figure>

HTTP/3 is HTTP over QUIC, and as of 2025 it carried about 21% of the requests Cloudflare saw
([Cloudflare Radar Year in Review](https://radar.cloudflare.com/year-in-review/2025)). The costs
are real too: QUIC runs in user space and encrypts every packet, so it usually needs more CPU per
byte than a kernel TCP stack with decades of hardware offloads behind it, and a few corporate
networks block UDP entirely, which is why every HTTP/3 client falls back to TCP.

QUIC is not magic for a single stream, though. If all your data is one ordered stream, a loss still
holds up everything behind it, exactly as in TCP. The win comes from splitting independent data
across streams. We will see that side by side in the comparison below.

### If you build on UDP yourself

Sometimes you will want UDP directly: a game server, a metrics pipe, a custom protocol between
your own services. RFC 8085, the IETF's guidelines for UDP applications, is a good checklist of
what you take on:

- **Stay small.** Keep datagrams within the path MTU, about 1,200 bytes if you cannot know it.
- **Control your rate.** Pace your sends, back off when you see loss, and stop entirely if things
  look badly wrong. Our demo needed only to slow down to stop losing everything.
- **Add the reliability you need, and only that.** Sequence numbers to detect loss and reordering,
  acknowledgements and timeouts if something must arrive. "Build it yourself" below does this.
- **Expect duplicates and reordering.** Design messages so applying one twice does no harm, or
  remember what you have seen.
- **Keep NATs and firewalls awake.** Home routers and mobile networks translate addresses (**NAT**,
  network address translation) and must remember each UDP flow to let replies back in. They
  forget after a silence.
- **Do not amplify attacks.** UDP has no handshake, so anyone can send a request with a forged
  source address. If your reply is much bigger than the request, you become a weapon pointed at the
  forged address. QUIC's rule: before an address is verified, send at most three times the bytes
  you received from it.

The NAT point deserves a picture, because it surprises everyone who thought UDP had no state.

<Figure caption="A NAT keeps a row for each UDP flow and deletes it after a silence. With a small keep-alive every 25 seconds the server's reply gets back in; after 30 seconds of silence the row is gone and the reply is dropped without a word.">
  <NatTimeout />
</Figure>

How long NATs remember varies wildly. The standard says at least 2 minutes and recommends 5
([RFC 4787](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4787)); QUIC's authors found that in practice
sending something every 30 seconds is necessary to keep most middleboxes from forgetting
([RFC 9000 §10.1.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000)). RFC 8085 asks you not to send
keep-alives more often than every 15 seconds. Somewhere between 15 and 30 seconds is the usual
compromise, and it is exactly why voice and game apps send a tiny packet even in silence.

## Choosing between them

Here is everything side by side. Read it as "what you get without writing code", because you can
always build more on top of UDP, at a cost.

| | TCP | UDP | QUIC (over UDP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setup before data | 1 round trip, 2 with TLS 1.3 | none | 1 round trip with encryption, 0 when resuming |
| Lost data | resent | gone | resent |
| Order | one ordered stream | none | ordered within each stream |
| Message boundaries | none: a byte stream | kept: one datagram per message | per stream, a byte stream |
| A loss delays | everything behind it | nothing | only its own stream |
| Flow and congestion control | yes, in the kernel | none: your job | yes, in the application |
| Encryption | add TLS | add DTLS or your own | always on |
| Through firewalls and NATs | almost always | usually; flows forgotten after silence | usually; falls back to TCP |
| Typical uses | HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, databases, SSH, email | DNS, voice and video media, games, VPN tunnels, metrics | HTTP/3, modern mobile apps |

The simulation below runs all three on identical traffic with identical losses. In the QUIC lane,
the messages are spread over four independent streams, like four images on one web page.

The three charts show three kinds of damage from the same network. TCP turns each loss into a
staircase of late messages. QUIC repairs the same loss, but only the lost message and its own
stream wait, so usually a single bar goes amber. UDP never delivers anything late, and loses exactly
the messages the network dropped. At the default 4% loss, our model shows about 14% of TCP messages
late, 5% of QUIC messages late and 4% of UDP messages lost.

**How to choose.** In practice you rarely choose TCP or UDP directly; you choose a protocol that
sits on one of them. So the useful questions are these:

1. **Must every byte arrive, and in order?** Payments, API calls, file transfers, database
   queries: use TCP, through HTTP, gRPC or your database driver. This is the default for almost
   everything, and the right one.
2. **Is late data worthless?** Voice, video, game state, live sensor readings: use UDP, usually
   through a library that adds what you need (WebRTC for media, a game networking library), and
   send a fresh update rather than repairing an old one.
3. **Is it one tiny question and one tiny answer?** DNS, time sync (NTP): UDP, with a retry and a
   TCP fallback for big answers.
4. **Do you need TCP's guarantees on lossy mobile networks, or many parallel streams, or
   connections that survive a network change?** Use HTTP/3 or QUIC through a library or your CDN.
   Do not write QUIC yourself.

:::tip[Mixing is normal]
Most real products use both. Our quiz app's final design sent voice over UDP (through WebRTC),
and scores, questions and the leaderboard over a WebSocket on TCP, framed by the WebSocket
protocol. Each kind of data got the promise it needed.
:::

## Build it yourself

We will build four things, each closer to what production code needs: framing on top of TCP,
reliability on top of UDP, the fix for a famous TCP latency trap, and a measurement of what a
handshake costs on the real internet. Everything uses only the standard library. Every file is in
[examples/tcp-vs-udp](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/tcp-vs-udp),

### Step 1: framing messages on a TCP stream

The fix for belief one is a length prefix: send 4 bytes saying how long the message is, then the
message. The reader reads exactly 4 bytes, then exactly that many more, looping because `recv()`
may return less than it was asked for:

```python title="framing.py"
# runnable version: examples/tcp-vs-udp/python/framing.py
HEADER = struct.Struct(">I")  # 4-byte unsigned length, network byte order
MAX_MESSAGE = 1 << 20  # refuse anything over 1 MB: a bad length must not make us allocate 4 GB


class ConnectionClosed(Exception):
    pass


def send_message(sock: socket.socket, message: dict) -> None:
    body = json.dumps(message).encode()
    sock.sendall(HEADER.pack(len(body)) + body)  # one buffer: header and body leave together


def recv_exactly(sock: socket.socket, n: int) -> bytes:
    """Read exactly n bytes. recv() may return fewer than asked for, so loop."""
    buf = bytearray()
    while len(buf) < n:
        chunk = sock.recv(min(n - len(buf), 65536))
        if not chunk:  # the peer closed: clean only if we were between messages
            raise ConnectionClosed("peer closed mid-message" if buf else "peer closed")
        buf += chunk
    return bytes(buf)


def recv_message(sock: socket.socket) -> dict:
    (length,) = HEADER.unpack(recv_exactly(sock, HEADER.size))
    if length > MAX_MESSAGE:
        raise ValueError(f"message of {length} bytes is over the {MAX_MESSAGE}-byte limit")
    return json.loads(recv_exactly(sock, length))
```

```js title="framing.mjs"
const MAX_MESSAGE = 1 << 20; // refuse anything over 1 MB: a bad length must not make us buffer 4 GB

function encode(message) {
  const body = Buffer.from(JSON.stringify(message));
  const header = Buffer.alloc(4);
  header.writeUInt32BE(body.length);
  return Buffer.concat([header, body]); // one buffer: header and body leave together
}

/** Turns a stream of chunks into whole messages. Call push() with each chunk. */
class MessageReader {
  buffer = Buffer.alloc(0);

  constructor(onMessage) {
    this.onMessage = onMessage;
  }

  push(chunk) {
    this.buffer = Buffer.concat([this.buffer, chunk]);
    while (this.buffer.length >= 4) {
      const length = this.buffer.readUInt32BE(0);
      if (length > MAX_MESSAGE) throw new Error(`message of ${length} bytes is over the ${MAX_MESSAGE}-byte limit`);
      if (this.buffer.length < 4 + length) return; // not all here yet: wait for more chunks
      this.onMessage(JSON.parse(this.buffer.subarray(4, 4 + length)));
      this.buffer = this.buffer.subarray(4 + length);
    }
  }

  end() {
    if (this.buffer.length) throw new Error('peer closed mid-message');
  }
}
```

### Step 2: reliability on top of UDP

Now the other direction: we start from UDP and add enough of TCP's machinery to deliver 100
messages completely and in order over a bad link. To make the link bad on purpose, every datagram,
in both directions, goes through a small wrapper that drops 10% of them and delays the rest by 20 to
30 ms, so they also arrive out of order. The sockets are real; only the losses are simulated.

The receiver keeps messages that arrive early in a buffer and hands them over strictly in order. The
sender keeps a timer per message, resends on timeout with doubling, and estimates the timeout from
measured round trips with the same formula as RFC 6298:

### Step 3: the 40 millisecond mystery

Here is a TCP trap that has puzzled engineers for decades. A client sends each request in two
writes, a small header and then the body, and waits for the reply. On Linux every request takes
about 40 ms, even on the same machine.

Two good ideas are colliding. **Nagle's algorithm**, on by default, says: while you have unacknowledged
data in flight, hold back small writes and combine them, to avoid flooding the network with tiny
packets. **Delayed ACKs** say: don't send an ACK at once; wait up to about 40 ms on Linux (up to
200 ms on some systems) in case you can piggyback it on a reply. So the client holds the body
until the header is acknowledged, and the server holds the acknowledgement because it has nothing to
reply until the body arrives. Each waits for the other until the delayed-ACK timer fires.

worth a page of reading.

## How real systems do it

The protocols barely change, but the habits around them do. Facts in this section can change, so
each is marked with when we checked.

**DNS: UDP first, TCP when needed.** As the [DNS](/networking/dns/) page showed, a resolver asks
over UDP and retries over TCP when an answer comes back truncated, and RFC 9210 requires both. Our
measurement above is the reason: for one small question, UDP halves the time.

**Voice and video calls ride on UDP.** WebRTC, used by browsers for calls, sends media over UDP
and can fall back to relaying over TCP when UDP is blocked. Discord described its voice system in
2018: native clients send voice over UDP to Discord's own servers, which forward it to everyone in
the channel, with periodic pings to keep firewalls open. At the time it served over 2.6 million
concurrent voice users from more than 850 voice servers in 13 regions
([Discord engineering](https://discord.com/blog/how-discord-handles-two-and-half-million-concurrent-voice-users-using-webrtc)).

**Games send state, not history.** Fast multiplayer games send frequent snapshots of the world over
UDP. A lost snapshot is never resent, because the next one, a few milliseconds later, replaces it.
Glenn Fiedler's long-running series on game networking makes the case that time-critical game data
should never go over TCP, precisely because of the stalls we simulated
([Gaffer on Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/udp_vs_tcp/)).

**HTTP/3 brings TCP's guarantees to lossy networks.** Google's 2017 paper on QUIC reported lower
search latency and 15–18% fewer YouTube rebuffers, with bigger gains on slower, longer paths
([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-quic-transport-protocol-design-and-internet-scale-deployment/)).
Uber moved its rider and driver apps to QUIC in 2019 and reported a 10–30% drop in tail latency for
HTTPS traffic on mobile networks ([Uber engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/employing-quic-protocol/)).
As of 2025, about one HTTP request in five on Cloudflare used HTTP/3.

**VPN tunnels use UDP so they don't fight themselves.** WireGuard deliberately does not run over TCP
([WireGuard](https://www.wireguard.com/known-limitations/)). If you carry TCP connections inside a
tunnel that is itself TCP, a single loss makes both layers retransmit and both back off, and their
timers fight each other, so performance collapses. Over UDP, only the inner TCP connections repair
losses.

**Metrics go over UDP so they never block requests.** StatsD-style metric clients traditionally
send each counter increment as a small UDP datagram. If the metrics server is slow or down, a few
counts are lost, and no request in your application ever waits on a metrics write. That is the
right trade for metrics and the wrong one for logs you need for an audit.

**Load balancers forget idle connections.** As of October 2026, AWS's Network Load Balancer drops
TCP flows idle for 350 seconds by default (configurable from 60 to 6,000 since September 2024) and
UDP flows after 120 seconds ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/networking-and-content-delivery/introducing-nlb-tcp-configurable-idle-timeout/));
its Application Load Balancer closes idle connections after 60 seconds by default
([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/edit-load-balancer-attributes.html)).
Linux's TCP keep-alive, when enabled at all, waits 2 hours before its first probe. Keep that gap in
mind for the war stories.

**Inside Kubernetes, UDP state is a real cost.** Linux's connection tracking removes a TCP entry
when the connection closes, but a UDP entry stays until it times out, 30 seconds by default. The
Kubernetes docs recommend a per-node DNS cache partly to keep short-lived UDP DNS flows from
filling that table, and to send DNS to the cluster's servers over TCP instead
([Kubernetes docs](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/administer-cluster/nodelocaldns/)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A real outage: 1.35 Tbps from a UDP amplifier]
On 28 February 2018, GitHub was hit by what was then the largest attack ever reported publicly:
1.35 Tbps at its peak. Attackers sent small requests to tens of thousands of memcached servers left
open to the internet on UDP port 11211, with GitHub's address forged as the source. Each server sent
its large reply to GitHub, amplifying the traffic up to 51,000 times. GitHub was unreachable from
17:21 to 17:26 UTC and intermittently until 17:30, when it moved its traffic behind Akamai's DDoS
protection ([GitHub incident report](https://github.blog/2018-03-01-ddos-incident-report/)).
:::

The lesson for anyone building on UDP: **without a handshake, the source address is a claim, not a
fact.** Never send a large reply to an address that has not proved it can receive, which is why
QUIC limits its first replies to three times the request and why DNS servers rate-limit answers.

:::caution[The five-second DNS lookups in Kubernetes]
Around 2017 to 2019, many Kubernetes clusters saw DNS lookups that occasionally took exactly 5
seconds. The C library sends the A and AAAA queries in parallel from the same UDP socket. A race in
Linux's connection tracking, during the address translation that routes those queries to the DNS
service, sometimes dropped one of the pair. Nothing retries a lost UDP packet except the
application, so the client waited out the resolver's default 5-second timeout. The kernel races were
fixed in Linux 4.19 and 5.0, and the common workarounds were a per-node DNS cache or the
`single-request-reopen` resolver option
([kernel patch discussion](https://lists.snapcraft.io/archives/kernel-team/2019-July/102291.html),
[kubernetes/kubernetes#56903](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/issues/56903)).
:::

A latency that is always the same odd number, 1 s, 3 s or 5 s, is almost never your code. It is a
timer somewhere: TCP's initial retransmission timeout, its first backoff, or a resolver's timeout.

:::danger[TCP itself can be the bug: SACK Panic]
In June 2019, Jonathan Looney at Netflix reported that a remote attacker could crash Linux machines
with crafted selective acknowledgements: by forcing a tiny segment size, an attacker could overflow a
counter in the kernel's TCP code and trigger a kernel panic. Kernels back to 2.6.29 were affected
(CVE-2019-11477). The fixes shipped in patched kernels, and the mitigations until then were to turn
SACK off or drop connections with a very small segment size
([Red Hat](https://access.redhat.com/security/vulnerabilities/tcpsack),
[Ubuntu](https://ubuntu.com/security/vulnerabilities/sack-panic)).
:::

**Idle connections that are dead without anyone knowing.** Your service keeps a pool of database or
HTTP connections behind a load balancer. Overnight, a connection sits idle for 10 minutes. The load
balancer forgot it at 350 seconds; Linux's keep-alive would not have probed for 2 hours. In the
morning, the first request on that connection fails with "connection reset". The fixes are to make
the pool discard connections idle for less than the shortest idle timeout on the path, or to enable
keep-alives with an interval below it. The mirror image bites HTTP servers behind an ALB: if your
server closes idle connections *before* the load balancer does, the load balancer may send a request
down a connection your server has closed a moment earlier and return a 502. AWS's advice is to make your
application's idle timeout longer than the load balancer's.

**CLOSE_WAIT is always your bug.** A growing number of sockets in CLOSE_WAIT means the other side
closed and your application never did: a missing `close()`, usually on an error path. The kernel
will wait for ever. Find the code path that leaks the socket.

**Ephemeral port exhaustion.** A client that opens many short connections to one destination can run
out of its 28,232 ports, between connections in use and those resting in TIME_WAIT. The symptom is
bizarre: errors such as "address already in use" or "cannot assign requested address" from code
that never binds a port. Cloudflare hit a version of this as WebSocket traffic grew its number of
long-lived outbound connections, and wrote up the careful socket options needed to go beyond one
port range ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-to-stop-running-out-of-ephemeral-ports-and-start-to-love-long-lived-connections/)).
For most of us the fix is simpler: reuse connections instead of opening a new one per request.

:::caution["send() succeeded" means "the kernel has it"]
On TCP, a successful write means your bytes are in your own machine's buffer. On UDP it means even
less. Never treat a successful send as proof that the other side received or acted on anything;
only a reply from the other application can tell you that.
:::

**Big datagrams vanish.** A UDP message slightly over the path MTU is fragmented, and on paths that
drop fragments it disappears every time, while smaller messages work. If some messages never
arrive and they are all the big ones, this is why. Keep datagrams under about 1,200 bytes.

**Corporate networks block UDP.** Some firewalls allow only TCP to ports 80 and 443. HTTP/3
clients fall back to HTTP/2 over TCP, and WebRTC falls back to relays over TCP. If you build your
own UDP protocol, you need a TCP fallback too, or some users cannot connect at all.

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**What TCP costs.** A round trip to open every connection, more with TLS. Head-of-line blocking,
which turns every loss into a stall for everything behind it. A byte stream you must frame yourself.
Kernel timers, measured in seconds and minutes, that your application must cut short with its own
timeouts. And slow evolution: improvements take years to reach every kernel and middlebox.

**When not to use TCP.** When late data is useless: live voice and video, game state, live sensor
feeds. When you need one-to-many delivery on a local network. When the whole exchange is one tiny
question and answer, and the handshake would double its cost.

**What UDP costs.** Everything TCP would have done, if you need it: loss detection, ordering,
pacing and congestion control, NAT keep-alives, protection against spoofing and amplification,
and the fallback for networks that block it. Each one can go subtly wrong, and the failures
appear only on bad networks you do not test on.

**When not to use UDP.** When all your data must arrive, in order: payments, API calls, file
transfers, anything a user expects to be complete. You would end up rebuilding TCP, and the first
version of a home-made TCP is always worse than the real one. If you need TCP's guarantees without
its head-of-line blocking, use QUIC through a well-tested library.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their transport choices during an incident, not a design review. The pattern
repeats: a protocol that worked perfectly on office Wi-Fi, a launch, and then complaints only from
users on trains, in basements and in small towns. Before you ship anything real-time, test it on a
bad network on purpose. On Linux, `tc qdisc add dev eth0 root netem loss 3% delay 100ms` turns a
good link into a mobile one in a second; browser developer tools can throttle too. Ten minutes of
that finds the head-of-line freeze, the missing timeout and the glued-together messages before
your users do.
:::

## Interview corner

TCP versus UDP is one of the most common networking warm-up questions. A weak answer recites
"reliable versus fast". A strong one talks about what each promise costs and what happens when
packets are lost.

1. **"What's the difference between TCP and UDP?"** Connection and handshake versus none; reliable,
   ordered byte stream versus independent datagrams with boundaries kept; flow and congestion control
   versus none. Then the trade-off: TCP costs a round trip to open and stalls everything behind a
   loss; UDP leaves loss, order and pacing to the application. Give one example of each, and QUIC as
   the modern middle ground.
2. **"Why does DNS use UDP? When does it use TCP?"** One small question and answer fits in a
   datagram; UDP takes one round trip, TCP two. TCP when the answer is truncated (too big), for zone
   transfers, and because RFC 9210 requires support for it.
3. **"How does TCP guarantee delivery?"** Byte sequence numbers, cumulative ACKs, retransmission on
   timeout (RTO from smoothed RTT and variance, with exponential backoff) and on three duplicate
   ACKs, plus SACK. Bonus: the guarantee ends at the receiving kernel, not the application.
4. **"Why would you build a voice or game protocol on UDP?"** Head-of-line blocking: a lost packet
   delays everything behind it by at least a round trip, and late audio is useless. A strong answer
   adds what you then must build: sequence numbers, jitter buffers, rate control, keep-alives for
   NATs, encryption.
5. **"What is QUIC and why is it on UDP?"** Reliable, encrypted, multi-stream transport in user
   space. On UDP because middleboxes and kernels ossified TCP, and UDP passes almost everywhere.
   Benefits: no head-of-line blocking across streams, one round trip setup (zero when resuming),
   connection migration.

In system design interviews, the transport usually comes up when you design chat, video, gaming or
real-time features: say which data needs which promise, as our quiz app's final design did.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A server reads messages over a raw TCP socket, one read per message. It passes every test and fails in production under load with JSON errors. Why, and what is the fix?</summary>

TCP is a byte stream with no message boundaries. Under load, several messages pile up in the
receive buffer and one read returns them together, or a large message is split across reads. In
tests, messages arrived one at a time by luck. The fix is framing: a length prefix (read exactly
the header, then exactly that many bytes), a delimiter, or a protocol such as WebSocket that frames
messages for you.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A voice app sends 50 packets a second over TCP. The round trip is 120 ms, and one packet is lost in the middle of the stream. Roughly how late is the lost packet, and how many packets miss a 150 ms deadline?</summary>

The three packets after it are sent at 20, 40 and 60 ms and arrive 60 ms later; the third duplicate
ACK gets back to the sender at 60 + 120 = 180 ms. The resend arrives at 180 + 60 = 240 ms, so the
lost packet's delay is 240 ms. Every packet that arrived before 240 ms waits until then: packets sent
at 20, 40, 60 and 80 ms have delays of 220, 200, 180 and 160 ms. So five packets miss the deadline
from a single loss, where UDP would have lost one and delivered the rest on time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Two processes on the same laptop talk over UDP. No network is involved, yet the receiver gets only a fraction of the datagrams. How is that possible?</summary>

The receiving socket has a fixed buffer. If the sender is faster than the receiver reads, the
buffer fills and the kernel drops new datagrams without telling anyone. UDP has no flow control, so
nothing slows the sender down. Sequence numbers reveal the gaps; pacing the sender, a bigger
buffer or a faster receiver fixes it. Our demo lost 87% at full speed and none when paced.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Every morning, the first few API calls from your service fail with "connection reset", then everything works. The service keeps a pool of TCP connections to another service behind an AWS Network Load Balancer, and traffic is near zero overnight. What is happening?</summary>

The connections sat idle for longer than the load balancer's idle timeout (350 seconds by default
for TCP), so it forgot them. Neither end was told; Linux's keep-alive, if enabled, would not probe
for 2 hours. The first request on each stale connection reaches the load balancer, which has no
record of it, and the connection is reset. Make the pool discard connections idle for, say, under 5
minutes, or enable keep-alives with an interval below the timeout, and retry idempotent requests
once on a reset.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A user is 100 ms away. Counting only round trips, when can the first byte of an HTTPS request reach the server over HTTP/2 (TCP with TLS 1.3), over HTTP/3 on a first visit, and over HTTP/3 when resuming?</summary>

HTTP/2 needs one round trip for TCP and one for TLS 1.3, so the request leaves after 200 ms and
reaches the server half a round trip later, at 250 ms. HTTP/3 sets up transport and encryption
together in one round trip, so the request reaches the server at about 150 ms. When resuming with
0-RTT, the request travels in the first flight and reaches the server after 50 ms.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Why is QUIC built on top of UDP instead of being a new protocol next to TCP and UDP?</summary>

Because the internet's middleboxes, the firewalls, NAT routers and load balancers, pass TCP and
UDP and very often drop anything else, and they also interfere with new TCP features. Building on
UDP gets QUIC through nearly every network, and running in the application rather than the kernel
lets it change at the speed of a library update. Encrypting nearly all of its headers stops
middleboxes from depending on its details, so it can keep evolving.

</details>

## Recap

- **IP is best effort**: packets can be lost, duplicated, reordered or corrupted, and nobody is told.
  The transport layer adds ports, and optionally repair.
- **UDP** adds ports, a checksum and message boundaries, nothing else: no setup, no resends, no
  order, no pacing. Keep datagrams under about 1,200 bytes.
- **TCP** opens a connection with a one-round-trip handshake and delivers a **reliable, ordered byte
  stream**, using sequence numbers, ACKs, fast retransmit and a timeout that starts at 1 s and
  doubles.
- **A byte stream has no message boundaries.** Frame your messages: a length prefix, a delimiter, or
  a protocol that does it for you.
- **Flow control** (the receive window) protects the receiver; **congestion control** (slow start,
  CUBIC, BBR) protects the network. Throughput is at most one window per round trip.
- **Head-of-line blocking** is TCP's price for order: one loss delays everything behind it. For
  real-time data, late equals lost, so UDP wins.
- **QUIC** rebuilds TCP's guarantees on UDP with independent streams, built-in TLS 1.3, one-round-trip
  setup and connection migration. It is what HTTP/3 runs on.
- **Building on UDP means taking on its duties**: rate control, the reliability you need, NAT
  keep-alives every 15 to 30 seconds, and never amplifying spoofed requests.
