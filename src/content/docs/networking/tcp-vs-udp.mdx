## The question

Open a web page on a weak mobile signal and it arrives complete: not one letter is missing. Make a
video call on the same signal and every few seconds a word drops out, or the call freezes and then
rushes to catch up. Both travel as the same small chunks of data, called packets, over the same network. Why does one
arrive whole and the other not, and why would anyone choose the one that loses words?

The short answer: the network underneath loses a few packets and delivers the rest when it can.
Programs choose one of two sets of rules to deal with that. **TCP**, which most web pages travel on,
numbers everything it sends, has the receiver confirm what arrived, resends what went missing and
hands the data over strictly in order. Nothing is lost, but when something is lost, everything
behind it waits for the repair. **UDP**, which most calls travel on, sends each message once and
forgets it. Nothing ever waits, but a lost message is gone. So the choice is never "reliable or
fast". It is: when a packet is lost, would you rather wait, or do without it? The rest of Part 1
walks through that slowly with one voice call; Part 2 opens up each mechanism and puts numbers on it.

## The idea in plain words

TCP and UDP are both **protocols**: rules that the computers at the two ends agree to follow so they
understand each other. HTTP is a protocol you already know. TCP and UDP sit one level below it: they
decide how the bytes of an HTTP request, a database query or a voice call get from one machine to
the other.

Follow one voice call. Your phone cuts your voice into small pieces, one piece for every 20 ms of
sound, so 50 pieces a second. Each piece takes 50 ms to cross the network to your friend's phone.
The mobile network is poor today and loses about 1 piece in 25, which is 4%. (These numbers are
illustrative, but a bad mobile link behaves like this.)

**Over UDP:**

1. Each piece leaves as one packet the moment it is ready. There is no set-up first, so the very
   first piece leaves at once.
2. Pieces 1 to 6 arrive 50 ms after they left, and your friend's phone plays each one as it lands.
3. Piece 7 is lost on the way. Nobody tells your phone, so it never finds out. Your friend's phone
   notices only because piece 8 is the next to arrive, and it covers the missing 20 ms of sound as
   best it can: a short click.
4. Pieces 8, 9 and 10 arrive on time. The whole cost of the loss is 20 ms of sound.

**Over TCP:**

5. Before any sound leaves, TCP sets up a **connection**: the two phones exchange greeting messages,
   which costs one trip there and back, 100 ms.
6. TCP numbers what it sends, and your friend's phone answers every arrival with a short note,
   "I have everything up to piece 6". That note is an **acknowledgement**.
7. Piece 7 is lost. Start a stopwatch when it leaves: 0 ms. Pieces 8, 9 and 10 leave at 20, 40 and
   60 ms and arrive at 70, 90 and 110 ms.
8. TCP must hand pieces over in order, so your friend's phone keeps 8, 9 and 10 in memory and does
   not play them. Each arrival triggers the same note again: "I still have everything only up to
   piece 6".
9. Your phone receives the third copy of that note at 160 ms (it was sent at 110 ms and needs 50 ms
   to come back). It waits for three copies, not one, because a single repeat could mean that two
   pieces only swapped places on the way. Now it resends piece 7.
10. The new copy of piece 7 arrives at 210 ms. Your friend's phone hands pieces 7 to 14, everything
    that arrived while it waited, to the call app at once.
11. Count the delays. Piece 7 left at 0 ms and was played at 210 ms. Piece 8 left at 20 ms and waited
    190 ms; piece 9, 170 ms; piece 10, 150 ms. A conversation starts to feel awkward when the sound
    is more than about 150 ms behind ([ITU-T G.114](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-G.114)). One lost
    piece made three pieces too late and pushed four more close to the limit. Your friend hears the
    call freeze for a fifth of a second, then rush.

Look at the bottom row: TCP did not lose anything, and it did not slow down the network. It made the
pieces behind the loss wait. That is the price of "in order".

Now flip the example. If those pieces were part of a web page, a payment or a database query, the UDP
outcome would be the disaster: a page with a missing chunk is broken, and a payment with a missing
digit is worse. The TCP outcome would be nothing worth noticing, because a page whose last chunk arrives
160 ms late (at 210 ms instead of 50) is still a whole page.

So, in one breath each:

- **UDP** sends each message as one packet. It arrives whole, or not at all, and possibly out of
  order. UDP never waits for anything and never slows down. If you need more, your program builds it.
- **TCP** first sets up a connection, then promises that every byte arrives, in the order it was
  sent, or the program gets an error. It keeps that promise by numbering, confirming and resending,
  and also by slowing the sender down when the receiver or the network cannot keep up. Every one of
  those mechanisms costs someone some waiting.

An everyday picture. UDP is a stack of postcards: each one travels on its own, a few get lost, and
nobody tells the sender. TCP is a courier delivering the numbered pages of a long letter, who hands
you the pages only in order: if page 7 goes missing, you get nothing after page 6 until a
replacement arrives. The picture stops working in one place: TCP's pages are not your pages. TCP
numbers bytes, not messages, and it cuts and joins them as it likes. The building blocks below come
back to this.

In practice the choice comes out like this. When every byte must arrive (web pages, API calls,
payments, database queries), use TCP. When late data is worthless (live voice and video, the
positions in a fast game), use UDP. When the whole exchange is one small question and one small
answer, such as looking up a website's address in DNS, UDP with a retry is enough. A newer third option, QUIC,
runs on UDP and rebuilds most of TCP inside the program; HTTP/3 runs on it, and Part 2 explains why
it exists.

## The building blocks

Seven ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### Packets, and a network that loses them

**What it is.** A **packet** is a small chunk of data, at most about 1,500 bytes, that the network
carries on its own, with the destination's address written on the front.

**Why it exists.** Networks are shared. Cutting data into small packets lets millions of
conversations use the same cables at once, a few packets each, and lets each packet take whatever
route is free.

**Example.** A 100 KB web page leaves the server as about 70 packets. One of them passes through a
busy **router**, a box that passes packets from one cable to the next, whose waiting line is full,
so the router drops it. The other 69 arrive.

**The precise version.** The protocol that carries packets is **IP** (Internet Protocol), and it is
"best effort": a packet can be lost, delayed, duplicated or delivered out of order, and IP tells
nobody. TCP and UDP both run on top of IP. Neither can make the network better; each only decides
what to do about what the network does.

### Bits, bytes and link speed

**What it is.** A **bit** is a single 0 or 1. A **byte** is 8 bits, enough to store one letter of
English text.

**Why it matters here.** Data sizes are counted in bytes (KB, MB), but link speeds are counted in
bits per second (Mbit/s). Mixing the two up puts you out by a factor of 8.

**Example.** A 50 Mbit/s link carries 50 million bits a second, which is 50,000,000 ÷ 8 = 6.25
million bytes, about 6.25 MB a second. A 1 MB file needs about 1 ÷ 6.25 = 0.16 s, 160 ms, of that
link's time.

**The precise version.** On this page KB means 1,000 bytes, Mbit/s a million bits per second and
Gbit/s a billion. Every packet starts with a **header**, a few bytes of labels such as the addresses;
the rest is the **payload**, your data. IP and TCP headers take 40 bytes together, so a 1,500-byte
packet carries 1,460 bytes of your data. A number stored in 16 bits can be anything from 0 to 65,535,
a limit that comes up several times below.

### Sockets and the kernel

**What it is.** A **socket** is the handle a program gets from the operating system to send and
receive data over the network, much as a file handle lets it read and write a file.

**Why it exists.** Programs do not build packets themselves. TCP and UDP run inside the **kernel**,
the core of the operating system, so that one well-tested implementation serves every program on the
machine. The program hands bytes to its socket, and the kernel does the rest.

**Example.** Your Python code calls `sock.send(b"hello")`. The call returns as soon as the kernel has
copied those 5 bytes into its own memory; the packet may leave a moment later. On the other machine,
the kernel keeps arriving bytes in the socket's **receive buffer**, an area of memory set aside for
them, until the program asks for them with `recv()`.

**The precise version.** A **port** is a 16-bit number that tells the kernel which socket an arriving
packet belongs to: a web server listens on port 443, PostgreSQL on 5432. TCP port 53 and UDP port 53
are different sockets. When a send call returns, your data has reached your own kernel and nothing
more.

### Round-trip time

**What it is.** The **round-trip time** (RTT) is how long it takes for a message to reach the other
machine and for an answer to come back.

**Why it matters.** Every TCP mechanism that waits for an answer waits at least one round trip, so
the RTT sets the size of most of the costs on this page.

**Example.** In the call, a piece takes 50 ms to reach your friend's phone and the note takes 50 ms
to come back: an RTT of 100 ms. Between two servers in the same data centre the RTT is often well
under a millisecond; between continents it is often over 100 ms, because signals in a cable take
time to cover the distance.

**The precise version.** The RTT is travel time both ways plus any time spent in router queues, so it
changes from moment to moment, and TCP measures it on every connection. RTT and link speed are
independent: a faster link puts the bits onto the cable faster, but it does not make the trip
shorter. Speed is also called **bandwidth**.

### Sequence numbers and acknowledgements

**What it is.** TCP numbers every byte it sends (the **sequence number**), and the receiver replies
with an **acknowledgement** (ACK) that says "I have every byte up to here".

**Why it exists.** The sender cannot see the network. The only way to know a byte arrived is for the
receiver to say so, and numbers let it say exactly which bytes, spot a gap, and put bytes back in
order.

**Example.** TCP cuts your bytes into **segments**, one per packet. Here are 4,000 bytes sent at once,
numbered from 1 for readability (real connections start from a random number):

| Segment | Carries bytes | Receiver answers | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–1,460 | ACK 1461 | "I have everything up to 1,460" |
| 2 | 1,461–2,920 | ACK 2921 | "everything up to 2,920" |
| 3 | 2,921–4,000 | ACK 4001 | "everything up to 4,000; 4,001 next" |

**The precise version.** An ACK names the next byte the receiver expects. ACKs are **cumulative**:
ACK 4001 also confirms segments 1 and 2, so a lost ACK costs nothing once a later one arrives. A byte
that stays unacknowledged for too long is taken as lost and sent again; much of Part 2 is about how
long "too long" is. UDP has none of this: no numbers, no ACKs. If your program needs them, it adds
them, and Part 2 does exactly that.

### A connection, and the handshake that opens it

**What it is.** A TCP **connection** is an agreement between two kernels to exchange one numbered
stream of bytes in each direction. The **handshake** is the three messages that set it up.

**Why it exists.** Before numbering anything, each side must tell the other where its numbers start,
and the client must learn that the server is there and willing to talk.

**Example.** With a 100 ms round trip, the client sends its greeting at 0 ms and gets the server's
answer at 100 ms. Only then may it send its request, which reaches the server at 150 ms, and the
response comes back at 200 ms. Without the handshake it would have been back at 100 ms: a new
connection costs one extra round trip before the first answer.

**The precise version.** The client sends a **SYN** ("my bytes start at *x*"), the server answers
**SYN-ACK** ("mine start at *y*, and I have yours"), and the client's ACK can carry the first
request. The starting numbers are random so that an outsider cannot guess them. At the end, each side
sends a **FIN** ("I have nothing more to send") and the other ACKs it; an **RST** (reset) instead
aborts the connection at once. UDP has no connection: the first packet is already data.

### A byte stream versus messages

**What it is.** TCP delivers a **byte stream**, one long line of bytes with no edges in it. UDP
delivers **datagrams**: separate messages, each with its own edges.

**Why it exists.** TCP picks its own segment sizes to use the network well, so it joins small writes
together and cuts large ones apart. Once it does that, the edges between your writes are gone. UDP
sends each write as one packet, so the edge survives, but the message must fit in a packet.

**Example.** Your program writes `HELLO` and then `WORLD` to a TCP socket. The other side may read
`HELLOWORLD` in one read, or `HEL` and then `LOWORLD`. On UDP, two sends are two receives, `HELLO`
and `WORLD`, unless one of them is lost.

**The precise version.** Every protocol built on TCP marks where its own messages end. This is called
**framing**, and the usual way is to put each message's length in front of it. Part 2 shows the bug
you get when you forget, and builds the fix. A UDP datagram can in theory hold 65,507 bytes; Part 2
explains why, in practice, you keep it under about 1,200.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- The network loses and reorders some packets. TCP and UDP are two ways to deal with that.
- **UDP** sends each message once, as one packet. Nothing waits; a lost message is gone.
- **TCP** numbers bytes, has the receiver acknowledge them, resends what is missing and delivers in
  order. Nothing is lost, but everything behind a loss waits for the repair, and a new connection
  costs a round trip before it starts.
- Use TCP when every byte must arrive (pages, APIs, payments), UDP when late data is useless (calls,
  games) or the exchange is one small question.
- TCP delivers a stream of bytes, not messages: your program must mark where each message ends.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 had one call and one lost piece. Here are four puzzles from real services, which the rest of
this page solves. A client sends each request to a server 0.1 ms away as two writes, a header and
then a body, and every request takes 44 ms. A 1 MB download on a new connection to a server 100 ms
away takes about 700 ms on a 50 Mbit/s link, and about 700 ms on a 1 Gbit/s link that is twenty
times faster. A voice call over TCP on a link that loses 4% of packets misses its deadline on 17% of
its pieces, four times the loss. And a service that opens 500 new connections a second to one
backend starts failing after about a minute with "cannot assign requested address", from code that
never chose an address.

None of these is a bug. Each is one of TCP's guarantees doing its job, enforced the way Part 1's lost
piece was: **somebody waits**, for a round trip, a timer or a missing packet. The rest of this page
finds each wait, puts a number on it, and works out what you take on when you choose UDP to avoid it.

### The promises, read precisely

**UDP** ([RFC 768](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc768); an **RFC** is a numbered standards
document from the IETF, the body that standardises internet protocols) adds three things to IP:
ports, message boundaries and a **checksum**. A checksum is a 16-bit number computed from the
datagram's bytes and sent with it. The receiver computes it again, and if one bit flipped on the way
the two no longer match and the kernel throws the datagram away. That is all: UDP's header is 8
bytes, and nothing in UDP knows how fast the receiver reads or how full the network is.

**TCP** ([RFC 9293](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293)) promises one thing, and the exact
wording matters: a **reliable, ordered stream of bytes between two kernels, or an error**. Read it
slowly and four consequences fall out, each of which this page measures:

1. **A stream of bytes, not messages.** Where one message ends is your problem.
2. **Ordered means a gap blocks everything behind it**, as piece 7 showed.
3. **Reliable means retried, by timers.** Those timers are measured in hundreds of milliseconds, and
   TCP keeps retrying for minutes before it reports the error.
4. **The sender is held back** by two limits: the free space in the receiver's buffer (**flow
   control**) and the sender's estimate of what the network can carry (**congestion control**). On a
   new connection that estimate starts small.

"Between two kernels" is the part people miss. When the other machine's kernel acknowledges your
bytes, your side is done. If the program on that machine crashes before it reads them, they are gone,
and you were told "success". Only a reply from the program itself can confirm that it acted. That is
why payment APIs add their own replies, and an
[**idempotency key**](/messaging/idempotency-keys/): a unique ID on each request, so that the
server can recognise a retry of a payment it already made and not charge twice.

### The map: one wait per guarantee

Every TCP guarantee is a wait with a known size. This table is the map; each row has its own section
below, where the mechanism gets its name and its numbers.

| Guarantee | What TCP waits for | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Both sides agree to talk | the handshake | 1 round trip per new connection |
| Don't flood the network | permission to send more, which grows one round trip at a time | several round trips on a new connection |
| Nothing lost | repeated ACKs, or a timer | 1 round trip, or 200 ms to seconds |
| In order | the missing segment | everything behind a loss waits too |
| Fewer tiny packets | an ACK that the other side delays on purpose | up to 40 ms on Linux, 200 ms elsewhere |
| No stale packets in a new connection | a cooling-off period after closing | the port, for 60 s on Linux |

UDP waits for none of these and guarantees none of them. So Part 1's choice, wait or do without,
becomes precise: **which of these waits can your data afford, and which guarantees are you willing
to rebuild yourself?**

Two beliefs cause most transport bugs. The first is that **one send on one side is one receive on

that moment, up to 65,536 bytes. The client sends five small score updates and then one 220 KB leaderboard,
while the server is busy for 50 ms:

The second belief is that **UDP is TCP minus the overhead.** `udp_loss.py` sends 5,000 numbered
1,000-byte datagrams from one process to another on the same machine, over **loopback** (the
machine's connection to itself, so no network is involved and no router can drop anything), to a
receiver that spends 0.2 ms on each:

```text title="python udp_loss.py (Linux)"
full speed:
sender:   5000 datagrams sent in 14 ms, 0 send errors
receiver: 104 received, 4896 lost (98%), 4 gaps, 0 out of order

receiver: 5000 received, 0 lost (0%), 0 gaps, 0 out of order
```

98% vanished, with no error on either side. The receiver handles one datagram every 0.2 ms, at most
5,000 a second, while the sender produced all 5,000 in 14 ms. The receiving socket's buffer filled
within milliseconds, and the kernel dropped everything that did not fit. TCP's flow control would
have slowed the sender down instead. Paced to 3,000 a second, below what the receiver can handle,
nothing was lost. UDP did not remove that overhead; it handed you the job.

## How it works, piece by piece

### A byte stream, so you frame your own messages

The bug above is Part 1's `HELLO` and `WORLD` at full size. TCP's specification says outright that
there is no link between how the application writes and how segments are cut
([RFC 9293 §3.7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293)).

On the sending side, the kernel may merge small writes into one segment and must split a large
write into pieces. The largest piece is the **MSS** (maximum segment size), the 1,460 bytes of data
that fit in a 1,500-byte packet after 40 bytes of headers. The 220 KB leaderboard therefore leaves
as about 150 segments.

On the receiving side, the kernel appends every arriving byte to the one receive buffer, and a read
returns whatever is there at that moment. Nothing in the buffer records where one write ended.

<Figure caption="The application writes three messages. TCP delivers the same bytes in whatever chunks happen to be ready, so a read can hold one and a half messages. UDP keeps each datagram whole. With a length field before each message, a TCP reader can find the boundaries again.">
  <Framing />
</Figure>

Every protocol on TCP marks its own boundaries, in one of three ways:

- **A length prefix**: a few bytes in front of each message saying how long it is. DNS over TCP uses
  2 bytes; HTTP/2 puts a 24-bit length on every **frame**, its name for one message
  ([RFC 9113](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113)). The most robust choice; we build it below.
- **A delimiter**: a byte that cannot appear inside a message, such as the newline in
  newline-delimited JSON or the blank line that ends HTTP/1.1 headers.
- **A library that does it**: WebSocket, gRPC (a framework for calls between services) and database
  drivers frame for you. The bug appears when someone opens a plain socket beside them.

A length prefix needs one more defence: the length comes from the network. Without a maximum, one
corrupt or hostile header claiming 4 GB makes your server try to allocate 4 GB.

UDP has the opposite problem. A datagram keeps its boundary, but it has a size limit. The largest
packet a link will carry is its **MTU** (maximum transmission unit), 1,500 bytes on most links. IP
can send a bigger datagram by cutting it into several packets called **fragments**, but the receiver
needs every fragment, so losing any one loses the whole datagram, and many **firewalls** (boxes that
filter traffic by rules) drop fragments outright.

So the theoretical limit, 65,507 bytes of payload over IPv4, the common version of IP (65,535 for the 16-bit length field,
minus 20 bytes of IP header and 8 of UDP header), is not the useful one. The largest payload that
fits one 1,500-byte packet is 1,500 − 20 − 8 = 1,472 bytes. QUIC, the UDP-based protocol near the
end of this page, stays at or below about 1,200 bytes, the size it assumes every path carries.

### Loss repair: fast retransmit, timers, and the one-second spike

Part 1's piece 7 was repaired because the sender saw the same note three times. Here is that
mechanism exactly, then the slower one TCP falls back on when it cannot be used.

When a segment in the middle of a busy stream is lost, the segments after it still arrive. The
receiver cannot acknowledge past the hole, so it repeats its last ACK for each of them. Five
1,000-byte segments, the second one lost:

| Arrives at the receiver | ACK it sends | What the sender concludes |
|---|---|---|
| bytes 1–1,000 | ACK 1001 | all fine |
| (1,001–2,000 is lost) | | |
| bytes 2,001–3,000 | ACK 1001, duplicate 1 | maybe two packets swapped places |
| bytes 3,001–4,000 | ACK 1001, duplicate 2 | still maybe reordering |
| bytes 4,001–5,000 | ACK 1001, duplicate 3 | lost: resend 1,001–2,000 now |

Networks reorder a packet or two often enough that one **duplicate ACK** proves nothing; three
are taken as "lost, not late". The sender resends without waiting for any timer: **fast
retransmit**, roughly one round trip after the loss. A plain ACK cannot say which later bytes
arrived, so **SACK** (selective acknowledgement, on by default everywhere) adds that: each
duplicate ACK also lists the later ranges that did arrive ("SACK 2001–5000"), so only the hole is
resent.

Fast retransmit needs later segments to produce those duplicate ACKs. When the lost segment is the
last of a burst, or the connection is quiet, nothing comes back. Then only the **retransmission
timeout** (RTO) is left: a timer on the oldest unacknowledged segment that resends it when it
fires. Too short, and it resends segments that were only late; too long, and every loss at the end
of a burst waits for nothing. So the sender sets it from what it measures.

Each ACK gives one **RTT sample**: the time from sending a segment to its ACK. Samples vary, because
router queues grow and shrink; that variation is called **jitter**. One sample is therefore noisy,
so [RFC 6298](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298) keeps two running averages. The **smoothed
RTT** (SRTT) moves 1/8 of the way towards each new sample. The **RTT variation** (RTTVAR) moves 1/4
of the way towards how far that sample was from SRTT. The timer is the average plus four times the
typical deviation:

> RTO = SRTT + 4 × RTTVAR

The first sample sets SRTT to itself and RTTVAR to half of it. Three steady samples, then a spike:

| Sample | SRTT | RTTVAR | SRTT + 4 × RTTVAR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 ms | 100 | 50 | 300 ms |
| 100 ms | 100 | 37.5 | 250 ms |
| 100 ms | 100 | 28.1 | 213 ms |
| 180 ms | 110 | 41.1 | 274 ms |

Read the last row like this. The 180 ms sample is 80 ms away from SRTT, so RTTVAR moves a quarter
of the way from 28.1 towards 80: 28.1 + (80 − 28.1) ÷ 4 = 41.1. SRTT moves an eighth of the way from
100 towards 180: 110. The timer becomes 110 + 4 × 41.1 = 274 ms. A steady path pulls the timer down
towards the RTT; jitter pushes it up, so a merely slow ACK is not mistaken for a loss.

Four rules surround it. Before any measurement, the RTO is **1 second**. It has a floor: the RFC
asks for at least 1 s, which would lift every row above to 1 s; Linux instead never lets the
4 × RTTVAR term count for less than **200 ms**, so its RTO is at least SRTT + 200 ms. It **doubles
after every expiry**, so a dead path is not flooded with copies. And a retransmitted segment is
never used to measure the round trip, because the sender cannot tell which copy the ACK was for
(**Karn's rule**).

**A worked example.** On a steady 100 ms path, Linux's RTO is therefore about 100 + 200 =
**300 ms**. The last segment of a response is lost: the sender waits 300 ms and
resends. If that copy is lost too, it waits 600 ms, then 1.2 s. One unlucky segment has cost over
two seconds on a path where a round trip is a tenth of a second. Linux softens the first case with
a **tail loss probe** that resends after about two round trips instead of the full RTO
([RFC 8985](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8985)), but repeated losses still back off.

A lost **SYN**, the handshake's first message, is worse, because there is no measurement yet: the
client waits the initial **1 second**. On older Linux kernels the SYN retries then double (1, 2,
4 s), so a request whose first two SYNs are lost connects 1 + 2 = 3 s late. That is why graphs of
request latency (how long each request takes) so often show a cluster of requests at almost exactly 1 s and 3 s above normal.

Newer kernels changed the spacing through a **sysctl**: a kernel setting, named like
`net.ipv4.tcp_syn_linear_timeouts`, that an administrator can read or change while the machine
runs. Since 2023 (Linux 6.5), this one is 4 by default, so the first four SYN retries are spaced
1 s apart (as of October 2026, [kernel docs](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/ip-sysctl.html)),
and the clusters sit at 1, 2, 3 and 4 s. Either way, a latency that is a suspiciously round number
of seconds is almost always a timer.

TCP is also patient past the point of usefulness. Linux keeps retrying a `connect()` (the call that
opens a connection) for about 2 minutes, and data on an open connection for about 15 minutes
(the `tcp_retries2` sysctl, 15 retries), before it reports an error. Your application needs its own,
much shorter timeouts. The **socket option** `TCP_USER_TIMEOUT`, a setting the program can change
on one socket, caps the kernel's patience for that socket.

### Head-of-line blocking: what "in order" costs

Part 1 counted the cost of one lost piece: 210 ms for the piece itself, and three pieces past the
150 ms deadline. That effect has a name. When a segment is lost, the segments behind it arrive and
wait in the receiver's buffer, because the application cannot have byte 2,001 until it has byte
2,000. This is **head-of-line blocking**: the segment at the head of the line holds up everything
behind it, for a round trip or more.

For a file download that stall is a rounding error. For anything with a deadline it is the whole
problem. The simulation runs Part 1's call for as long as you like: 50 small messages a second, a
100 ms round trip, 4% loss. Each bar is one message's delay from being sent to reaching the
application; the dashed line is the 150 ms deadline. Press "Lose the next packet" to make a loss
happen on demand.

<TransportSim mode="tcp" />

Every loss looks the same: one tall bar for the lost message, then a falling staircase of bars for
the messages that had already arrived and were held behind the gap, all released at the same
instant. That staircase is Part 1's freeze-then-rush.

Over a long run at 4% loss, the model shows **17%** of messages late, four times the loss rate. Most
of that is the staircase: each loss makes three or four messages late, not one. The rest comes from
the occasional resend that is lost too, which only the RTO from the previous section can recover. At
a 200 ms round trip, 42% of messages are late, because every wait is a round trip long.

UDP on the same link has no staircase: every message that arrives is 50 ms old, and 4% never arrive.
For voice that is the better trade. The **codec**, the code that compresses and plays the audio, can
hide a missing 20 ms of sound; nobody can hide a 200 ms freeze.

**HTTP/2 inherits this.** HTTP/1.1 answers one request at a time on each connection, so browsers
open about six connections per site to fetch files in parallel. HTTP/2 instead sends many requests
at once over one TCP connection, each as its own **stream**, with their frames interleaved. The
streams are independent to HTTP, but they share one TCP byte stream, so one lost segment stalls
every stream on the connection, including ones whose data all arrived. On a lossy mobile link the
six HTTP/1.1 connections can beat one HTTP/2 connection, because a loss on one of six stalls only a
sixth of the traffic. That is the problem QUIC was built to remove, and we come back to it with the
same simulation below.

### Flow control: the receive window and the bandwidth-delay product

The UDP demo above showed what happens without this: a fast sender overflows a slow receiver's
buffer. TCP prevents it with a number in every ACK, the **receive window**: how many more bytes the
receiver's buffer can take right now. The sender may have at most that many bytes **in flight**,
meaning sent but not yet acknowledged. Picture the stream as a row of bytes with the window as a
frame laid over it:

<Figure caption="With a 4,000-byte window, after ACK 2001 the sender may have bytes 2,001 to 6,000 out; byte 6,001 must wait. ACK 3001 moves the frame 1,000 bytes to the right and lets 1,000 more bytes go. This is the sliding window.">
  <SlidingWindow />
</Figure>

Each ACK slides the frame forward by the bytes it confirms. If the receiving application stops
reading, its buffer fills, the window it advertises shrinks to zero and the sender stops without
losing a byte. A receiver telling a sender to slow down like this is called **backpressure**, and it
is why the UDP demo above lost 98% while a TCP version would have lost nothing.

The window has a second effect: it caps **throughput**, the bytes delivered per second, at **one
window per round trip**, however fast the link. The sender sends a full window, then waits a round
trip for the ACKs that let it send more. With a 4,000-byte window and a 100 ms round trip, that is
4,000 bytes every 0.1 s, 40 KB/s, even on a 1 Gbit/s link.

Turn it around to ask how big the window must be. To keep a 100 Mbit/s link busy over a 100 ms
round trip, you need 100,000,000 ÷ 8 × 0.1 = **1.25 MB** in flight at all times. That product of
bandwidth and round-trip time is the **bandwidth-delay product** (BDP): the number of bytes that fit
"inside the pipe" at once. The original window field has 16 bits, so it allows at most 65,535
bytes, which caps that path at 65,535 × 8 ÷ 0.1 ≈ **5.2 Mbit/s**.

The window scale option ([RFC 7323](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323)) multiplies the field
and lifts the limit to about 1 GB, and Linux grows socket buffers automatically up to the maximum in
the `tcp_rmem` sysctl. When a transfer between two distant data centres is slow on a fast link and
nothing is lost, a buffer that an application or a sysctl capped is the first suspect.

### Congestion control: slow start in numbers

The receive window protects the receiver. Nothing in it protects the routers in between. Senders
that resend lost packets into a network that is already full make it fuller, and in 1986 that
nearly stopped the early internet: throughput between two Berkeley sites fell by a factor of about
a thousand ([Jacobson and Karels, 1988](https://ee.lbl.gov/papers/congavoid.pdf)). Their fix is
still the shape of every TCP.

The sender keeps a second limit, the **congestion window** (cwnd): its own guess at how much the
network path can hold. It may have in flight only the smaller of cwnd and the receive window. With a
256 KB receive window and a cwnd of 14.6 KB, it sends 14.6 KB; once cwnd grows past 256 KB, the
receiver is the limit.

**Why a guess.** The sender cannot see the routers. A router sends packets out of each link at that
link's speed and holds any excess in a queue; when the queue is full, it drops new arrivals. A
50 Mbit/s link forwards about 4,170 full-size packets a second (50,000,000 ÷ 8 ÷ 1,500). If senders push 5,000 a second,
its queue grows by about 830 packets a second, a 1,000-packet queue is full in 1.2 s, and from
then on every extra packet is dropped. The sender sees only the effects: ACKs come back later as
the queue grows, and one goes missing when a packet is dropped. So a classic TCP probes: it raises
cwnd while everything is acknowledged and cuts it when a loss shows that a queue overflowed.

**Slow start.** A new connection starts at 10 segments, about 14.6 KB
([RFC 6928](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6928)). Every segment acknowledged raises cwnd by one
segment, so 10 segments acknowledged make a window of 20: the window doubles every round trip.

| Round trip | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cwnd (segments) | 10 | 20 | 40 | 80 |
| Sent so far | 14.6 KB | 43.8 KB | 102 KB | 219 KB |

After *k* round trips it has sent 10 × (2^*k* − 1) segments, so a response of *S* bytes on a new
connection needs about log₂(*S* ÷ 14.6 KB + 1) round trips (log₂ *x* is the number of times you
double 1 to reach *x*), rounded up: 3 for 100 KB, 7 for 1 MB, 10 for 10 MB, **whatever the
bandwidth**.

Here is the bill for one ordinary request: a 100 KB HTTPS response on a new connection, 100 ms
away, on a 50 Mbit/s link. HTTPS adds **TLS**, the layer that encrypts the connection, and TLS 1.3
needs one more round trip after TCP's handshake to agree on keys (the [TLS page](/networking/tls/)
explains it).

<Figure caption="One 100 KB response on a new connection takes about 509 ms. The bytes need 16 ms of the link; the rest is two handshake round trips and three slow-start round trips, each waiting for acknowledgements before the window may grow.">
  <RequestTimeline />
</Figure>

Two of the five round trips are handshakes (TCP, then TLS), and three are slow start sending 10, 20
and then the last 39 segments. The link could have carried the whole response in one round trip plus
16 ms (100,000 bytes × 8 ÷ 50,000,000 bits per second). Nothing in this picture is lost or broken;
it is TCP being careful. The rest of the algorithm:

- **A loss means congestion.** The sender assumes a queue overflowed and cuts cwnd: to 70% under
  **CUBIC** ([RFC 9438](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438)), the default in the TCP code of
  Linux, Windows and Apple's systems, or to 50% under **Reno**, the classic algorithm older TCPs
  used.
- **Then it probes again**, more carefully than in slow start (below).
- **Idle resets it.** If a connection sits idle for longer than one RTO, Linux shrinks cwnd back
  towards 10 segments before sending again, because what it learned about the path may be stale
  (`tcp_slow_start_after_idle`, on by default, from [RFC 2861](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2861)).

**How it climbs back.** Reno adds one segment per round trip: after a cut from 100 segments to 50
on a 100 ms path, it needs 50 round trips, 5 s, to get back. CUBIC grows by elapsed time instead,
along a cubic curve centred on *W*max, the window at which the last loss happened. With RFC 9438's
constants and *W*max = 100 segments:

| Seconds after the loss | 0 | 2 | 4.2 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| cwnd (segments) | 70 | 96 | 100 | 102 | 122 |

Fast while well below the old limit, nearly flat around it, where the last loss happened, then
faster again to look for more.

Here is one connection sending the same response again and again. Each bar is one round trip: how
many segments the sender had in flight. The dashed line is the pipe, the BDP. The **bottleneck**,
the slowest link on the path, can queue one more pipe's worth, and anything beyond that is dropped.

<CwndSim />

The defaults are a 1 MB response, 100 ms away, on 50 Mbit/s. The first response climbs 10, 20, 40,
80, 160, 320 and finishes in the seventh round trip, **713 ms**. The link alone needs 260 ms (one
round trip plus 164 ms of bytes), which is what the second response takes: the connection is now
**warm**, meaning its window has already grown, so it sends at full speed from the start. Slide
the bandwidth to 1,000 Mbit/s and the first response takes 701 ms: twenty times the bandwidth
bought 12 ms. On a new connection, round-trip time is the only number that matters.

Then slide the idle gap to 1,000 ms, longer than the 300 ms RTO. The window halves once per RTO of
idle time, so three times during the gap (1,000 ÷ 300 ≈ 3), falls from 640 segments to 80, and every warm response takes 429 ms instead of 260.
This is the trap of "we reuse connections, so slow start is not our problem": a connection that
idles for a second between bursts pays part of slow start again on every burst. Servers that hold
long-lived connections (HTTP/2, gRPC, database proxies) often turn `tcp_slow_start_after_idle` off
for exactly this reason.

**Loss caps throughput, not bandwidth.** Set the response to 5,000 KB and random loss to 1%. The
window never gets near the pipe: each loss cuts it, and it climbs back by only a few segments per
round trip. The sim settles at about **1.6 Mbit/s on a 50 Mbit/s link**.

The classic model of this is the Mathis formula
([Mathis et al., 1997](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/fall24/compsci514/readings/mathis-tcpmodel-ccr97.pdf)),
and it follows from the shape of Reno's window over time, a **sawtooth**. The window climbs by one
segment per round trip to a peak *W*, one loss halves it to *W*/2, and it climbs again. One cycle
lasts *W*/2 round trips at an average of 3*W*/4 segments each, so it sends about
*W*/2 × 3*W*/4 = (3/8)*W*² segments with one loss. The loss rate *p* is therefore
1 ÷ ((3/8)*W*²).

At *p* = 1%, (3/8)*W*² = 100 gives *W* ≈ 16: the window swings between 8 and 16 segments and averages
about 12 per 100 ms round trip, 12 × 1,460 × 8 ÷ 0.1 ≈ **1.4 Mbit/s**, whatever the link. Solved in
general, the long-run rate is about (MSS ÷ RTT) × 1.22 ÷ √*p*, where √*p* is the square root of the
loss rate. Halve the round trip and the rate doubles; cut the loss to 0.1% and it rises about three
times (√10 ≈ 3.2). This is why a long-distance transfer over a slightly lossy link is slow even on
a huge pipe.

Two refinements matter in practice. Linux's CUBIC uses **HyStart** to leave slow start before the
first loss, by watching round-trip times rise as the queue fills, so it rarely overshoots as hard as
the sim's 5 MB run does ([netdev 0x1A, 2026](https://netdevconf.info/0x1A/sessions/talk/validation-and-evaluation-of-hystart-for-linux.html)).
And Google's **BBR** stops treating loss as the signal at all: it measures the bottleneck bandwidth
and the minimum round trip, and spaces its packets evenly in time to match, which holds up far
better on links with random loss ([ACM Queue, 2016](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3022184)). BBR's third version
is still an IETF draft as of October 2026
([draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr/)).

UDP has none of this, which is fine for one DNS question and harmful for a program that sends
megabytes as fast as the socket accepts them. [RFC 8085](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8085),
the IETF's rules for UDP applications, says anything sending more than a little over UDP must
control its rate in a way comparable to TCP.

### Nagle and delayed ACK: the 44 ms request

Back to the first puzzle, the 44 ms request. It comes from two features that are each sensible
alone, and both exist to avoid packets that carry almost nothing.

IP and TCP headers take 40 bytes, so a 1-byte write, such as one keystroke typed into a remote
terminal over SSH, would cost a 41-byte packet. **Nagle's algorithm**
([RFC 896](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc896)), on by default, holds back small writes while any
sent data is unacknowledged, and sends them together when the ACK comes. Ten keystrokes typed during
one round trip leave as one packet instead of ten.

A bare ACK is also a 40-byte packet. So **delayed ACK** makes the receiver wait a little before
acknowledging, hoping to send the ACK inside its reply instead of on its own: at least 40 ms on
Linux, up to 200 ms on Windows. Now the request sent as two writes:

| Time | Client | Server |
|---|---|---|
| 0 ms | writes the header; it goes out at once | has the header, cannot reply yet, so holds the ACK |
| 0 ms | writes the body; Nagle holds it, the header is unacknowledged | waits for the body |
| ~40 ms | | delayed-ACK timer fires; sends the bare ACK |
| ~40 ms | ACK arrives; sends the body | has the body; replies |

Each side waits for the other until the timer fires. `nagle.py` and `nagle.mjs` time 50 such
requests on loopback and print the **median** (the middle value: half the requests were faster) and
the worst:

```text title="python nagle.py (Linux; node nagle.mjs gave the same)"
nagle on, two writes     median  44.01 ms   worst  48.20 ms
TCP_NODELAY, two writes  median   0.06 ms   worst   0.16 ms
nagle on, one write      median   0.06 ms   worst   0.10 ms
```

The first line is the stall: about 44 ms, the delayed-ACK timer firing a little after its 40 ms
minimum. That is
seven hundred times slower than the other two lines, from one extra write. The better fix is the
last line: build the whole message in memory and write it once, as the framing code below does. The
socket option `TCP_NODELAY` turns Nagle off for one socket. Most HTTP and database libraries set it;
Node's `http.createServer` does by default since version 18, but a plain `net.Socket` does not ([Node docs](https://nodejs.org/api/http.html)).
Windows' loopback path does not show the stall, so measure on the operating system you deploy to.

### Closing: TIME-WAIT, CLOSE_WAIT and running out of ports

A TCP connection is named by four numbers, the **four-tuple**: source address, source port,
destination address, destination port. The kernel uses it to find which socket an arriving segment
belongs to, so no two live connections may share one. For a client calling one database, three of
the four never change:

| Source address | Source port | Destination address | Destination port |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10.0.1.7 | 51000 | 10.0.2.9 | 5432 |
| 10.0.1.7 | 51001 | 10.0.2.9 | 5432 |

Only the source port tells these two connections apart, and the kernel picks it for you from a
range of temporary **ephemeral ports**. That is the limit this section ends on.

The side that closes first does not free its four-tuple at once. It enters **TIME-WAIT**, a
cooling-off state, for twice the **maximum segment lifetime** (MSL), the longest a segment is assumed
to survive in the network before a router discards it.

That wait does two jobs. If the last ACK of the closing exchange is lost, the other side resends
its FIN and still gets an answer. And a late copy of a segment from this connection, still
wandering the network, cannot be mistaken for data in a new connection with the same four numbers.
The RFC's MSL is 2 minutes, so TIME-WAIT lasts 4; Linux uses a fixed **60 seconds**.

Which side holds it is decided by who calls `close()` first.
[`time_wait.py`](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/tcp-vs-udp/python/time_wait.py)
makes 500 requests three ways and counts the TIME-WAIT sockets left behind:

```text title="python time_wait.py (Linux; node time-wait.mjs gave the same)"
client closes first, 500 connections     TIME_WAIT {'client side': 500, 'server side': 0}
server closes first, 500 connections     TIME_WAIT {'client side': 0, 'server side': 500}
one connection reused for 500 requests   TIME_WAIT {'client side': 1, 'server side': 0}

ephemeral ports 32768-60999: 28232. Linux keeps TIME_WAIT for 60 s, so one client
can open at most about 470 new connections a second to one server address and port.
```

TIME-WAIT on a server costs a little memory and nothing else, because the server's port is fixed
and each four-tuple differs by the client's address and port. On a client it is a real limit. Each
outgoing connection takes an ephemeral port from 32,768–60,999 on Linux by default (28,232
ports, as of October 2026), and a port stays taken for 60 s after the client closes. Per
destination address and port:

> maximum new connections per second ≈ 28,232 ÷ (connection lifetime + 60 s) ≈ 470

The connection lifetime is small next to 60 s, so the limit is about 28,232 ÷ 60 ≈ 470. The service
from the Going deeper puzzles, at 500 new connections a second, needs 500 × 60 = 30,000 ports. It
runs out after about 28,232 ÷ 500 ≈ 56 seconds, and `connect()` fails with `EADDRNOTAVAIL`,
"cannot assign requested address", from code that never chose a port. The typical culprit is an
HTTP client created per request, or a proxy (a server that forwards requests to another server)
calling one backend without **keep-alive** (keeping a
connection open for the next request instead of closing it).

The fixes, best first:

1. **Reuse connections.** A **connection pool** keeps a few connections open and lends them to
   requests in turn, so 500 connections a second become a handful of long-lived ones; the third line
   of the output is the whole argument.
2. **Spread over more four-tuples**: more backend addresses or ports, or more source addresses
   (Cloudflare describes the socket options for this:
   [Cloudflare, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-to-stop-running-out-of-ephemeral-ports-and-start-to-love-long-lived-connections/)).
3. **Widen `ip_local_port_range`** (the sysctl that sets the ephemeral range) or enable **`tcp_tw_reuse`** (default 2: loopback only), which
   lets a new outgoing connection take a port in TIME-WAIT. It relies on **TCP timestamps**, an
   option that stamps every segment with a clock value, to reject old segments. Its cousin
   `tcp_tw_recycle` broke clients that share one public address (the NAT setups of the next
   section) and was removed in Linux 4.12.

Do not "fix" TIME-WAIT by closing with the socket option `SO_LINGER` set to 0: that sends an RST
instead of a FIN, skips the safety TIME-WAIT exists for, and can throw away data the other side has
not read yet.

**CLOSE_WAIT is the opposite state, and it is always your bug.** It means the other side closed and
your application never called `close()`, usually on an error path. The kernel will wait for ever.
Each such socket holds a **file descriptor**, the number the operating system gives a program for
each open file or socket, and a process may hold only so many. A growing CLOSE_WAIT count in
`ss -tan` (a Linux command that lists TCP sockets) is a leak in progress.

### Idle connections: the middleboxes that forget them

Between your two kernels sit boxes that remember every connection passing through them; engineers
call them **middleboxes**. The commonest is a **NAT** (network address translation). Many machines
with private addresses, such as 10.0.1.7, share one public address, so the NAT rewrites the source
of each outgoing packet and remembers the swap in a table (written as address:port):

| Inside | Rewritten to | Talking to |
|---|---|---|
| 10.0.1.7:51000 | 198.51.100.7:40001 | 203.0.113.5:5432 |

A reply addressed to 198.51.100.7:40001 reaches 10.0.1.7:51000 only because that row exists. Many
boxes keep rows like this one: cloud NAT gateways; **load balancers**, which spread incoming
connections over several servers; firewalls that track connections; and Linux's **conntrack**, the
kernel's connection-tracking table, which Linux firewalls and Kubernetes (a system that runs
containers across many machines) use to route traffic.

Each row has an idle timer, and when it expires the row is deleted without telling either end. The
next packet on that connection is dropped silently, or answered with an RST. TCP has its own
keep-alive, a small probe packet sent on an idle connection, which could keep the row fresh. But it
is off unless you enable it, and once enabled Linux waits **2 hours** before its first probe. Every
one of these timeouts is shorter (as of October 2026):

| Box | Idle timeout | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| AWS NAT gateway | 350 s | RST on the next packet ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/vpc/latest/userguide/nat-gateway-troubleshooting.html)) |
| AWS Network Load Balancer | TCP 350 s (60–6,000), UDP 120 s | silent drop ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/networking-and-content-delivery/introducing-nlb-tcp-configurable-idle-timeout/)) |
| Azure Load Balancer | 4 min (up to 100) | silent drop unless "TCP reset on idle" is on ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/load-balancer/load-balancer-tcp-reset)) |
| Google Cloud NAT | TCP 20 min, UDP 30 s | port mapping freed ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/nat/docs/tune-nat-configuration)) |
| Linux conntrack | TCP 5 days, UDP 30 s (120 s once two-way) | entry deleted ([kernel docs](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/nf_conntrack-sysctl.html)) |

The classic symptom: a service keeps a pool of database connections through a NAT gateway; traffic
is near zero overnight; the first queries every morning fail with "connection reset", then
everything works. Make the pool discard connections idle for longer than the shortest timeout on
the path, or send keep-alives more often than it (the socket option `TCP_KEEPIDLE` sets how
long TCP waits before its first probe; an application-level ping works too). Retry requests that
are **idempotent**, safe to run twice, such as a read, once on a reset on a pooled connection,
because that is the common case of a stale entry. The mirror image, a backend that closes idle connections before its load balancer does and causes
502s, is on the [proxy and load balancer page](/traffic/proxy-lb-gateway/#what-every-extra-hop-costs).

UDP has no FIN, so these boxes can only guess when a UDP conversation is over, and they guess short:
[RFC 4787](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4787) asks NATs to keep a UDP row at least 2 minutes,
but QUIC's designers found that sending something every 30 seconds is needed in practice
([RFC 9000 §10.1.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000)). The figure follows one UDP client
behind a NAT whose rows expire after 30 seconds of silence:

<Figure caption="A NAT keeps a row for each UDP flow and deletes it after a silence. With a small keep-alive every 25 seconds the server's reply gets back in; after 30 seconds of silence the row is gone and the reply is dropped without a word.">
  <NatTimeout />
</Figure>

That is why voice and game clients send a tiny packet every 15 to 30 seconds even when nobody
speaks; RFC 8085 asks for no more than one every 15 seconds.

### Why QUIC moved to UDP

If TCP's waits hurt, why not change TCP? Because TCP lives in two places that are very hard to
change. It is in the operating system kernel, so a new feature reaches users only as fast as phones
and servers update their kernels. And it is inspected by millions of firewalls, NATs and load
balancers that parse its headers and drop what they do not recognise. **TCP Fast Open**, which
lets data ride on the SYN ([RFC 7413](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7413)), is the cautionary
tale: boxes that drop SYNs carrying data kept it from wide use. The network has frozen around what
TCP looked like; engineers call this **ossification**.

UDP is the one other transport every network passes. **QUIC**
([RFC 9000](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000), 2021) runs in **user space**, as a library
inside the application rather than in the kernel, on top of UDP. It rebuilds TCP's machinery there
(numbers, acknowledgements, timers, congestion control) and changes the parts that hurt:

- **Independent streams.** One connection carries many streams, each ordered on its own, like
  HTTP/2's streams but without one shared byte stream underneath. A loss stalls only its own
  stream: head-of-line blocking moves from the connection to the stream.
- **Encryption built in.** TLS 1.3 is part of the handshake, so transport and encryption are set
  up in one round trip; a returning client can send data in its very first packet (**0-RTT**,
  zero round trips).
- **Connection IDs.** A connection is named by an ID in each packet, not by the four-tuple, so it
  survives a phone moving from Wi-Fi to mobile data (a new address) and a NAT changing its port.
- **Nearly every header byte encrypted**, so middleboxes cannot learn to depend on it and freeze it
  again.

messages over four independent streams by default. The TCP lane is also what HTTP/2's streams get,
since they share one TCP connection.

At the defaults, TCP settles at about 17% of messages late, QUIC with four streams at about 6%,
and UDP loses exactly the 4% the network dropped. Now set QUIC streams to 1: its chart becomes
identical to TCP's, 17%. QUIC's gain comes entirely from splitting independent data across
streams. A single ordered stream over QUIC (one video, one file, one voice channel sent as a
reliable stream) blocks behind a loss exactly as TCP does; voice over QUIC uses unreliable
datagrams for that reason.

QUIC has real costs. Over decades, kernels and network cards have learned to do much of TCP's work
in optimised code or in hardware; QUIC in user space has little of that help, so it uses more CPU
per byte. Google's 2017 paper put it at about 3.5 times TLS over TCP at the time
([Google, SIGCOMM 2017](https://research.google/pubs/the-quic-transport-protocol-design-and-internet-scale-deployment/)).
Some corporate networks block UDP, so every HTTP/3 client must be able to fall back to TCP. And an
attacker who records 0-RTT data can send it again (a **replay**), so 0-RTT is only safe for
idempotent requests. Even so,
HTTP/3 carried about 21% of the requests Cloudflare saw in 2025
([Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/year-in-review/2025)).

### What you must rebuild if you choose UDP

Choosing UDP directly, for a game server, the audio and video of a call, or a stream of monitoring
measurements, means taking on the duties TCP would have done. RFC 8085 is the checklist; here are the parts that bite:

- **Size**: stay at or under about 1,200 bytes per datagram, unless your protocol measures the
  largest packet the whole path carries (**path MTU discovery**).
- **Rate**: pace your sends and back off when you see loss. The loopback demo above needed nothing
  more than pacing to go from 98% loss to none.
- **Only the reliability you need**: sequence numbers to detect loss and reordering, and
  acknowledgements with timers only for data that must arrive. A game resends inputs but never old
  position snapshots, because the next snapshot replaces them.
- **Duplicates**: a retransmission whose original was only late arrives twice; make applying a
  message twice harmless.
- **NAT keep-alives** every 15 to 30 seconds, as above.
- **Spoofing and amplification.** With no handshake, the source address on a datagram is a claim,
  not a fact: anyone can write someone else's address there (**spoofing**). If your reply is much
  larger than the request, an attacker can send you small requests "from" a victim and let your
  server bury the victim in large replies (**amplification**). QUIC's rule: before an address is
  verified, send at most three times the bytes received from it.

:::danger[A real outage: 1.35 Tbps from a UDP amplifier]
On 28 February 2018, GitHub received what was then the largest **DDoS** (distributed denial of
service: flooding a target from many machines at once) publicly reported, 1.35 Tbps (terabits, a
million million bits, per second) at its peak. Attackers sent small requests to tens of thousands
of memcached servers (a popular in-memory cache) left open on UDP port 11211, forging GitHub's
address as the source, and each server sent its much larger reply to GitHub: amplification of up
to 51,000 times. GitHub was unreachable for about five minutes and intermittently for nine, until
its traffic moved behind DDoS protection
([GitHub incident report](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/ddos-incident-report/)).
:::

One more rule: **never carry TCP inside a TCP tunnel.** A **VPN** (virtual private network) is a
tunnel: it carries a whole network's packets inside one connection to a VPN server. If that outer
connection is TCP and the traffic inside is TCP too, a lost packet makes the inner and outer
connections both retransmit and both back off, with timers that fight each other. This is why
WireGuard, a widely used VPN, runs only over UDP ([WireGuard](https://www.wireguard.com/known-limitations/)), and why
VPNs that fall back to TCP feel so much worse on a lossy network.

## Choosing between them

Read the table as "what you get without writing code".

| | TCP | UDP | QUIC (on UDP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setup before data | 1 round trip; 2 with TLS 1.3 | none | 1 with encryption; 0 when resuming |
| A loss delays | everything behind it | nothing | only its own stream |
| Message boundaries | none: a byte stream | kept, up to ~1,200 bytes in practice | none within a stream |
| Rate control | in the kernel | yours to build | in the library |
| Survives a network change | no | if your protocol does | yes, with connection IDs |
| State in middleboxes | until close, or idle timeout | idle timeout only, often 30 s | idle timeout only |
| Typical uses | APIs, databases, HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, SSH | DNS, voice and video media, games, VPN tunnels, metrics | HTTP/3, mobile apps on lossy networks |

In practice you rarely pick TCP or UDP directly; you pick a protocol that sits on one of them.

1. **Every byte must arrive, in order:** payments, API calls, queries. TCP, through HTTP, gRPC or
   your driver, over pooled connections. This is the default for almost everything.
2. **Late data is worthless:** voice, video, game state, live sensor readings. UDP, through a
   library that adds what you need: WebRTC, the standard browsers and apps use for calls, or a game
   networking library.
3. **One tiny question, one tiny answer:** DNS, time sync. UDP, with a retry, and TCP for answers
   too big for one datagram.
4. **TCP's guarantees, but lossy networks, many parallel streams, or roaming clients:** HTTP/3 or
   QUIC through a library, or through your **CDN** (content delivery network, the servers near
   users that many sites put in front of their own). Do not write QUIC yourself.

## Build it yourself

Two pieces: framing on a TCP stream, and reliability on UDP. Standard library only; every file is in
[examples/tcp-vs-udp](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/tcp-vs-udp),
and every output on this page came from running them in October 2026.

### Step 1: a length prefix on a TCP stream

Four bytes of length, then the message. The reader reads exactly 4 bytes, then exactly that many
more, however TCP happens to chunk them:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="framing.py"
# runnable version: examples/tcp-vs-udp/python/framing.py
HEADER = struct.Struct(">I")  # 4-byte unsigned length, network byte order
MAX_MESSAGE = 1 << 20  # refuse anything over 1 MB: a bad length must not make us allocate 4 GB


def send_message(sock: socket.socket, message: dict) -> None:
    body = json.dumps(message).encode()
    sock.sendall(HEADER.pack(len(body)) + body)  # one buffer: header and body leave together


def recv_exactly(sock: socket.socket, n: int) -> bytes:
    """Read exactly n bytes. recv() may return fewer than asked for, so loop."""
    buf = bytearray()
    while len(buf) < n:
        chunk = sock.recv(min(n - len(buf), 65536))
        if not chunk:  # the peer closed: clean only if we were between messages
            raise ConnectionClosed("peer closed mid-message" if buf else "peer closed")
        buf += chunk
    return bytes(buf)


def recv_message(sock: socket.socket) -> dict:
    (length,) = HEADER.unpack(recv_exactly(sock, HEADER.size))
    if length > MAX_MESSAGE:
        raise ValueError(f"message of {length} bytes is over the {MAX_MESSAGE}-byte limit")
    return json.loads(recv_exactly(sock, length))
```

```js title="framing.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/tcp-vs-udp/js/framing.mjs
const MAX_MESSAGE = 1 << 20; // refuse anything over 1 MB: a bad length must not make us buffer 4 GB

function encode(message) {
  const body = Buffer.from(JSON.stringify(message));
  const header = Buffer.alloc(4);
  header.writeUInt32BE(body.length);
  return Buffer.concat([header, body]); // one buffer: header and body leave together
}

/** Turns a stream of chunks into whole messages. Call push() with each chunk. */
class MessageReader {
  buffer = Buffer.alloc(0);

  constructor(onMessage) {
    this.onMessage = onMessage;
  }

  push(chunk) {
    this.buffer = Buffer.concat([this.buffer, chunk]);
    while (this.buffer.length >= 4) {
      const length = this.buffer.readUInt32BE(0);
      if (length > MAX_MESSAGE) throw new Error(`message of ${length} bytes is over the ${MAX_MESSAGE}-byte limit`);
      if (this.buffer.length < 4 + length) return; // not all here yet: wait for more chunks
      this.onMessage(JSON.parse(this.buffer.subarray(4, 4 + length)));
      this.buffer = this.buffer.subarray(4 + length);
    }
  }
}
```
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

Read it like this. The sender turns the body's length into 4 bytes, biggest part first (called
**network byte order**, the order internet protocols use), and sends those 4 bytes and the body in
one write. A 47-byte score update goes out as four bytes with the values 0, 0, 0 and 47, followed by the
47 bytes of JSON. The reader never trusts one read to be one message: it reads until it has 4 bytes,
learns the length, then reads until it has that many more. The Node version does the same with a
growing buffer that it cuts whenever a whole message is inside.

With the same traffic and the same busy server, all six messages parse, the leaderboard included.
Note the single `sendall` of header plus body: two writes would invite the Nagle stall above.

### Step 2: reliability on top of UDP

Now the other direction: enough of TCP's machinery on UDP to deliver 100 messages in order over a
bad link. A wrapper drops 10% of datagrams each way and delays the rest by 20 to 30 ms, so they
also reorder. The receiver buffers early arrivals and delivers in order; the sender keeps a timer
per message and doubles it on each resend. The full file also sets the timeout with RFC 6298's
formula and Karn's rule.

The same code runs three ways: plain UDP, **stop-and-wait** (a window of 1: send one message, wait
for its ACK), and a **sliding window** of 16:

```text title="python reliable_udp.py (Linux)"
link: 10% loss each way, 20-30 ms delay each way

1. plain UDP, no acks
    91/100 arrived, 9 lost, 50 arrived after a later one
2. stop-and-wait (window of 1)
    100/100 delivered, in order: True, in 6.71 s
    18 retransmissions; final RTO 66 ms
3. sliding window of 16
    100/100 delivered, in order: True, in 0.89 s
    28 retransmissions; final RTO 65 ms
    76 messages arrived but waited for an earlier one; longest wait 206 ms
```

Plain UDP lost 9 messages and delivered half out of order. Stop-and-wait paid one round trip per
message. A round trip on this link is about 50 ms (20 to 30 ms each way), so 100 messages need at
least 5 s, and the 18 timeouts add the rest: almost 7 seconds. That is the flow-control rule, one
window per round trip, with a window of one. The sliding window was seven times faster. The last line is head-of-line blocking
again, now in our own code: 76 of 100 messages arrived and waited for an earlier one. This is
roughly TCP without congestion control or decades of tuning, which makes it a good way to
understand TCP and a bad thing to ship.

lists the leaks. `ss -ti` shows each connection's `cwnd`, `rtt` and retransmissions. On a test box,
Linux's traffic-control tool can add loss and delay to a network interface:
`tc qdisc add dev eth0 root netem loss 3% delay 100ms` turns a good link into a bad mobile one in
one command.

## How real systems do it

**Voice and games ride on UDP, with only the reliability they need.** Discord described in 2018 how
its native clients send voice over UDP to its own servers, with periodic pings to keep NATs and
firewalls open, serving 2.6 million concurrent voice users at the time
([Discord engineering](https://discord.com/blog/how-discord-handles-two-and-half-million-concurrent-voice-users-using-webrtc)).
Fast multiplayer games send frequent snapshots of the world and never resend an old one, because
the next snapshot replaces it ([Gaffer on Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/udp_vs_tcp/)).

**HTTP/3 pays off where loss and distance are high.** Google's 2017 QUIC paper reported 15–18%
fewer YouTube **rebuffers** (moments when a video stops to wait for more data) and Search latency
down 3.6–8%, with the biggest gains on slow, distant paths, exactly where head-of-line blocking and
handshake round trips cost most
([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-quic-transport-protocol-design-and-internet-scale-deployment/)).
Uber moved its rider and driver apps to QUIC in 2019 and reported **tail latency**, the time taken
by the slowest few percent of requests, down 10–30% on mobile networks
([Uber engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/employing-quic-protocol/)).

## Gotchas and war stories

:::caution[The five-second DNS lookups in Kubernetes]
Around 2017 to 2019, many Kubernetes clusters saw DNS lookups that occasionally took exactly 5
seconds. The standard C library on Linux (glibc), which most programs use to look up names, sends
the queries for a name's IPv4 and IPv6 addresses at the same moment from one UDP socket. A **race**
in conntrack, a bug that appears only when two packets are handled at almost the same instant,
sometimes dropped one of the pair. Nothing retries a lost UDP packet except the application, so the
client waited out the lookup's 5-second timeout. The kernel races were fixed in Linux 4.19 and 5.0
([kubernetes/kubernetes#56903](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/issues/56903)).
:::

**TCP's own machinery is attack surface.** In June 2019 Netflix disclosed "SACK Panic"
(CVE-2019-11477; a CVE is the public ID given to a security hole). A peer that announced a tiny MSS
of 48 bytes and then sent crafted SACKs made Linux split its **retransmission queue**, the sent
segments it keeps in case they must be resent, into so many pieces that a 16-bit counter overflowed
and the kernel crashed, on every version since 2.6.29. The mitigations, before patched kernels shipped,
were to disable SACK or drop connections with a very small MSS
([Red Hat](https://access.redhat.com/security/vulnerabilities/tcpsack)). Every feature in the
kernel's TCP is code an untrusted peer can drive.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their transport behaviour during an incident, not a design review. Three
numbers worth knowing for every service you own: how many new connections it opens per second to
each dependency, the shortest idle timeout on each path, and the RTO it would face on its longest
path. Each one predicts an outage described on this page.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

**TCP costs** round trips before and during every new connection, a stall behind every loss,
kernel timers your application must cut short, and a port held for a minute after every close.
Avoid it when late data is useless (live voice and video, game state), when the whole exchange is
one tiny question, or when you need to send one packet to every machine, or to a group of machines,
on a local network (**broadcast** and **multicast**, which only UDP supports).

**UDP costs** every guarantee you need, rebuilt by you, plus a TCP fallback for networks that block
it. Avoid it when all your data must arrive in order: you would end up rebuilding TCP, and the
first home-made TCP is always worse than the real one. If you need TCP's guarantees without its
head-of-line blocking or handshakes, use QUIC through a well-tested library.

## Interview corner

A weak answer recites "reliable versus fast". A strong one names the mechanism behind each
guarantee and what it costs when packets are lost.

1. **"TCP or UDP for a voice chat app?"** UDP for audio, because head-of-line blocking turns each
   loss into a stall of at least one round trip and late audio is useless; then list what you must
   add (sequence numbers; a **jitter buffer**, a short delay at the receiver that lets unevenly
   arriving pieces play smoothly; rate control; NAT keep-alives; encryption) and say you would use
   WebRTC. Chat and settings stay on TCP.
2. **"A 1 MB response is slow for distant users though bandwidth is fine. Why?"** Slow start:
   about seven round trips on a new connection, plus two handshakes. Reuse and warm connections,
   serve closer to the user.
3. **"Why does HTTP/3 run on UDP?"** TCP is ossified by kernels and middleboxes; QUIC rebuilds
   reliability and congestion control in user space with independent streams (no cross-stream
   head-of-line blocking), a combined transport and TLS handshake, and connection IDs that
   survive a change of network. Bonus: on a single stream it blocks exactly like TCP.
4. **"Your service logs `EADDRNOTAVAIL` under load."** Ephemeral ports held in client-side
   TIME-WAIT; about 470 new connections a second per destination. Pool connections first.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A 300 KB response, a new HTTPS connection with TLS 1.3, 100 ms round trip, a 1 Gbit/s link, no loss. Roughly how long until the last byte arrives?</summary>

300 KB is about 206 segments. Slow start sends 10, 20, 40, 80 (150 so far) and the remaining 56 in
the fifth round trip, so five round trips of data. Add one for TCP and one for TLS: about seven
round trips, 700 ms. The 1 Gbit/s link needs about 2.4 ms for the bytes; it barely matters.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A voice app sends 50 packets a second over TCP, the round trip is 120 ms, and one packet in the middle of the stream is lost. How late is the lost packet, and how many packets miss a 150 ms deadline?</summary>

The three packets after it are sent at 20, 40 and 60 ms and arrive 60 ms later; the third duplicate
ACK reaches the sender at 60 + 120 = 180 ms, and the resend arrives at 240 ms. Packets sent at 20,
40, 60 and 80 ms wait until then too, with delays of 220, 200, 180 and 160 ms. Five packets miss the
deadline from one loss; UDP would have lost one and delivered the rest on time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A gRPC service holds one HTTP/2 connection to a backend 80 ms away. Requests come in bursts every 2 seconds and each burst is slow, though the connection is never closed. Why, and what would you change?</summary>

Between bursts the connection is idle for longer than the RTO (about 280 ms here), so Linux's
`tcp_slow_start_after_idle` shrinks the congestion window back towards 10 segments, and each burst
pays part of slow start again. Turn `tcp_slow_start_after_idle` off on that host, or keep a little
traffic flowing; also check that the receive window is not the limit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A client makes 800 requests a second to one backend address and port, opening a new connection for each and closing it first. When does it start failing, and with what?</summary>

Each closed connection holds its ephemeral port in TIME-WAIT for 60 s, so the client needs about
800 × 60 = 48,000 ports and has 28,232. It runs out after about 28,232 ÷ 800 ≈ 35 seconds, and
`connect()` fails with `EADDRNOTAVAIL`. Pool and reuse connections; spreading over more backend
addresses or enabling `tcp_tw_reuse` only raises the ceiling.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Every morning the first few queries from your service fail with "connection reset", then everything works. Its database connections go through an AWS NAT gateway and traffic is near zero overnight. What is happening?</summary>

The pooled connections sat idle for longer than the NAT gateway's 350-second idle timeout, so it
deleted their entries without telling either end, and answers the next packet on each with an RST.
Make the pool discard connections idle for under 5 minutes, or enable keep-alives with an idle time
below 350 s, and retry idempotent queries once on a reset.

</details>

## Recap

- **UDP** adds ports, a checksum and message boundaries to IP. Rate, order and loss are yours.
- **TCP** is a reliable, ordered **byte stream between kernels, or an error**. Frame your messages,
  and confirm important work at the application level.
- **Every guarantee is a wait**: handshake round trips, slow start round trips, a stall behind
  every loss, RTO timers from 200 ms (1 s for a SYN), 40 ms Nagle stalls, 60 s of TIME-WAIT.
- **On a new connection, round-trip time decides speed**, not bandwidth: about log₂(size ÷ 14.6 KB)
  round trips of slow start. Under loss, rate ≈ (MSS ÷ RTT) × 1.22 ÷ √p.
- **Reuse connections**, keep them warm, and expire them before the shortest idle timeout on the
  path; a new connection per request runs out of ports at about 470 a second per destination.
- **QUIC** moves TCP's machinery onto UDP with independent streams, a one-round-trip encrypted
  handshake and connection IDs. Its gain is from streams; one stream blocks like TCP.

**Papers & engineering blogs**

- [Jacobson and Karels: Congestion Avoidance and Control](https://ee.lbl.gov/papers/congavoid.pdf) (1988) — the 1986 congestion collapse and the ideas in every TCP since.
- [Mathis, Semke, Mahdavi and Ott: The Macroscopic Behavior of the TCP Congestion Avoidance Algorithm](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/fall24/compsci514/readings/mathis-tcpmodel-ccr97.pdf) (1997) — why loss, not bandwidth, caps a long transfer.
- [Cardwell et al.: BBR](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3022184) (ACM Queue, 2016) and [the BBR draft](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr/) — measuring the path instead of reacting to loss.
- [Validation and evaluation of HyStart for Linux](https://netdevconf.info/0x1A/sessions/talk/validation-and-evaluation-of-hystart-for-linux.html) (netdev 0x1A, 2026) — leaving slow start before the first loss.
- [Google: The QUIC transport protocol](https://research.google/pubs/the-quic-transport-protocol-design-and-internet-scale-deployment/) (SIGCOMM 2017) — why QUIC exists, what it improved and what it cost.
- [Uber: Employing QUIC](https://www.uber.com/blog/employing-quic-protocol/) (2019) — tail latency on lossy mobile networks.
- [Discord: 2.5 million concurrent voice users](https://discord.com/blog/how-discord-handles-two-and-half-million-concurrent-voice-users-using-webrtc) (2018) — voice over UDP at scale.
- [GitHub: February 28th DDoS incident report](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/ddos-incident-report/) (2018) — UDP amplification at 1.35 Tbps.
- [Cloudflare: How to stop running out of ephemeral ports](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-to-stop-running-out-of-ephemeral-ports-and-start-to-love-long-lived-connections/) (2022) — port exhaustion and the socket options around it.
- [kubernetes/kubernetes#56903](https://github.com/kubernetes/kubernetes/issues/56903) (2017–2019) — the 5-second DNS delays from a conntrack race.
- [Red Hat: TCP SACK Panic](https://access.redhat.com/security/vulnerabilities/tcpsack) (2019) — CVE-2019-11477, the crash and its mitigations.
- [Cloudflare Radar Year in Review 2025](https://radar.cloudflare.com/year-in-review/2025) — how much traffic uses HTTP/3.

**Explainers**

- [Ilya Grigorik: High Performance Browser Networking, TCP](https://hpbn.co/building-blocks-of-tcp/) and [UDP](https://hpbn.co/building-blocks-of-udp/) — the classic treatment of handshakes, slow start and NAT traversal; written in 2013, before QUIC and BBR.
- [Glenn Fiedler: UDP vs. TCP](https://gafferongames.com/post/udp_vs_tcp/) (2008) — the game developer's case against TCP for real-time data.
