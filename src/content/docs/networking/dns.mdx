## One line changed, three different outages

Imagine you are the most junior engineer at a small payments startup in Bengaluru. On a quiet
Saturday morning the team moves its API, `api.example.in`, from an old virtual machine to a new
load balancer. Your job is the DNS part. You open the DNS provider's dashboard, change one line
from the old address to the new one, and press Save. Then you run `nslookup api.example.in` on
your laptop and it shows the new address. It worked. Ten minutes later, to save money, the team
switches the old machine off.

By lunchtime three different problems arrive, and none of them look like the others.

The support channel fills with users whose app says "cannot reach server". Not all users, maybe
a third of them, and fewer each minute, until about an hour later it stops by itself. Then a
partner bank emails: their payment callbacks to you have failed since 10:15, every single one,
and they are still failing at 4 PM. Their service looked up your address when it started on
Monday and has used it ever since. And your own team lead cannot open `pay.example.in`, a new
subdomain you created that morning, even though it works for everyone else. She had tried the
link before the record existed, and her office's DNS server remembered the answer "no such name".

Nothing was hacked and no server crashed. One line changed, and every one of these failures is
DNS behaving exactly as designed. The record had a TTL of 3,600 seconds that nobody looked at.
Your laptop's `nslookup` asked a server that happened to fetch a fresh copy. The partner's code
ignored TTLs completely. The office resolver was obeying a rule about caching "no" that most
engineers have never heard of.

The foundations page, [What happens when you type a URL](/foundations/how-the-web-works/), showed
DNS as step one of a page load: a quick walk from the root to the answer. This page goes inside
that step. It answers one question: **what is DNS really, who keeps copies of its answers and for
how long, and how do you change it, and depend on it, without an outage?**

## Why this problem exists

Computers on the internet find each other by **IP address**, a number such as `104.20.23.154`.
People, and the code people write, use names such as `api.example.in`. Something has to turn
names into numbers, and keep doing it correctly while the numbers change underneath.

Early on, that something was a single text file. Until the mid-1980s, every machine on the
ARPANET downloaded a file called `HOSTS.TXT` from one central computer at the Stanford Research
Institute, and anyone who wanted a new name emailed the people who maintained it. A remnant of
that file still sits on your laptop today, as `/etc/hosts` (on Windows,
`C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts`). It worked for a few hundred machines. It could not work
for millions: one office could not keep up with every change, and every machine downloading the
whole file again for each change was hopeless.

The **Domain Name System** (DNS), designed by Paul Mockapetris and written down in its current
form in 1987 as [RFC 1034](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034) and
[RFC 1035](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1035), replaced the file with three ideas that still
define it:

1. **A tree of names.** Names are read right to left, from the general to the specific:
   `api.example.in` is `api`, inside `example`, inside `in`, inside the root.
2. **Delegation.** Each part of the tree can be handed to a different owner, who runs it on
   their own servers. Nobody needs permission from the centre to add `api` under `example.in`.
3. **Caching with an expiry date.** Every answer says how long it may be kept, so the servers at
   the top are not asked the same question billions of times.

These three ideas are why DNS scales to every device on the planet. They are also exactly what
went wrong in our story. Because of delegation, the truth about your name lives on your
provider's servers and nowhere else. Because of caching, copies of the old truth live on
thousands of servers you do not control, and you cannot call them back.

A few terms will come up on every line of this page:

- A **domain name** is a sequence of **labels** separated by dots, such as `api.example.in`. Each
  label is at most 63 bytes and the whole name at most 255.
- A **fully qualified domain name** (FQDN) is a name written all the way to the root. Strictly,
  it ends in a dot, `api.example.in.`, because the root's own label is empty. Tools usually hide
  the dot; DNS software does not, and it matters more often than you would expect.
- A **record** (resource record, or RR) is one fact about a name: "`api.example.in` has the IPv4
  address `192.0.2.20`". Each record has a name, a **type** (A, AAAA, CNAME, MX…), a value and a
  **TTL**.
- The **TTL** (time to live) is how many seconds anyone may keep a copy of the record before
  they must ask again.
- An **authoritative server** holds the original records for part of the tree and answers with
  the truth. A **recursive resolver** answers on behalf of clients by asking authoritative
  servers and caching what it learns.

Who gets hurt when this goes wrong? Everyone, at once. DNS sits in front of every connection.
When it fails, a perfectly healthy service is invisible, which is why "it's always DNS" became a
running joke among operations engineers. The cost is also sneaky: DNS failures are partial (some
users, some resolvers), delayed (they start when a cache expires, not when you made the change)
and self-healing (they stop when the next cache expires), which makes them hard to see and very
hard to debug.

## The obvious picture, and why it breaks

The picture most of us start with is a lookup table. You ask "what is the address of
`api.example.in`?", a server checks its table, and you get the answer. If the table changes, the
next lookup gets the new value. The code we write first matches that picture, and comes in two
flavours.

The first looks the name up once, when the program starts, and keeps the address in a variable.
The second looks the name up before every single request, "to always be fresh". Here are both,
talking to a small DNS server we wrote for this page that serves the made-up zone
`shop.example.test` on your own machine (it is in "Build it yourself" below):

Each breaks in its own way.

1. **Resolve once is the partner bank.** It works perfectly until the address changes, then fails
   on every request until someone restarts the process. Nothing in its logs says "DNS"; they say
   "connection refused" or "timed out", which sends people looking at the network and the server
   first.
2. **Resolve every time is correct and expensive.** Every request now waits for a lookup first,
   and every request adds load to the DNS servers. At 2,000 requests per second, that is 2,000
   DNS queries per second from one client. In a real system, the operating system's cache usually
   hides some of this, which is exactly the point: the right answer is a cache, with the expiry
   the record itself asks for.

Before we go further, here are the neighbours DNS is most often confused with.

| These are often confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| **Registrar** vs **DNS provider** vs **hosting** | The registrar is where you rent the name and say which name servers serve it. The DNS provider runs those servers and stores your records. Hosting is the machines your records point to. They are often three different companies, and each can fail on its own. |
| **Recursive resolver** vs **authoritative server** | A recursive resolver (your ISP's, 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8) answers anyone's question by asking around and caching. An authoritative server answers only for its own zones, from its own records, and never asks anyone else. |
| **DNS** vs **`/etc/hosts`** | The hosts file is a local override that the operating system checks first. It is not DNS at all, which is why `dig` ignores it and your browser does not. |
| **DNS round robin** vs **load balancing** | Returning several addresses spreads clients roughly across servers. It knows nothing about load or health, and clients cache their pick. A load balancer decides per connection. |
| **DNSSEC** vs **DoH / DoT** | DNSSEC signs records so a resolver can prove they were not forged. DNS over HTTPS and DNS over TLS encrypt the conversation between you and your resolver so nobody on the path can read or change it. Different problems; you can have either without the other. |

## The core idea

Think of a large company's internal phone directory, before computers. The directory is not one
book. Each department keeps its own page and is the only one allowed to change it. A small front
office knows only which department owns which page. When you need a number you have never called,
you ask a receptionist. The receptionist asks the front office which department to ask, asks that
department, writes the number on a sticky note with "valid until Friday" on it, and gives you a
copy. Next time anyone asks the same receptionist, they read it off the sticky note.

That is DNS. The departments are **zones** run by different owners, the front office is the
parent zone that **delegates**, the receptionist is your **recursive resolver**, and the sticky
note's date is the **TTL**.

The analogy breaks in two places, and both matter.

- **There are millions of receptionists, and you cannot phone them.** When a department changes a
  number, nobody tells the receptionists. Each one keeps using its sticky note until the date on it
  passes. There is no "recall all copies" button, anywhere, at any price.
- **The department can give different answers to different callers.** A phone directory gives
  everyone the same number. DNS servers often answer based on who is asking, so users in Mumbai
  and Frankfurt get different addresses for the same name. That is a feature, and it is how CDNs
  send you to a nearby server.

Put precisely: **DNS is a hierarchical, delegated database. The tree of names is cut into zones,
each served by its owner's authoritative servers, and each parent says which servers own each
child. Clients almost never talk to those servers. They read through layers of caches, and every
answer, including the answer "this name does not exist", carries a TTL that says how long it may
be copied. Changing DNS means changing the truth in one place and then waiting for every copy to
expire.**

The diagram shows the tree. Look at the dashed outlines: each one is a zone, owned and run by
one operator.

## How it works, piece by piece

We will build the full picture in seven pieces: the tree and its delegations, what a zone holds,
who asks whom, how caching and TTLs behave, how "no" is cached, how DNS travels on the wire, and
the two newer layers of privacy and trust. Then we look at DNS used as a traffic switch.

Where we measured something, we say so. The real-world numbers come from our `lookup` script,
asking Cloudflare's public resolver 1.1.1.1 from a network in India in October 2026; the toy
numbers come from our own small server.

### Names, zones and delegation

A **zone** is a piece of the name tree that is managed as one unit, with one set of
authoritative servers. A zone usually starts at a name its owner bought, such as `example.in`,
and covers every name below it until the owner hands a subtree to someone else. That hand-over is
a **delegation**, and the point where it happens is a **zone cut**.

The parent marks a delegation with **NS records** (name server records): "the zone `example.in`
is served by `ns1.example.in`". That creates a puzzle. To find `ns1.example.in` you must ask the
servers for `example.in`, which is `ns1.example.in`. The parent breaks the loop by also
publishing the name server's address, called a **glue record**.

<Figure caption="The parent zone holds only the delegation: an NS record and the glue address for the child's name server. The child zone holds the real records, including its own copy of the NS record.">
  <Delegation />
</Figure>

So the NS record exists twice, once in the parent and once in the child, and in practice they can
disagree when someone updates one and forgets the other. When you buy a domain and "set its name
servers" at your registrar, you are editing the parent's copy: the registrar sends the change to
the registry that runs the TLD zone.

At the very top is the **root zone**. As of October 2026 it delegates 1,437 **top-level domains**
(TLDs) such as `in`, `com` and `dev` ([IANA](https://data.iana.org/TLD/tlds-alpha-by-domain.txt)),
and is served by 13 root server identities run by 12 organisations from 2,047 sites
([root-servers.org](https://root-servers.org/)). The foundations page walked through the referrals
from the root down. What matters here is the TTL on those referrals: the `.com` delegation may be
cached for two days, so busy resolvers almost never need the root at all.

**A worked example: why "changing name servers takes 48 hours".** When you move a domain from one
DNS provider to another, you change the NS records in the parent. The `.com` servers, for example, serve
those records with a TTL of 172,800 seconds, two days. A resolver that cached the old delegation
an hour before your change will keep asking your old provider for up to 47 more hours. That is
the real source of the "allow 48 hours for DNS changes" warning. Changing an A record inside a
zone you keep is a different and much faster operation: it waits only for that record's own TTL.
The safe move is to keep the old provider serving identical records for at least two days after
switching.

### What a zone holds: records

A zone is, in the end, a list of records. Many DNS providers can import and export them in the
**zone file** format from RFC 1035. Here is a realistic small zone; `@` means "the zone's own
name", and names without a trailing dot get the zone name appended:

```text title="example.in zone file (illustrative)"
$ORIGIN example.in.
$TTL 3600                                   ; default TTL for records below
@        SOA   ns1.example.in. hostmaster.example.in. (
               2026101801 ; serial: bump on every change
               7200 900 1209600            ; refresh, retry, expire (for secondary servers)
               300 )                        ; minimum: how long others may cache "no such name"
@        NS    ns1.example.in.
@        NS    ns1.other-dns-provider.net.  ; a second provider, so one outage is not total
@        A     192.0.2.10
www      CNAME shop.cdn.net.
api 300  A     192.0.2.20                   ; a shorter TTL: this one may need to move
api 300  AAAA  2001:db8::20
@        MX    10 mail.example.in.
@        TXT   "v=spf1 include:_spf.mailhost.example -all"
@        CAA   0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
```

The types you will meet in your first year:

| Type | Holds | Used for | Real example we saw |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** / **AAAA** | An IPv4 / IPv6 address | Where to connect | `example.com` → `104.20.23.154`, `2606:4700:10::6814:179a` |
| **CNAME** | Another name (an alias) | Pointing your name at a provider's name | `www.github.com` → `github.com` |
| **NS** | A name server | Delegation | `example.com` → `hera.ns.cloudflare.com` |
| **SOA** | Zone metadata | Serial number, timers, negative-cache TTL | `example.com` minimum 1,800 s |
| **MX** | A mail server and a preference | Where to deliver email; lowest number first | `gmail.com` → `5 gmail-smtp-in.l.google.com` |
| **TXT** | Free text | Email anti-spoofing (SPF, DKIM), proving domain ownership | `example.com` → `v=spf1 -all` |
| **CAA** | Which certificate authorities may issue | Stopping a stray CA from issuing for you | `google.com` → `issue "pki.goog"` |
| **HTTPS** / **SVCB** | Connection hints | HTTP versions, ports, addresses before connecting | `cloudflare.com` → `alpn=h3,h2` |
| **PTR** | A name, for an address | Reverse lookups, mostly logging and email checks | |
| **SRV** | Host and port for a service | Some protocols (SIP, XMPP, LDAP) find servers this way | |

Two of these deserve a closer look. A **CNAME** says "this name is really that name": the
resolver must start again with the target. Our `lookup` script saw it for `www.github.com`:

```text title="lookup.py www.github.com A (output)"
www.github.com A via UDP 1.1.1.1: NOERROR [rd ra] 73 bytes in 14 ms
    www.github.com.              2998  CNAME  github.com.
    github.com.                    46  A      20.207.73.82
```

The alias may be kept for about 50 minutes, the address for only 46 seconds, so the useful
lifetime of the whole answer is 46 seconds. A chain is only as fresh as its shortest link.

<Figure caption="A CNAME sends the resolver to another name, often in another company's zone. The combined answer lives only as long as its shortest TTL. And the zone apex cannot be a CNAME at all.">
  <CnameChain />
</Figure>

The red box is a rule that surprises everyone once: a name with a CNAME may have no other records
([RFC 1034 §3.6.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034)). The **apex** of a zone, the bare
`example.in`, always has SOA and NS records, so it can never be a CNAME. Yet hosting platforms
tell you to "point your domain at `yoursite.platform.app`". Providers work around this with
non-standard **ALIAS** or **ANAME** records, or Cloudflare's **CNAME flattening**: the DNS
provider resolves the target itself and answers with plain A and AAAA records
([Cloudflare docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/dns/cname-flattening/)). The standard fix is
the newer **HTTPS** record ([RFC 9460](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9460), November 2023),
which can alias at the apex. It can also tell a browser, before it connects, that the server speaks
HTTP/3. That is what `cloudflare.com`'s record above says with `alpn=h3,h2`.

### Who asks whom: stubs, resolvers and authoritative servers

A lookup involves four roles, and the confusing part is that three of them keep caches with
different rules.

<Figure caption="Your app asks the stub resolver in the OS, which asks a recursive resolver, which on a miss asks the authoritative servers. Only the authoritative servers hold the truth; every layer above holds copies, each with its own cache rules.">
  <Roles />
</Figure>

- Your **app** calls a library function, usually `getaddrinfo()`. Some runtimes add their own
  cache on top: browsers keep one, the Java runtime keeps one, and so can your own code.
- The **stub resolver** is the small client inside the operating system. It reads
  `/etc/resolv.conf` (or the Windows network settings) to learn which recursive resolver to use,
  checks `/etc/hosts`, and often keeps a cache for the whole machine (systemd-resolved on many
  Linux systems, the DNS Client service on Windows). It does not walk the tree; it asks one
  question and waits.
- The **recursive resolver** does the real work. Your home router usually forwards to your
  internet provider's resolver; companies run their own; public ones such as 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8
  and 9.9.9.9 serve anyone. Its cache is shared by thousands or millions of users, which is why a
  popular name is almost always answered from memory.
- The **authoritative servers** answer from their zones and keep no cache.

Two practical consequences follow from these roles. First, **the TTL you see depends on who you
ask.** A resolver tells you how much time is *left* on its copy, not the original value. In our
runs, three different caches answered `example.com` with 264, 81 and 300 seconds remaining at the
same moment. Only an authoritative server shows the real TTL.

Second, **your test is not your users' test.** When you run `nslookup` after a change, you are
asking one resolver, which may have fetched a fresh copy a second ago, while millions of users sit
behind resolvers that fetched the old one ten minutes ago. That is why the laptop in our story
showed the new address while a third of the users could not connect.

### Caching and TTLs: why "propagation" is not a thing

People say a DNS change "takes time to propagate", which suggests the new record spreading
outward from server to server. Nothing like that happens. When you change a record, the change
reaches your authoritative servers in seconds. Nobody pushes it anywhere else. Each cache that
holds a copy keeps serving the old value until its own copy expires, and then fetches the new one
the next time someone asks. "Propagation" is millions of independent timers running out.

That gives a simple rule: **after a change, old answers can be served for up to one TTL, the TTL
the record had before the change.** Lowering the TTL at the moment you change the address does
nothing for copies already cached with the old TTL.

Watch it happen. Five resolver caches each fetched their copy at a different moment, so their
timers run out at different times. The sixth row is an app like our partner bank's, which
resolved once at startup.

<DnsCacheSim mode="propagation" />

The picture you watched is the whole of "propagation". With the default TTL of 300 seconds,
the last cache switches up to five minutes after the change, at a moment set by when it happened
to fetch, not by anything you did. Move the TTL slider to 3,600 and the same wait becomes an hour.
Lower the lookups slider to 1 per minute and some caches switch even later than one TTL after the
change, because a cache only fetches again when someone asks.

**A worked example.** Our story's record had a TTL of 3,600 seconds and was changed at 10:05. A
resolver that fetched at 10:04 keeps the old address until 11:04. The team switched off the old
server at 10:15, so every user behind such a resolver failed from 10:15 until their resolver's
copy expired, sometime before 11:05. If caches fetched at random moments, their expiries spread
evenly across that hour, so the failures faded out roughly in a straight line: about two thirds of
users failing at 10:25, a third at 10:45, none after 11:05, which is what support saw.

The fix is a plan, drawn below. The key move is that the slow wait happens *before* the change,
while nothing is broken.

<Figure caption="Lower the TTL a day or two ahead and wait out the old TTL. Then the change itself only needs the short TTL to reach everyone, and the old server can be switched off once its traffic falls to zero.">
  <MigrationTimeline />
</Figure>

Three things make this harder in real life:

- **Not every cache obeys the TTL.** Resolvers may enforce a minimum, a maximum, or both. Some
  apps never re-resolve at all. Browsers keep connections open, so a browser may keep talking to
  the old address over a connection it opened before the change, whatever DNS now says. Watch the
  old server's traffic rather than the clock.
- **Applications cache too.** The Java runtime caches successful lookups for 30 seconds by
  default; older Java versions with a security manager installed cached them for ever
  ([`InetAddressCachePolicy`](https://code.yawk.at/java/8/sun/net/InetAddressCachePolicy.java)).
  Node's `dns.lookup()` has no cache of its own but uses the operating system's. nginx resolves a
  hostname written in `proxy_pass` once, when it starts.
- **The serial matters for secondaries.** If a zone is copied to secondary servers by **zone
  transfer**, the secondaries only pull a new copy when the SOA serial number goes up. Forget to
  bump it, and half your name servers keep serving yesterday's records. Most hosted DNS providers
  handle this for you; self-hosted zones are where it bites.

### Negative caching: "no" is cached too

The third failure in our story is the one almost nobody expects. When a resolver asks for a name
that does not exist, it gets back a negative answer, and **it caches that too**
([RFC 2308](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308)).

There are two kinds of "no":

- **NXDOMAIN** (result code 3): the name does not exist at all, for any type.
- **NODATA**: the name exists, but has no records of the type you asked for. There is no special
  code for this. The answer says NOERROR, with an empty answer section.

Either way, the authoritative server includes its zone's SOA record, and the resolver may cache
the "no" for the smaller of the SOA record's own TTL and its last field, the **minimum**. That
field's name is a historical accident; today it means only "how long to cache negative answers".
RFC 2308 suggests resolvers cap negative caching at one to three hours.

Our `ttl_cache` demo reproduces the team lead's problem. It asks for `beta.shop.example.test`
before the operator creates it; the toy zone's SOA minimum is 5 seconds:

```text title="ttl_cache.py output, part 2"
t=4.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: NXDOMAIN, cached for 5.0 s more
t=4.1s  operator: created beta.shop.example.test. -> 198.51.100.30
t=5.6s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 3.5 s more
t=7.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 2.0 s more
t=8.6s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 0.5 s more
t=10.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: 198.51.100.30
```

The record existed from 4.1 s, yet the cache kept saying "no such name" for five more seconds.
With a real zone the numbers are bigger: `example.com`'s SOA allows a "no" to be cached for 1,800
seconds, and `wikipedia.org`'s for 3,600. Anyone who tried the link before you created the record
may wait up to an hour.

There is one more twist we found while testing. Asking for a name that does not exist under
`wikipedia.org` returns NXDOMAIN, as you would expect. Asking for one under `example.com` returns
NOERROR with an empty answer:

```text title="lookup.py, two names that do not exist"
no-such-name.wikipedia.org A via UDP 1.1.1.1: NXDOMAIN [rd ra] 116 bytes in 141 ms
    (no answer; the SOA says: cache this 'no' for 3600 s)
no-such-name.example.com A via UDP 1.1.1.1: NOERROR [rd ra] 115 bytes in 16 ms
    (no answer; the SOA says: cache this 'no' for 1800 s)
```

That is deliberate. `example.com` is signed with DNSSEC on Cloudflare, which answers "no such
name" as a signed NODATA, a technique now standardised as **compact denial of existence**
([RFC 9824](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9824)). It makes the proof of non-existence smaller
and cheaper to sign. The practical lesson is that code checking `rcode == NXDOMAIN` to mean
"does not exist" is wrong for some zones; treat "no records of the type I asked for" as the
signal.

### On the wire: UDP, TCP and message size

Every DNS message, question or answer, has the same shape, whether it travels over UDP, TCP, TLS
or HTTPS.

<Figure caption="A 12-byte header (ID, flags, result code, counts), then the question, then three sections of records. The OPT record in the additional section says how large a UDP answer the sender can take.">
  <Message />
</Figure>

The header's **ID** is a random 16-bit number the answer must repeat, which is how a client
matches answers to questions. The flags say whether this is an answer (QR), whether it came from
an authoritative server (AA), whether recursion was asked for and offered (RD, RA), and whether
it was truncated (TC). Our resilient resolver below also checks that the question in the answer
matches the one it sent, so a stray or forged packet is not believed.

DNS normally uses **UDP** on port 53: one packet out, one packet back, no connection set-up, so a
lookup costs one round trip. The original standard limited UDP answers to 512 bytes. **EDNS(0)**
([RFC 6891](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6891)) lets a client say "I can take more" with an
OPT pseudo-record. How much more is a practical question: packets larger than the path allows get
split into IP fragments, and many networks drop fragments. Since
[DNS Flag Day 2020](https://www.dnsflagday.net/2020/), major resolver software defaults to 1,232
bytes, which is IPv6's minimum packet size of 1,280 bytes minus 48 bytes of headers.

When an answer does not fit, the server does not send part of it. It sends the header with the
**TC** flag set, and the client asks again over **TCP**. Our toy server holds one deliberately
large record, 12 long TXT strings, to show exactly that:

[RFC 9210](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9210) (2022) is blunt about it: every DNS resolver
and server must support both UDP and TCP, and network operators must allow both. "DNS is UDP" is
the most common half-truth in networking.

### Private DNS: DoT, DoH and DoQ

Plain DNS is readable by anyone on the path: the café Wi-Fi, your internet provider, any network
in between. It can also be changed by them. Three standards wrap the same DNS message in
encryption, between you and your recursive resolver:

- **DNS over TLS** (DoT, [RFC 7858](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7858), 2016): DNS inside a
  TLS connection on port 853. Its own port makes it simple for a network to recognise, and to
  block.
- **DNS over HTTPS** (DoH, [RFC 8484](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8484), 2018): the DNS
  message is the body of an HTTPS request with the type `application/dns-message`. It looks like
  any other web traffic on port 443. Browsers use it.
- **DNS over QUIC** (DoQ, [RFC 9250](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9250), 2022): DNS on QUIC,
  the transport under HTTP/3.

DoH is less exotic than it sounds. Our `lookup` script sends the very same bytes it would send
over UDP, as the body of a `POST`:

```text title="node lookup.mjs (the end of the output, records trimmed)"
example.com A via UDP 1.1.1.1: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 18 ms
example.com A via DoH cloudflare-dns.com: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 213 ms
example.com A via DoH dns.google: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 67 ms
example.com A via DoH cloudflare-dns.com: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 17 ms
```

The first DoH query to Cloudflare took 213 ms because it had to open a TCP connection and complete a TLS handshake
first. The second, on the same open connection, took 17 ms, the same as plain UDP. Encrypted DNS
is only slow when you keep opening new connections.

One thing encryption does not do: it protects the conversation with *your resolver*, not the
answer itself. Your resolver could still be wrong, or lying, and it sees every name you look up.
Choosing a DoH provider moves trust; it does not remove it.

### Trust: DNSSEC

DNS was designed in a friendlier era. A resolver believes an answer if the ID and question match,
and an attacker who can guess those, or who sits on the path, can inject a false one. A false
answer in a resolver's cache sends every user of that resolver to the attacker for the whole TTL.
This is **cache poisoning**. Random IDs and random source ports make it much harder from outside
the path, but do not make it impossible.

**DNSSEC** ([RFC 4033](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4033) and its companions) adds
signatures. Each signed zone publishes its public key in a **DNSKEY** record and a signature,
**RRSIG**, over each set of records. Each parent publishes a **DS** record: a hash of its child's
key. A **validating resolver** starts from one key it already trusts, the root's, and checks the
chain down.

<Figure caption="The root's key is built into validating resolvers. Each parent vouches for its child's key with a DS record, and each zone signs its own records. One broken link and a validating resolver returns SERVFAIL rather than an unverifiable answer.">
  <DnssecChain />
</Figure>

The root's key itself was replaced recently, for only the second time. As of October 2026, the
new key, KSK-2024, began signing the root zone on 11 October 2026 at 14:00 UTC, and ICANN reports
no widespread disruption ([ICANN](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/ksk-rollover-en)). It had
been published in the root zone since January 2025, so validating resolvers could learn it in
advance.

DNSSEC's strength is also its risk. Validation fails closed: if any signature in the chain is
wrong, a validating resolver returns SERVFAIL, and the name disappears for its users, even though
every server is up. The Slack incident on the foundations page and the `.de` incident below are
both this failure. DNSSEC gives authenticity, not privacy: the records are signed, not encrypted.

### DNS as a traffic switch

Because authoritative servers can answer differently depending on who asks, DNS is also one of
the simplest ways to steer traffic across the world.

<Figure caption="The same name, two answers. The authoritative server sees roughly where each question comes from and returns the nearest healthy site, with a short TTL so it can change its mind.">
  <GeoDns />
</Figure>

Managed DNS services offer this as **routing policies**. As of October 2026, Amazon Route 53
offers simple, weighted (send 10% to the new version), latency-based (the region with the lowest
measured latency), geolocation, geoproximity, IP-based, failover (send everything to a standby
when health checks fail) and multivalue (up to eight healthy addresses at random)
([Route 53 docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/routing-policy.html)).

The catch is that the authoritative server sees the *resolver's* address, not the user's. A user
in Chennai using a public resolver that happens to reach the server from Singapore looks like a
user in Singapore. **EDNS Client Subnet** (ECS, [RFC 7871](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7871))
lets the resolver pass along part of the user's address, typically the first 24 bits of an IPv4
address. It improves the answer and leaks a little of who is asking, which is why its own authors
recommend it be off unless it clearly helps, and why some privacy-focused resolvers do not send
it.

DNS steering is coarse. Every decision is cached for a TTL, so failover through DNS takes at least
one TTL plus health-check time, and clients that pinned an address never fail over at all. That
is why records used for failover usually have TTLs of 30 to 60 seconds, and why the load balancer
behind the address still does the fine-grained work.

## Choosing TTLs, side by side

Almost every DNS decision you make as an application engineer comes down to choosing TTLs. A
short TTL lets you change things quickly; a long one makes lookups faster, survives an outage of
your DNS provider longer, and costs less. The simulation runs the same change in two worlds, with
the same traffic. The only difference is the TTL.

<DnsCacheSim mode="compare" />

The trade-off is visible in one glance: the 1-minute world switches almost at once and asks the
authoritative server about sixty times more often; the 1-hour world takes a full hour to switch.
That ratio is close to the ratio of the TTLs. For a popular name it matters less than you might
think, because one shared resolver's query serves thousands of users. For your DNS bill, which
many providers charge per query, and for how long your name survives a DNS provider outage, it
matters a lot: when the authoritative servers are unreachable, cached copies are all that keep
your name alive, and they last one TTL.

Here is where I would start, and why:

| Record | Typical TTL | Why |
|---|---|---|
| NS and their glue | 1–2 days (set by the TLD) | Rarely change; long caching keeps load off the TLD servers |
| Apex and `www` behind a CDN | 300 s (often set by the CDN) | The CDN's own name changes behind it; 5 minutes balances agility and load |
| API or app endpoint you control | 60–300 s | You will want to move it; lower to 60 s ahead of planned work |
| Failover or weighted records | 30–60 s | The whole point is to change quickly when health checks fail |
| MX, TXT for email and verification | 1 hour – 1 day | Change rarely; a long TTL rides out DNS provider outages |
| SOA minimum (negative caching) | 60–300 s | Short enough that a new name works quickly for people who tried early |

And here are the caching layers between your change and your users, because each treats TTLs
differently:

| Layer | Honours the TTL? | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Recursive resolvers | Yes, within their own minimum and maximum | Remaining TTL counts down; some keep serving expired answers when your servers are down (serve-stale) |
| OS stub cache | Usually | Windows, macOS and systemd-resolved cache; flush with `ipconfig /flushdns` and friends when testing |
| Browsers | Mostly, with their own limits | Open connections keep using the old address regardless of DNS |
| Language runtimes | Varies | Java caches 30 s by default; older versions with a security manager cached for ever |
| Your own code, proxies | Only if you make them | Resolve-once is the classic bug; nginx `proxy_pass` with a fixed hostname resolves once at start |

**How to choose.** Use long TTLs for anything that rarely changes, short TTLs for anything you
expect to move, and remember the rule from the migration plan: the TTL that matters on the day of a
change is the one set the day before.

### Step 2: a cache that honours TTLs, including "no"

The correct version of our client from the start of the page is a cache keyed by name and type.

```python title="ttl_cache.py"
# runnable version: examples/dns/python/ttl_cache.py
class CachingResolver:
    def __init__(self, server: tuple[str, int]):
        self.server = server
        self.cache: dict[tuple[str, str], Entry] = {}
        self.hits = self.misses = 0

    def resolve(self, name: str, rtype: str = "A") -> list[str]:
        key = (name.lower(), rtype)
        entry = self.cache.get(key)
        if entry and time.monotonic() < entry.expires:
            self.hits += 1
        else:
            self.misses += 1
            entry = self._ask(name, rtype)
            self.cache[key] = entry
        if not entry.addresses:
            raise NameNotFound(f"{entry.rcode}, cached for {entry.expires - time.monotonic():.1f} s more")
        return entry.addresses

    def _ask(self, name: str, rtype: str) -> Entry:
        _, query = w.build_query(name, rtype)
        reply = w.parse_message(w.ask_udp(*self.server, query))
        now = time.monotonic()
        # Follow any CNAMEs in the answer; the chain is only as fresh as its shortest-lived link.
        records = [r for r in reply.answer if r.type in (rtype, "CNAME")]
        addresses = [r.value for r in records if r.type == rtype]
        if reply.rcode == "NOERROR" and addresses:
            return Entry(addresses, "NOERROR", now + min(r.ttl for r in records))
        # NXDOMAIN (no such name) or NODATA (name exists, no records of this type).
        # Negative TTL = the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field (RFC 2308).
        soa = next((r for r in reply.authority if r.type == "SOA"), None)
        negative_ttl = min(soa.ttl, soa.data["minimum"]) if soa else 0  # no SOA: do not cache
        return Entry([], reply.rcode if reply.rcode != "NOERROR" else "NODATA", now + negative_ttl)
```

```js title="ttl-cache.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/dns/js/ttl-cache.mjs
class CachingResolver {
  cache = new Map(); // "name|type" -> { addresses, rcode, expires }
  hits = 0;
  misses = 0;

  constructor(server) {
    this.server = server;
  }

  async resolve(name, type = 'A') {
    const key = `${name.toLowerCase()}|${type}`;
    let entry = this.cache.get(key);
    if (entry && performance.now() < entry.expires) {
      this.hits++;
    } else {
      this.misses++;
      entry = await this.ask(name, type);
      this.cache.set(key, entry);
    }
    if (!entry.addresses.length) {
      throw new NameNotFound(`${entry.rcode}, cached for ${((entry.expires - performance.now()) / 1000).toFixed(1)} s more`);
    }
    return entry.addresses;
  }

  async ask(name, type) {
    const { query } = w.buildQuery(name, type);
    const reply = w.parseMessage(await w.askUdp(...this.server, query));
    const now = performance.now();
    // Follow any CNAMEs in the answer; the chain is only as fresh as its shortest-lived link.
    const records = reply.answer.filter((r) => r.type === type || r.type === 'CNAME');
    const addresses = records.filter((r) => r.type === type).map((r) => r.value);
    if (reply.rcode === 'NOERROR' && addresses.length) {
      return { addresses, rcode: 'NOERROR', expires: now + Math.min(...records.map((r) => r.ttl)) * 1000 };
    }
    // NXDOMAIN (no such name) or NODATA (name exists, no records of this type).
    // Negative TTL = the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field (RFC 2308).
    const soa = reply.authority.find((r) => r.type === 'SOA');
    const negativeTtl = soa ? Math.min(soa.ttl, soa.data.minimum) : 0; // no SOA: do not cache
    return { addresses: [], rcode: reply.rcode === 'NOERROR' ? 'NODATA' : reply.rcode, expires: now + negativeTtl * 1000 };
  }
}
```

### Step 3: a resolver that survives bad days

The production-shaped version adds what goes wrong in real life: an upstream resolver that is
down, an answer that does not fit in UDP, a reply that does not match the question, a TTL that is
silly, and a moment when every DNS server is unreachable. The last one uses **serve-stale**
([RFC 8767](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767)): when nobody answers, an expired answer is
better than none. The RFC suggests keeping expired data for one to three days and handing it out
with a TTL of 30 seconds so the client asks again soon.

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="resilient_resolver.py"
# runnable version: examples/dns/python/resilient_resolver.py
    def resolve(self, name: str, rtype: str = "A") -> list[str]:
        key, now = (name.lower(), rtype), time.monotonic()
        entry = self.cache.get(key)
        if entry and now < entry.expires:
            return entry.values
        try:
            values, ttl = self._ask_upstreams(name, rtype)
        except LookupFailed as err:
            if entry and now < entry.stale_until:  # serve stale: an old answer beats no answer
                entry.expires = now + STALE_TTL
                print(f"    ! every upstream failed ({err}); serving the expired answer for {STALE_TTL} s")
                return entry.values
            raise
        ttl = max(MIN_TTL, min(MAX_TTL, ttl))
        self.cache[key] = Entry(values, now + ttl, now + ttl + MAX_STALE)
        return values

    # _ask_upstreams() tries each upstream in turn; SERVFAIL or a timeout moves to the next one.

    def _exchange(self, host: str, port: int, name: str, rtype: str) -> w.Message:
        msg_id, query = w.build_query(name, rtype)
        reply = w.parse_message(w.ask_udp(host, port, query, timeout=TIMEOUT))
        if reply.tc:  # too big for UDP: same question again over TCP
            print(f"    UDP answer from {host}:{port} was truncated ({reply.size} bytes, TC=1); retrying over TCP")
            reply = w.parse_message(w.ask_tcp(host, port, query, timeout=TIMEOUT))
            print(f"    TCP answer: {reply.size} bytes, {len(reply.answer)} records")
        if reply.id != msg_id or reply.question[:1] != [(name.lower(), rtype)]:
            raise ConnectionError("answer does not match the question")  # possible spoofing
        return reply
```

### Step 4: a tiny dig, over UDP and over HTTPS

```python title="lookup.py"
def ask_doh(url: str, name: str, rtype: str) -> None:
    """DNS over HTTPS (RFC 8484): the same binary message, POSTed over HTTPS. ID 0 for cacheability."""
    _, query = w.build_query(name, rtype, msg_id=0)
    request = urllib.request.Request(url, data=query, method="POST", headers={
        "Content-Type": "application/dns-message", "Accept": "application/dns-message"})

        # Seen on Windows: Python uses the system trust store, which may not hold every root yet.
```

The message ID is 0 because RFC 8484 asks for it: identical questions then produce identical HTTP
requests, which HTTP caches can share. The `try` in the Python version is there for a real reason
we hit while testing. On Windows, Python checks certificates against the system's trust store,
which on our machine did not yet hold the root that `cloudflare-dns.com` chains to, so that one
request failed. Node ships its own list of roots and worked. Same code, different trust stores.

directly, which shows the real TTL and the truth after a change. On Windows, `nslookup` and the
PowerShell command `Resolve-DnsName` do most of the same. When you debug a change, always ask the
authoritative server and at least two public resolvers, not only your own machine.

## How real systems do it

The protocol has barely changed since 1987; what has changed is the scale and the habits around
it. Facts in this section change over time, so each is marked with when we checked.

**Public resolvers live everywhere at once.** 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8 and 9.9.9.9 are each announced from
many cities by **anycast**: the same address in many places, with internet routing delivering
each packet to the nearest one. That is why our UDP lookups to 1.1.1.1 from India usually took
12 to 20 ms when the answer was already cached. It is also why the remaining TTLs differed between runs: we were often talking to different
caches behind the same address.

**Big zones can be served by more than one provider.** The standard answer to the 2016 Dyn attack
(below) is to list name servers from two independent DNS providers in the NS set, so one
provider's outage leaves the other answering. It costs effort: both providers must hold identical
records, and features such as GeoDNS and DNSSEC signing must work the same way on both.

**Kubernetes leans on DNS for everything inside the cluster.** Every Service gets a name, and
CoreDNS answers for it. Each pod's resolver is configured like this
([Kubernetes docs](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/services-networking/dns-pod-service/)):

```text title="/etc/resolv.conf inside a pod"
nameserver 10.32.0.10
search my-namespace.svc.cluster.local svc.cluster.local cluster.local
options ndots:5
```

`ndots:5` means "a name with fewer than five dots might be a short internal name, so try every
search suffix first". A lookup of `api.stripe.com`, with two dots, is tried as
`api.stripe.com.my-namespace.svc.cluster.local`, then `api.stripe.com.svc.cluster.local`, then
`api.stripe.com.cluster.local`, each failing, before the real name is asked. With A and AAAA
asked separately, one external lookup becomes eight queries. The fix is to write external names
fully qualified with a trailing dot (`api.stripe.com.`), or to lower `ndots` for the pod with
`dnsConfig`.

**Proxies resolve once unless told otherwise.** nginx resolves a hostname written directly in
`proxy_pass` when it starts or reloads, and keeps that address. To follow DNS changes you give it a
`resolver` and either put the hostname in a variable or, since nginx 1.27.3, use the `resolve`
parameter on an upstream server, which was previously only in the commercial version
([nginx docs](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html), as of October 2026):

```nginx title="nginx: follow DNS changes for an upstream"
resolver 10.0.0.2 valid=30s;          # re-resolve at most every 30 s, whatever the TTL says

upstream payments {
    zone payments 64k;                # resolved addresses live in shared memory
    server api.partner-bank.example resolve;
}
```

**Runtimes have opinions.** In Node, `dns.lookup()`, which `fetch` and `http` use, calls the
operating system's `getaddrinfo()` on libuv's thread pool, four threads by default, and returns no
TTL; `dns.resolve4()` queries the network directly and can return TTLs
([Node docs](https://nodejs.org/api/dns.html)). Slow DNS can therefore stall unrelated file and
crypto work that shares those threads. Java caches successful lookups for about 30 seconds unless
configured otherwise.

**Signing is spreading, slowly and unevenly.** DNSSEC validation is measured per user by APNIC
Labs and varies enormously by country and by internet provider
([APNIC Labs](https://stats.labs.apnic.net/dnssec)). Browsers have started asking for HTTPS
records, but a 2024 measurement study found they often did not use all the parameters in them
([arXiv 2403.15672](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15672v2)), so treat HTTPS records as a speed-up for
clients that understand them, not something to depend on yet.

## Gotchas and war stories

:::danger[A real outage: the empty record behind half of AWS us-east-1]
On 19–20 October 2025, the DNS record for `dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com` was suddenly empty.
DynamoDB's automation has a Planner that writes DNS "plans" and three independent Enactors that
apply them to Route 53. One Enactor stalled while applying an old plan. Meanwhile a faster one
applied a newer plan and then, as routine clean-up, deleted plans much older than it. The slow
Enactor finally overwrote the regional endpoint with its stale plan, and the clean-up deleted that
plan, removing every address. From 11:48 PM PDT nothing could resolve the endpoint, including many
AWS services that depend on DynamoDB. Engineers found the cause by 12:38 AM and restored DNS by
2:25 AM. Clients recovered as their cached copies of the empty answer expired, by 2:40 AM.
Knock-on effects in EC2 and load balancing lasted until 2:20 PM. AWS disabled the automation
worldwide until the race was fixed
([AWS post-event summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/)).
:::

The lesson is not "automation is bad". Hand-edited DNS fails too. It is that the systems that
*write* DNS deserve the same care as the systems that serve traffic: check that a plan is still
the newest one at the moment you write it, refuse to publish an empty answer for a name that has
always had addresses, and have a tested way to put a known-good record back by hand.

:::danger[A real outage: a TLD's signatures go bad]
On 5 May 2026, during a routine DNSSEC key rollover, DENIC, the registry for `.de`, published
signatures that validating resolvers could not verify. Its new signing system had generated a
different key on each of three hardware security modules but published only one, so about a
third of signatures were valid. Validating resolvers returned SERVFAIL for `.de` names, even for
domains that were not signed themselves. Failures grew for about three hours, from around 19:30
UTC, as cached answers expired. Monitoring had detected the problem, but its alerts were not
acted on. Large resolver operators, Cloudflare among them, temporarily switched off validation for
`.de`, and serve-stale cushioned users meanwhile
([DENIC's analysis](https://blog.denic.de/en/analysis-of-the-dns-outage-on-5-may-2026/),
[Cloudflare's write-up](https://blog.cloudflare.com/de-tld-outage-dnssec/)).
:::

Nobody running a `.de` website did anything wrong. Every name sits under a chain of zones you do
not run, and each can fail for you.

**One DNS provider is one point of failure.** On 21 October 2016, a botnet of hacked cameras and
home routers, called Mirai, flooded Dyn, a large DNS provider, in waves that lasted hours. Twitter,
GitHub, Netflix, Reddit and Spotify became unreachable for many users, although their own servers
were fine; Dyn later reported that much of the traffic came from legitimate resolvers retrying
([APNIC](https://blog.apnic.net/2016/10/26/quick-look-attack-dyn/),
[Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2016/10/27/dyn-ddos-post-mortem/)). A site whose
NS set also listed a second provider still had a server answering for it.

**"It works for me" after a change.** Your laptop, your browser and your office resolver may all
hold different copies. Ask the authoritative server first, then two public resolvers, then the
user's own. Remember the old TTL, not the new one.

**Testing a name before it exists.** The negative cache will remember. Create records first, then
test. If you must test early, ask the authoritative server directly, which never caches.

:::danger[Dangling records are a security hole]
A CNAME that points at a cloud resource you deleted, say `promo.example.in` pointing at an app
name on a hosting platform, is a **dangling record**. If anyone else can claim that resource name
on the platform, they now serve content, and can even get certificates, for your subdomain. This
is **subdomain takeover**, and it is common in organisations that create and delete many
resources ([Microsoft's guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/fundamentals/subdomain-takeover)).
Put "remove the DNS record" on every decommissioning checklist, and audit CNAMEs that point
outside your own zones.
:::

**`ndots` and search domains multiply queries.** Inside Kubernetes, and on any machine with a
`search` line in `resolv.conf`, short names are tried with every suffix first. If your DNS servers
see five times more queries than your app makes, and most are NXDOMAIN, this is why.

**Blocking TCP 53.** A firewall rule that allows only UDP port 53 works for months, until a
signed zone or a long TXT record produces an answer over 1,232 bytes. Then those lookups, and only
those, fail. Allow both.

**The hosts file and VPNs.** A forgotten line in `/etc/hosts`, or a VPN that installs its own
resolver, overrides everything on this page for one machine. Check them when one person sees
something nobody else does.

:::caution[Never resolve once and keep it for ever]
Long-running processes are where resolve-once bites: connection pools, message consumers, proxies,
batch workers that run for weeks. Make sure whatever opens their connections re-resolves within
the TTL. A connection pool that refreshes its connections every few minutes is enough.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

DNS is the right tool for one job, finding the current address for a stable name, and it is very
good at it. Its trade-offs come from the caching that makes it scale.

- **Freshness versus load and resilience.** Short TTLs mean fast changes, more queries, a bigger
  bill, and a shorter survival time when your DNS provider is down. Long TTLs mean the opposite.
  You choose per record.
- **DNS steering is coarse.** It works at the level of resolvers, not users, and every decision is
  cached. For fast failover between servers in one region, a load balancer with health checks
  reacts in seconds; DNS reacts in TTLs. Use DNS to choose a region and the load balancer to choose
  a server.
- **DNSSEC trades availability for authenticity.** It stops forged answers, and a signing mistake
  makes your name vanish for every validating resolver. Sign through a provider that automates key
  rollovers, or do not sign at all; a half-managed DNSSEC setup is worse than none.
- **Encrypted DNS moves trust.** DoH hides your lookups from the network and shows them all to one
  resolver operator.

When should you *not* lean on DNS? For service discovery where endpoints change every few seconds,
such as autoscaling pods or containers starting and stopping, DNS caching works against you; a
service registry or a load balancer is a better fit, a topic of the Service discovery page. For
anything that needs per-request decisions, such as sticky sessions or weighting by live load, DNS
does not see requests at all.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their DNS habits during an outage. The pattern repeats: a record changed in
a hurry, a TTL nobody had looked at in years, an old server switched off too early, and an hour of
partial failures that fix themselves before anyone understands them. Before your next migration,
write down every record involved, its current TTL, every client that might cache it, and who can
change it at 3 AM. That ten-minute list prevents most outages of this kind.
:::

## Interview corner

DNS questions come up in almost every networking and system design interview, usually as a
warm-up. What separates a strong answer is knowing that DNS is a cache with rules, not a table,
and talking about how it fails.

1. **"What happens when you look up a name?"** Stub resolver and OS cache, recursive resolver and
   its cache, then root, TLD and authoritative servers via referrals and glue. Mention that the
   root is rarely asked because referrals are cached for days, that the TTL you see is what is left
   in a cache, and that QNAME minimisation limits what each server learns.
2. **"You changed a DNS record and some users still reach the old server. Why, and what do you
   do?"** Caches hold the old answer for up to the old TTL; some clients ignore TTLs. Keep the old
   server running and watch its traffic; next time, lower the TTL a day ahead. Bonus: connections
   opened before the change keep using the old address.
3. **"How would you use DNS for failover or global load balancing?"** Health-checked records with
   short TTLs, latency or geolocation routing, ECS and its privacy cost, and the limits: failover
   takes at least a TTL, pinned clients never move, so pair it with load balancers.
4. **"Is DNS UDP or TCP?"** Both. UDP by default; TCP when the answer is truncated, for zone
   transfers, and as required by RFC 9210. Mention the 1,232-byte EDNS buffer and why fragments
   are avoided.
5. **"What's the difference between DNSSEC and DNS over HTTPS?"** Authenticity of records, checked
   by the resolver, versus privacy of the conversation between client and resolver. Different
   threats; neither replaces the other.

In system design interviews, DNS is also where you say how users reach your system at all: a name
with a short TTL in front of a global load balancer or CDN, and two DNS providers if the stakes
are high.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Your record has a TTL of 3,600 seconds. At 14:00 you change the address and set the TTL to 60 seconds in the same edit. When can you safely switch off the old server?</summary>

Not until about 15:00, and only after its traffic has dropped to zero. Caches that fetched the
record shortly before 14:00 got it with the old TTL of 3,600 seconds and may keep it until 15:00. The
new 60-second TTL only applies to copies fetched after the change. To make the cut-over fast you
had to lower the TTL at least an hour (the old TTL) before the change.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A colleague created report.example.in at 09:30. At 09:20 they had tried to open it and got "no such name". At 09:35 it still fails for them but works for you. The zone's SOA has TTL 3600 and minimum 900. What is going on, and when will it fix itself?</summary>

Their resolver cached the negative answer at 09:20. Its lifetime is the smaller of the SOA's TTL
and its minimum field: min(3600, 900) = 900 seconds, so the "no" can last until 09:35 at the
latest, plus however long their own OS or browser caches it. Your resolver had never asked, so it
fetched the new record. They can test against the authoritative server directly, or use a
different resolver, until it expires.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why can you not put a CNAME on example.in itself, and what do providers do instead?</summary>

A name with a CNAME may have no other records, and the zone apex always has SOA and NS records.
Providers offer ALIAS or ANAME records, or CNAME flattening: they resolve the target themselves
and answer with ordinary A and AAAA records. The standard alternative is an HTTPS record in
AliasMode, which is allowed at the apex, for clients that support it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your service runs in Kubernetes and calls api.partner.com thousands of times a second. CoreDNS is overloaded and its logs are full of NXDOMAIN for names like api.partner.com.default.svc.cluster.local. Why, and what are two fixes?</summary>

The pod's resolver has `ndots:5`, so a name with only two dots is first tried with each search
suffix, and each of those fails with NXDOMAIN, for both A and AAAA. Fixes: use the fully qualified
name with a trailing dot (`api.partner.com.`), lower `ndots` for that pod with `dnsConfig`, and
cache lookups in the application or a node-local DNS cache so each request does not cause a query
at all.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A firewall change at your company allows UDP port 53 to the outside but blocks TCP port 53. What breaks, and why might nobody notice for weeks?</summary>

Only answers too large for UDP, which come back with the TC flag and must be retried over TCP.
Most answers are small, so almost everything keeps working. The failures appear for names with
large DNSSEC-signed answers, many addresses, or long TXT records, such as some email security
records, and they look like random "cannot resolve" errors for a few names.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Your DNS provider is down for 90 minutes. Users of a record with a 300-second TTL and users of one with a 1-day TTL have very different experiences. Explain, and say what serve-stale changes.</summary>

When authoritative servers cannot be reached, resolvers can only use cached copies. A 300-second
record disappears for most users within five minutes, as copies expire and refreshes fail. A 1-day
record keeps working for nearly everyone who looked it up in the past day. Resolvers that
implement serve-stale (RFC 8767) keep handing out expired answers, with a short TTL, when every
authoritative server fails, which keeps even the short-TTL name working for users who had it
cached, but never helps for a name nobody had asked for yet.

</details>

## Recap

- DNS is a **tree of names cut into zones**. Each zone's owner runs authoritative servers; each
  parent delegates its children with **NS records and glue**.
- Clients read DNS through **layers of caches**: app runtime, OS stub, recursive resolver. Only
  authoritative servers hold the truth, and nothing pushes changes to the caches.
- Every answer carries a **TTL**. After a change, old answers live for up to the **old** TTL. Lower
  it ahead of time, change, keep the old server until its traffic is zero, then raise it.
- **"No" is cached too**: NXDOMAIN and NODATA live for min(SOA TTL, SOA minimum). Create records
  before anyone tests them.
- A **CNAME** must stand alone, so not at the apex; chains live only as long as the shortest TTL.
  ALIAS, flattening and HTTPS records fill the gap.
- DNS runs over **UDP and TCP**; answers over about 1,232 bytes come back truncated and retry over
  TCP. **DoT, DoH and DoQ** encrypt the hop to your resolver; **DNSSEC** signs the records. Both
  fail in their own ways.
- DNS can **steer traffic** by location, latency, weight and health, but every decision is cached,
  so it is coarse and slow to fail over.
- In code: **never resolve once and keep it for ever**, cache with the record's TTL, use several
  resolvers with short timeouts, and serve stale answers when everything is down.

## Further reading

**Specs & official docs**

- [RFC 1034](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034) and [RFC 1035](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1035) (1987) — the concepts, the CNAME rule, the message format and the zone file syntax our examples use.
- [RFC 9499, DNS terminology](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9499) — the current, careful definitions of every term on this page.
- [RFC 2308, negative caching](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308) — NXDOMAIN, NODATA, and why the SOA minimum field means what it means.
- [RFC 8767, serve-stale](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767) — answering from expired cache when authorities are unreachable, with recommended timers.
- [RFC 9156, QNAME minimisation](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9156) — telling each server only what it needs, and what that costs.
- [RFC 6891, EDNS(0)](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6891) and [DNS Flag Day 2020](https://www.dnsflagday.net/2020/) — bigger UDP answers, and why 1,232 bytes became the default.
- [RFC 9210, DNS over TCP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9210) — why TCP support is mandatory for clients, servers and networks.
- [RFC 7858, DoT](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7858), [RFC 8484, DoH](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8484) and [RFC 9250, DoQ](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9250) — the three encrypted transports.
- [RFC 4033, DNSSEC introduction](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4033) — the chain of trust, DNSKEY, RRSIG and DS.
- [RFC 9824, compact denial of existence](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9824) — why some signed zones answer "no such name" with NOERROR.
- [RFC 9460, SVCB and HTTPS records](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9460) — connection hints and aliasing at the apex.
- [RFC 7871, EDNS Client Subnet](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7871) — how resolvers pass part of the user's address for GeoDNS, and its privacy cost.
- [RFC 6761, special-use domain names](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6761) — why `.test` is safe for our toy zone.
- [IANA root zone TLD list](https://data.iana.org/TLD/tlds-alpha-by-domain.txt) and [root-servers.org](https://root-servers.org/) — the size of the top of the tree.
- [ICANN: root KSK rollover](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/ksk-rollover-en) and [IANA trust anchors](https://www.iana.org/dnssec/files) — the October 2026 change of the root key.
- [Kubernetes: DNS for Services and Pods](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/services-networking/dns-pod-service/) — pod `resolv.conf`, search domains and `dnsConfig`.
- [Node.js: dns module](https://nodejs.org/api/dns.html) — `lookup()` versus `resolve()`, and the thread pool.
- [nginx upstream module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html) and [proxy module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_proxy_module.html) — when nginx resolves names, and the `resolve` parameter.
- [Amazon Route 53 routing policies](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/routing-policy.html) — what managed DNS steering offers.
- [Cloudflare: CNAME flattening](https://developers.cloudflare.com/dns/cname-flattening/) — the common workaround for CNAMEs at the apex.
- [Microsoft: prevent dangling DNS and subdomain takeover](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/fundamentals/subdomain-takeover) — the attack and the checklist against it.
