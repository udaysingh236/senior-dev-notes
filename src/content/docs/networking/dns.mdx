## One line changed, three different outages

Imagine you are the most junior engineer at a small payments startup in Bengaluru. On a quiet
Saturday morning the team moves its API, `api.example.in`, from an old virtual machine to a new
load balancer. Your job is the DNS part. You open the DNS provider's dashboard, change one line
from the old address to the new one, and press Save. Then you run `nslookup api.example.in` on
your laptop and it shows the new address. It worked. Ten minutes later, to save money, the team
switches the old machine off.

By lunchtime three different problems arrive, and none of them look like the others.

The support channel fills with users whose app says "cannot reach server". Not all users, maybe
a third of them, and fewer each minute, until about an hour later it stops by itself. Then a
partner bank emails: their payment callbacks to you have failed since 10:15, every single one,
and they are still failing at 4 PM. Their service looked up your address when it started on
Monday and has used it ever since. And your own team lead cannot open `pay.example.in`, a new
subdomain you created that morning, even though it works for everyone else. She had tried the
link before the record existed, and her office's DNS server remembered the answer "no such name".

Nothing was hacked and no server crashed. One line changed, and every one of these failures is
DNS behaving exactly as designed. The record had a TTL of 3,600 seconds that nobody looked at.
Your laptop's `nslookup` asked a server that happened to fetch a fresh copy. The partner's code
ignored TTLs completely. The office resolver was obeying a rule about caching "no" that most
engineers have never heard of.

The foundations page, [What happens when you type a URL](/foundations/how-the-web-works/), showed
DNS as step one of a page load: a quick walk from the root to the answer. This page goes inside
that step. It answers one question: **what is DNS really, who keeps copies of its answers and for
how long, and how do you change it, and depend on it, without an outage?**

## Why this problem exists

Computers on the internet find each other by **IP address**, a number such as `104.20.23.154`.
People, and the code people write, use names such as `api.example.in`. Something has to turn
names into numbers, and keep doing it correctly while the numbers change underneath.

Until the mid-1980s that something was one text file, `HOSTS.TXT`, which every machine on the
ARPANET downloaded from one central computer. Its remnant is the `/etc/hosts` file on your laptop.
It worked for a few hundred machines and could never work for millions.

The **Domain Name System** (DNS), designed by Paul Mockapetris and written down in its current
form in 1987 as [RFC 1034](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034) and
[RFC 1035](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1035), replaced the file with three ideas that still
define it:

1. **A tree of names.** Names are read right to left, from the general to the specific:
   `api.example.in` is `api`, inside `example`, inside `in`, inside the root.
2. **Delegation.** Each part of the tree can be handed to a different owner, who runs it on
   their own servers. Nobody needs permission from the centre to add `api` under `example.in`.
3. **Caching with an expiry date.** Every answer says how long it may be kept, so the servers at
   the top are not asked the same question billions of times.

These three ideas are why DNS scales to every device on the planet, and exactly what went wrong
in our story. Because of delegation, the truth about your name lives on your provider's servers
and nowhere else. Because of caching, copies of the old truth live on thousands of servers you do
not control, and you cannot call them back.

A few terms will come up on every line of this page:

- A **domain name** is a sequence of **labels** separated by dots, such as `api.example.in`. Each
  label is at most 63 bytes and the whole name at most 255.
- A **fully qualified domain name** (FQDN) is written all the way to the root, ending in a dot
  for the root's empty label: `api.example.in.`. Tools hide the dot; DNS software does not.
- A **record** (resource record, or RR) is one fact about a name: "`api.example.in` has the IPv4
  address `192.0.2.20`". Each record has a name, a **type** (A, AAAA, CNAME, MX…), a value and a
  **TTL**.
- The **TTL** (time to live) is how many seconds anyone may keep a copy of the record before
  they must ask again.
- An **authoritative server** holds the original records for part of the tree and answers with
  the truth. A **recursive resolver** answers on behalf of clients by asking authoritative
  servers and caching what it learns.

When DNS fails, a healthy service is invisible, which is why "it's always DNS" is a running joke
among operations engineers. DNS failures are also partial (some users), delayed (they start when
a cache expires) and self-healing (they stop when the next one does), which makes them very hard
to debug.

## The obvious picture, and why it breaks

The picture most of us start with is a lookup table. You ask "what is the address of
`api.example.in`?", a server checks its table, and you get the answer. If the table changes, the
next lookup gets the new value. The code we write first matches that picture, in two flavours:
look the name up once when the program starts, or look it up before every request "to always be
fresh". Both talk to a small DNS server we wrote for this page, which serves the made-up zone
`shop.example.test` on your own machine:

```python title="naive_client.py"
        return self.address
```

The demo replays our Saturday in five seconds. The record's TTL is 2 seconds. At 0.9 s the
operator moves `api` to a new server; at 3.6 s, well over one TTL later, the old server is
switched off. Both clients send a request every 0.45 s:

```text title="naive_client.py output (trimmed)"
t=0.0s  [ once] startup: api.shop.example.test. -> 203.0.113.10 (TTL 2 s, ignored)
t=0.9s  operator: new server is up, api now points to 198.51.100.20
t=3.6s  [ once] request  9 -> 203.0.113.10   FAILED: connection refused (nothing runs there now)
t=3.6s  [every] request  9 -> 198.51.100.20  200 OK

resolve-every-time made 10 DNS lookups for 10 requests; the server answered 11 queries in total.
```

Each breaks in its own way.

1. **Resolve once is the partner bank.** It works until the address changes, then fails on every
   request until someone restarts the process. Its logs say "connection refused", not "DNS",
   which sends people looking at the network and the server first.
2. **Resolve every time is correct and expensive.** Every request waits for a lookup first. At
   2,000 requests per second, that is 2,000 DNS queries per second from one client. The right
   answer is a cache, with the expiry the record itself asks for.

Before we go further, here are the neighbours DNS is most often confused with.

| These are often confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| **Registrar** vs **DNS provider** vs **hosting** | The registrar is where you rent the name and say which name servers serve it. The DNS provider runs those servers and stores your records. Hosting is the machines your records point to. They are often three different companies, and each can fail on its own. |
| **Recursive resolver** vs **authoritative server** | A recursive resolver (your ISP's, 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8) answers anyone's question by asking around and caching. An authoritative server answers only for its own zones, from its own records, and never asks anyone else. |
| **DNS** vs **`/etc/hosts`** | The hosts file is a local override that the operating system checks first. It is not DNS at all, which is why `dig` ignores it and your browser does not. |
| **DNS round robin** vs **load balancing** | Returning several addresses spreads clients roughly across servers. It knows nothing about load or health, and clients cache their pick. A load balancer decides per connection. |

## The core idea

Think of a large company's internal phone directory, before computers. The directory is not one
book. Each department keeps its own page and is the only one allowed to change it. A small front
office knows only which department owns which page. When you need a number you have never called,
you ask a receptionist. The receptionist asks the front office which department to ask, asks that
department, writes the number on a sticky note with "valid until Friday" on it, and gives you a
copy. Next time anyone asks the same receptionist, they read it off the sticky note.

That is DNS. The departments are **zones** run by different owners, the front office is the
parent zone that **delegates**, the receptionist is your **recursive resolver**, and the sticky
note's date is the **TTL**.

The analogy breaks in two places, and both matter.

- **There are millions of receptionists, and you cannot phone them.** When a department changes a
  number, nobody tells the receptionists. Each keeps using its sticky note until the date passes.
  There is no "recall all copies" button, at any price.
- **The department can give different answers to different callers.** DNS servers often answer
  based on who is asking, so users in Mumbai and Frankfurt get different addresses for the same
  name. That is a feature, and it is how CDNs send you to a nearby server.

Put precisely: **DNS is a hierarchical, delegated database. The tree of names is cut into zones,
each served by its owner's authoritative servers, and each parent says which servers own each
child. Clients almost never talk to those servers. They read through layers of caches, and every
answer, including the answer "this name does not exist", carries a TTL that says how long it may
be copied. Changing DNS means changing the truth in one place and then waiting for every copy to
expire.**

The diagram shows the tree. Look at the dashed outlines: each one is a zone, owned and run by
one operator.

`example.in` without asking anyone. And `cdn.net` is somebody else's zone: when your record points

## How it works, piece by piece

We will build the full picture in eight pieces, then look at DNS used as a traffic switch. The
real-world numbers come from our `lookup` script, asking Cloudflare's public resolver 1.1.1.1
from a network in India in October 2026; the toy numbers come from our own small server.

### Names, zones and delegation

A **zone** is a piece of the name tree that is managed as one unit, with one set of
authoritative servers. A zone usually starts at a name its owner bought, such as `example.in`,
and covers every name below it until the owner hands a subtree to someone else. That hand-over is
a **delegation**, and the point where it happens is a **zone cut**.

The parent marks a delegation with **NS records** (name server records): "the zone `example.in`
is served by `ns1.example.in`". That creates a puzzle. To find `ns1.example.in` you must ask the
servers for `example.in`, which is `ns1.example.in`. The parent breaks the loop by also
publishing the name server's address, called a **glue record**.

<Figure caption="The parent zone holds only the delegation: an NS record and the glue address for the child's name server. The child zone holds the real records, including its own copy of the NS record.">
  <Delegation />
</Figure>

So the NS record exists twice, once in the parent and once in the child, and the two can disagree
when someone updates one and forgets the other. When you "set your name servers" at your
registrar, you are editing the parent's copy: the registrar sends the change to the registry that
runs the TLD zone.

At the very top is the **root zone**. As of October 2026 it delegates 1,437 **top-level domains**
(TLDs) such as `in`, `com` and `dev` ([IANA](https://data.iana.org/TLD/tlds-alpha-by-domain.txt)).
The foundations page walked through the referrals from the root down; what matters here is their
TTL. The `.com` delegation may be cached for two days, so busy resolvers almost never ask the root.

**A worked example: why "changing name servers takes 48 hours".** Moving a domain to another DNS
provider changes the NS records in the parent, and the `.com` servers serve those with a TTL of
172,800 seconds, two days. A resolver that cached the old delegation an hour before your change
keeps asking your old provider for up to 47 more hours. Changing an A record inside a zone you keep
waits only for that record's own TTL. So keep the old provider serving identical records for at
least two days after switching.

### What a zone holds: records

A zone is, in the end, a list of records. Many DNS providers can import and export them in the
**zone file** format from RFC 1035. Here is a small, illustrative zone; `@` means "the zone's own
name", and names without a trailing dot get the zone name appended:

```text title="example.in zone file (illustrative)"
$TTL 3600                                   ; default TTL for records below
@        SOA   ns1.example.in. hostmaster.example.in. 2026101801 7200 900 1209600 300
@        NS    ns1.example.in.
@        NS    ns1.other-dns-provider.net.  ; a second provider, so one outage is not total
www      CNAME shop.cdn.net.
api 300  A     192.0.2.20                   ; a shorter TTL: this one may need to move
@        MX    10 mail.example.in.
```

The SOA line holds a serial number, timers for secondary servers and, last, the **minimum**: how
long others may cache "no such name" (300 s here).

The types you will meet in your first year:

| Type | Holds | Used for | Real example we saw |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** / **AAAA** | An IPv4 / IPv6 address | Where to connect | `example.com` → `104.20.23.154` |
| **CNAME** | Another name (an alias) | Pointing your name at a provider's name | `www.github.com` → `github.com` |
| **NS** / **SOA** | Name servers / zone metadata | Delegation; serial, timers, negative-cache TTL | `example.com` → `hera.ns.cloudflare.com` |
| **MX** | A mail server and a preference | Where to deliver email; lowest number first | `gmail.com` → `5 gmail-smtp-in.l.google.com` |
| **TXT** | Free text | Email anti-spoofing (SPF, DKIM), proving domain ownership | `example.com` → `v=spf1 -all` |
| **CAA** | Which certificate authorities may issue | Stopping a stray CA from issuing for you | `google.com` → `issue "pki.goog"` |
| **HTTPS** / **SVCB** | Connection hints | HTTP versions, ports, addresses before connecting | `cloudflare.com` → `alpn=h3,h2` |

A **CNAME** deserves a closer look. It says "this name is really that name": the resolver must
start again with the target. Our `lookup` script saw it for `www.github.com`:

```text title="lookup.py www.github.com A (output)"
www.github.com A via UDP 1.1.1.1: NOERROR [rd ra] 73 bytes in 14 ms
    www.github.com.              2998  CNAME  github.com.
    github.com.                    46  A      20.207.73.82
```

The alias may be kept for about 50 minutes, the address for only 46 seconds, so the useful
lifetime of the whole answer is 46 seconds. A chain is only as fresh as its shortest link.

<Figure caption="A CNAME sends the resolver to another name, often in another company's zone. The combined answer lives only as long as its shortest TTL. And the zone apex cannot be a CNAME at all.">
  <CnameChain />
</Figure>

The red box is a rule that surprises everyone once: a name with a CNAME may have no other records
([RFC 1034 §3.6.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034)). The **apex** of a zone, the bare
`example.in`, always has SOA and NS records, so it can never be a CNAME. Yet hosting platforms
tell you to "point your domain at `yoursite.platform.app`". Providers work around this with
non-standard **ALIAS** or **ANAME** records, or Cloudflare's **CNAME flattening**: the DNS
provider resolves the target itself and answers with plain A and AAAA records
([Cloudflare docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/dns/cname-flattening/)). The standard fix is
the newer **HTTPS** record ([RFC 9460](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9460), November 2023),
which can alias at the apex and also tell a browser that the server speaks HTTP/3.

### Who asks whom: stubs, resolvers and authoritative servers

A lookup involves four roles, and the confusing part is that three of them keep caches with
different rules.

<Figure caption="Your app asks the stub resolver in the OS, which asks a recursive resolver, which on a miss asks the authoritative servers. Only the authoritative servers hold the truth; every layer above holds copies, each with its own cache rules.">
  <Roles />
</Figure>

- Your **app** calls a library function, usually `getaddrinfo()`. Browsers, the Java runtime and
  your own code may add a cache on top.
- The **stub resolver** inside the operating system reads `/etc/resolv.conf` to learn which
  recursive resolver to use, checks `/etc/hosts`, and often caches for the whole machine.
- The **recursive resolver** does the real work. Its cache is shared by thousands or millions of
  users, so a popular name is almost always answered from memory.
- The **authoritative servers** answer from their zones and keep no cache.

The foundations page showed the basic walk from the root. This step-through adds the part it
skipped: a CNAME into a CDN. Watch how little each server is told, and how the alias forces a
second walk.

Two practical consequences follow. First, **the TTL you see depends on who you ask.** A resolver
tells you how much time is *left* on its copy: three caches answered `example.com` with 264, 81
and 300 seconds remaining at the same moment. Only an authoritative server shows the real TTL.
Second, **your test is not your users' test.** Your `nslookup` asks one resolver, which may have
fetched a fresh copy a second ago, while millions of users sit behind resolvers holding the old
one. That is why the laptop in our story showed the new address.

### Caching and TTLs: why "propagation" is not a thing

People say a DNS change "takes time to propagate", which suggests the new record spreading
outward from server to server. Nothing like that happens. When you change a record, the change
reaches your authoritative servers in seconds. Nobody pushes it anywhere else. Each cache that
holds a copy keeps serving the old value until its own copy expires, and then fetches the new one
the next time someone asks. "Propagation" is millions of independent timers running out.

That gives a simple rule: **after a change, old answers can be served for up to one TTL, the TTL
the record had before the change.** Lowering the TTL at the moment you change the address does
nothing for copies already cached with the old TTL.

Watch it happen. Five resolver caches each fetched their copy at a different moment, so their
timers run out at different times. The sixth row is an app like our partner bank's, which
resolved once at startup.

<DnsCacheSim mode="propagation" />

That is the whole of "propagation". With the default TTL of 300 seconds, the last cache switches
up to five minutes after the change, at a moment set by when it happened to fetch. Move the TTL
slider to 3,600 and the wait becomes an hour. Lower the lookups slider to 1 per minute and some
caches switch even later, because a cache only fetches again when someone asks.

**A worked example.** Our story's record had a TTL of 3,600 seconds and was changed at 10:05. A
resolver that fetched at 10:04 keeps the old address until 11:04. The team switched off the old
server at 10:15, so every user behind such a resolver failed from 10:15 until their resolver's
copy expired, sometime before 11:05. If caches fetched at random moments, their expiries spread
evenly across that hour, so the failures faded out roughly in a straight line: about two thirds of
users failing at 10:25, a third at 10:45, none after 11:05, which is what support saw.

The fix is a plan, drawn below. The key move is that the slow wait happens *before* the change,
while nothing is broken.

<Figure caption="Lower the TTL a day or two ahead and wait out the old TTL. Then the change itself only needs the short TTL to reach everyone, and the old server can be switched off once its traffic falls to zero.">
  <MigrationTimeline />
</Figure>

Two things make this harder in real life:

- **Not every cache obeys the TTL.** Resolvers may clamp it. Java caches lookups for 30 seconds by
  default, and older versions with a security manager cached them for ever
  ([`InetAddressCachePolicy`](https://code.yawk.at/java/8/sun/net/InetAddressCachePolicy.java)).
  Browsers keep talking to the old address over connections opened before the change. Watch the
  old server's traffic rather than the clock.
- **The serial matters for secondaries.** Secondary servers copy a zone by **zone transfer** only
  when the SOA serial number goes up. Forget to bump it on a self-hosted zone, and half your name
  servers keep serving yesterday's records.

### Negative caching: "no" is cached too

The third failure in our story is the one almost nobody expects. When a resolver asks for a name
that does not exist, it gets back a negative answer, and **it caches that too**
([RFC 2308](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308)).

There are two kinds of "no":

- **NXDOMAIN** (result code 3): the name does not exist at all, for any type.
- **NODATA**: the name exists, but has no records of the type you asked for. There is no special
  code for this. The answer says NOERROR, with an empty answer section.

Either way, the authoritative server includes its zone's SOA record, and the resolver may cache
the "no" for the smaller of the SOA record's own TTL and its last field, the **minimum**. That
field's name is a historical accident; today it means only "how long to cache negative answers".

Our `ttl_cache` demo reproduces the team lead's problem. It asks for `beta.shop.example.test`
before the operator creates it; the toy zone's SOA minimum is 5 seconds:

```text title="ttl_cache.py output, part 2"
t=4.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: NXDOMAIN, cached for 5.0 s more
t=4.1s  operator: created beta.shop.example.test. -> 198.51.100.30
t=5.6s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 3.5 s more
t=7.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 2.0 s more
t=8.6s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 0.5 s more
t=10.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: 198.51.100.30
```

The record existed from 4.1 s, yet the cache kept saying "no such name" for five more seconds.
With real zones the numbers are bigger: `example.com`'s SOA allows a "no" to be cached for 1,800
seconds, and `wikipedia.org`'s for 3,600. Anyone who tried the link before you created the record
may wait up to an hour.

One twist from testing: a missing name under `example.com` returns NOERROR with an empty answer,
not NXDOMAIN. Its DNSSEC-signed zone answers "no such name" as a signed NODATA, now standardised
as **compact denial of existence** ([RFC 9824](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9824)). So treat
"no records of the type I asked for" as the signal, not `rcode == NXDOMAIN`.

### On the wire: UDP, TCP and message size

Every DNS message, question or answer, has the same shape, whether it travels over UDP, TCP, TLS
or HTTPS.

<Figure caption="A 12-byte header (ID, flags, result code, counts), then the question, then three sections of records. The OPT record in the additional section says how large a UDP answer the sender can take.">
  <Message />
</Figure>

The header's **ID** is a random 16-bit number the answer must repeat, which is how a client
matches answers to questions. The flags say whether this is an answer (QR), whether it came from
an authoritative server (AA), whether recursion was asked for and offered (RD, RA), and whether
it was truncated (TC).

DNS normally uses **UDP** on port 53: one packet out, one back, one round trip. The original
standard limited UDP answers to 512 bytes. **EDNS(0)**
([RFC 6891](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6891)) lets a client say "I can take more" with an
OPT pseudo-record, but larger packets get split into IP fragments, which many networks drop. Since
[DNS Flag Day 2020](https://www.dnsflagday.net/2020/), major resolver software defaults to 1,232
bytes, which is IPv6's minimum packet size of 1,280 bytes minus 48 bytes of headers.

When an answer does not fit, the server does not send part of it. It sends the header with the
**TC** flag set, and the client asks again over **TCP**. Our toy server holds one deliberately
large record, 12 long TXT strings, to show exactly that:

[RFC 9210](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9210) (2022) is blunt: every DNS resolver and server
must support both UDP and TCP, and networks must allow both. "DNS is UDP" is the most common
half-truth in networking. The [TCP vs UDP](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/) page measures what the choice
costs.

### Private DNS: DoT, DoH and DoQ

Plain DNS can be read, and changed, by anyone on the path: the café Wi-Fi, your internet provider,
any network in between. Three standards wrap the same DNS message in encryption, between you and
your recursive resolver:

- **DNS over TLS** (DoT, [RFC 7858](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7858), 2016): DNS inside
  TLS on port 853. Its own port makes it simple to spot, and to block.
- **DNS over HTTPS** (DoH, [RFC 8484](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8484), 2018): the DNS
  message is the body of an HTTPS request, so it looks like any other web traffic on port 443.
  Browsers use it.
- **DNS over QUIC** (DoQ, [RFC 9250](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9250), 2022): DNS on QUIC,
  the transport under HTTP/3.

Our `lookup` script sends the very same bytes it would send over UDP, as the body of a `POST`:

```text title="node lookup.mjs (the end of the output, records trimmed)"
example.com A via UDP 1.1.1.1: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 18 ms
example.com A via DoH cloudflare-dns.com: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 213 ms
example.com A via DoH cloudflare-dns.com: NOERROR [rd ra] 72 bytes in 17 ms
```

The first DoH query to Cloudflare took 213 ms because it had to open a TCP connection and complete
a TLS handshake first. The second, on the same connection, took 17 ms, the same as plain UDP.
Encryption protects the conversation with *your resolver*, not the answer: your resolver could
still be wrong, and it sees every name you look up.

### Trust: DNSSEC

A resolver believes an answer if the ID and question match, and an attacker who can guess those,
or who sits on the path, can inject a false one. A false answer in a resolver's cache sends every
user of that resolver to the attacker for the whole TTL. This is **cache poisoning**. Random IDs
and random source ports make it much harder from outside the path, but not impossible.

**DNSSEC** ([RFC 4033](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4033) and its companions) adds
signatures. Each signed zone publishes its public key in a **DNSKEY** record and a signature,
**RRSIG**, over each set of records. Each parent publishes a **DS** record: a hash of its child's
key. A **validating resolver** starts from one key it already trusts, the root's, and checks the
chain down.

<Figure caption="The root's key is built into validating resolvers. Each parent vouches for its child's key with a DS record, and each zone signs its own records. One broken link and a validating resolver returns SERVFAIL rather than an unverifiable answer.">
  <DnssecChain />
</Figure>

The root's key itself was replaced for only the second time this month: as of October 2026, the
new key, KSK-2024, signs the root zone from 11 October 2026
([ICANN](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/ksk-rollover-en)).

DNSSEC's strength is also its risk. Validation fails closed: if any signature in the chain is
wrong, a validating resolver returns SERVFAIL, and the name disappears for its users, even though
every server is up. The `.de` incident below is this failure. And DNSSEC gives authenticity, not
privacy: the records are signed, not encrypted.

### DNS as a traffic switch

Because authoritative servers can answer differently depending on who asks, DNS is also one of
the simplest ways to steer traffic across the world.

<Figure caption="The same name, two answers. The authoritative server sees roughly where each question comes from and returns the nearest healthy site, with a short TTL so it can change its mind.">
  <GeoDns />
</Figure>

Managed DNS services offer this as **routing policies**. As of October 2026, Amazon Route 53
offers weighted (send 10% to the new version), latency-based, geolocation, failover (send
everything to a standby when health checks fail) and several more
([Route 53 docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/routing-policy.html)).

The catch is that the authoritative server sees the *resolver's* address, not the user's. A user
in Chennai whose public resolver reaches the server from Singapore looks like a user in Singapore.
**EDNS Client Subnet** (ECS, [RFC 7871](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7871)) lets the
resolver pass along part of the user's address, which improves the answer and leaks a little of
who is asking.

DNS steering is coarse. Every decision is cached for a TTL, so failover through DNS takes at least
one TTL plus health-check time, and clients that pinned an address never fail over at all. That
is why failover records use TTLs of 30 to 60 seconds, and why a load balancer behind the address
still does the fine-grained work.

## Choosing TTLs, side by side

Almost every DNS decision an application engineer makes comes down to choosing TTLs. The
simulation runs the same change in two worlds, with the same traffic; only the TTL differs.

<DnsCacheSim mode="compare" />

The 1-minute world switches almost at once and asks the authoritative server about sixty times
more often; the 1-hour world takes a full hour to switch. That matters for your DNS bill, which
many providers charge per query, and for surviving a DNS provider outage: when the authoritative
servers are unreachable, cached copies are all that keep your name alive, and they last one TTL.

Here is where I would start, and why:

| Record | Typical TTL | Why |
|---|---|---|
| NS and their glue | 1–2 days (set by the TLD) | Rarely change; long caching keeps load off the TLD servers |
| Apex and `www` behind a CDN | 300 s (often set by the CDN) | The CDN's own name changes behind it; 5 minutes balances agility and load |
| API or app endpoint you control | 60–300 s | You will want to move it; lower to 60 s ahead of planned work |
| Failover or weighted records | 30–60 s | The whole point is to change quickly when health checks fail |
| MX, TXT for email and verification | 1 hour – 1 day | Change rarely; a long TTL rides out DNS provider outages |
| SOA minimum (negative caching) | 60–300 s | Short enough that a new name works quickly for people who tried early |

**How to choose.** Use long TTLs for anything that rarely changes, short TTLs for anything you
expect to move, and remember the rule from the migration plan: the TTL that matters on the day of a
change is the one set the day before.

## Build it yourself

We built four things, each closer to production: a tiny authoritative server, a cache that honours
TTLs, a resilient resolver, and a small `dig` that speaks plain DNS and DoH. Everything uses only
the standard library, with a shared `dnswire` module for the binary messages. Every file is in
[examples/dns](https://github.com/udaysingh236/senior-dev-notes/tree/main/examples/dns), and every
output on this page came from running them in October 2026.

### Step 1: an authoritative server you can break

The demos run against `toy_server`, which serves `shop.example.test` on `127.0.0.1:8053` (`.test`
is reserved for testing by [RFC 6761](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6761)). It returns the
three kinds of answer you have met, records, NXDOMAIN and NODATA, and sets the TC flag when an
answer is too big for UDP. Run `python toy_server.py` and query it:
`nslookup -port=8053 www.shop.example.test 127.0.0.1`.

### Step 2: a cache that honours TTLs, including "no"

The correct version of our client is a cache keyed by name and type. `resolve()` returns the
cached entry while it is fresh and calls `_ask()` when it is not. The interesting part is where
the expiry comes from:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="ttl_cache.py"
# runnable version: examples/dns/python/ttl_cache.py
    def _ask(self, name: str, rtype: str) -> Entry:
        _, query = w.build_query(name, rtype)
        reply = w.parse_message(w.ask_udp(*self.server, query))
        now = time.monotonic()
        # Follow any CNAMEs in the answer; the chain is only as fresh as its shortest-lived link.
        records = [r for r in reply.answer if r.type in (rtype, "CNAME")]
        addresses = [r.value for r in records if r.type == rtype]
        if reply.rcode == "NOERROR" and addresses:
            return Entry(addresses, "NOERROR", now + min(r.ttl for r in records))
        # NXDOMAIN (no such name) or NODATA (name exists, no records of this type).
        # Negative TTL = the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field (RFC 2308).
        soa = next((r for r in reply.authority if r.type == "SOA"), None)
        negative_ttl = min(soa.ttl, soa.data["minimum"]) if soa else 0  # no SOA: do not cache
        return Entry([], reply.rcode if reply.rcode != "NOERROR" else "NODATA", now + negative_ttl)
```

```js title="ttl-cache.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/dns/js/ttl-cache.mjs
  async ask(name, type) {
    const { query } = w.buildQuery(name, type);
    const reply = w.parseMessage(await w.askUdp(...this.server, query));
    const now = performance.now();
    // Follow any CNAMEs in the answer; the chain is only as fresh as its shortest-lived link.
    const records = reply.answer.filter((r) => r.type === type || r.type === 'CNAME');
    const addresses = records.filter((r) => r.type === type).map((r) => r.value);
    if (reply.rcode === 'NOERROR' && addresses.length) {
      return { addresses, rcode: 'NOERROR', expires: now + Math.min(...records.map((r) => r.ttl)) * 1000 };
    }
    // NXDOMAIN (no such name) or NODATA (name exists, no records of this type).
    // Negative TTL = the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field (RFC 2308).
    const soa = reply.authority.find((r) => r.type === 'SOA');
    const negativeTtl = soa ? Math.min(soa.ttl, soa.data.minimum) : 0; // no SOA: do not cache
    return { addresses: [], rcode: reply.rcode === 'NOERROR' ? 'NODATA' : reply.rcode, expires: now + negativeTtl * 1000 };
  }
```

### Step 3: a resolver that survives bad days

The production-shaped version handles a dead upstream, an answer too big for UDP, a reply that
does not match the question (a possible forgery), a silly TTL, and a moment when every DNS server
is unreachable. The last uses **serve-stale** ([RFC 8767](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767)):
an expired answer beats none. The RFC suggests keeping expired data for one to three days and
handing it out with a 30-second TTL so the client asks again soon.

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="resilient_resolver.py"
# runnable version: examples/dns/python/resilient_resolver.py
    def resolve(self, name: str, rtype: str = "A") -> list[str]:
        key, now = (name.lower(), rtype), time.monotonic()
        entry = self.cache.get(key)
        if entry and now < entry.expires:
            return entry.values
        try:
            values, ttl = self._ask_upstreams(name, rtype)
        except LookupFailed as err:
            if entry and now < entry.stale_until:  # serve stale: an old answer beats no answer
                entry.expires = now + STALE_TTL
                print(f"    ! every upstream failed ({err}); serving the expired answer for {STALE_TTL} s")
                return entry.values
            raise
        ttl = max(MIN_TTL, min(MAX_TTL, ttl))
        self.cache[key] = Entry(values, now + ttl, now + ttl + MAX_STALE)
        return values
```

```js title="resilient-resolver.mjs"
    const now = performance.now();

    if (entry && now < entry.expires) return entry.values;
    let values, ttl;

    } catch (err) {
      if (err instanceof LookupFailed && entry && now < entry.staleUntil) { // serve stale: an old answer beats no answer
        entry.expires = now + STALE_TTL * 1000;
        console.log(`    ! every upstream failed (${err.message}); serving the expired answer for ${STALE_TTL} s`);
        return entry.values;

      throw err;
```

### Step 4: a tiny dig, over UDP and over HTTPS

The `lookup` script produced the real-world outputs on this page. It asks 1.1.1.1 over UDP, then

identical, cacheable HTTP requests. One surprise: on Windows, Python's system trust store did not

## How real systems do it

The protocol has barely changed since 1987; what has changed is the scale and the habits around
it. Facts in this section change over time, so each is marked with when we checked.

**Public resolvers live everywhere at once.** 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8 and 9.9.9.9 are each announced from
many cities by **anycast**: the same address in many places, with internet routing delivering
each packet to the nearest one. That is why our UDP lookups to 1.1.1.1 from India usually took
12 to 20 ms when the answer was cached, and why the remaining TTLs differed between runs: we were
often talking to different caches behind the same address.

**Kubernetes leans on DNS for everything inside the cluster.** Every Service gets a name, and
CoreDNS answers for it. Each pod's `/etc/resolv.conf` lists search suffixes such as
`my-namespace.svc.cluster.local` and sets `options ndots:5`
([Kubernetes docs](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/services-networking/dns-pod-service/)):
"a name with fewer than five dots might be a short internal name, so try every search suffix
first". A lookup of `api.stripe.com` is tried as `api.stripe.com.my-namespace.svc.cluster.local`
and two more suffixes, each failing, before the real name is asked. With A and AAAA asked
separately, one external lookup becomes eight queries. Write external names with a trailing dot
(`api.stripe.com.`), or lower `ndots` for the pod with `dnsConfig`.

**Proxies resolve once unless told otherwise.** nginx resolves a hostname written directly in
`proxy_pass` when it starts, and keeps that address: the partner bank's bug, in a config file. As
of October 2026, since nginx 1.27.3 the open-source version can re-resolve upstream servers
([nginx docs](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html)):

```nginx title="nginx: follow DNS changes for an upstream"
resolver 10.0.0.2 valid=30s;          # re-resolve at most every 30 s, whatever the TTL says

upstream payments {
    zone payments 64k;                # resolved addresses live in shared memory
    server api.partner-bank.example resolve;
}
```

**Runtimes have opinions.** In Node, `dns.lookup()`, which `fetch` uses, calls `getaddrinfo()` on
libuv's four-thread pool and returns no TTL, so slow DNS can stall unrelated file and crypto work
that shares those threads ([Node docs](https://nodejs.org/api/dns.html)).

## Gotchas and war stories

The foundations page covers the Facebook, Slack and 1.1.1.1 incidents. Here are three more, all
about the zones above and around yours.

:::danger[A real outage: the empty record behind half of AWS us-east-1]
On 19–20 October 2025, the DNS record for `dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com` was suddenly empty.
Two of DynamoDB's DNS automation workers raced: a slow one overwrote the endpoint with a stale
plan just as a faster one's clean-up deleted that plan, removing every address. From 11:48 PM PDT
nothing could resolve the endpoint, including many AWS services that depend on DynamoDB. DNS was
restored by 2:25 AM, and clients recovered as their cached empty answers expired
([AWS post-event summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/)).
:::

The lesson is not "automation is bad". It is that the systems that *write* DNS deserve the same
care as the systems that serve traffic: check that a plan is still the newest one at the moment
you write it, refuse to publish an empty answer for a name that has always had addresses, and have
a tested way to put a known-good record back by hand.

:::danger[A real outage: a TLD's signatures go bad]
On 5 May 2026, during a routine DNSSEC key rollover, DENIC, the registry for `.de`, published
signatures that validating resolvers could not verify: its new signing system had generated a
different key on each of three hardware security modules but published only one. Validating
resolvers returned SERVFAIL for `.de` names, even for domains that were not signed themselves, and
failures grew for about three hours as cached answers expired. Large resolver operators,
Cloudflare among them, temporarily switched off validation for `.de`, and serve-stale cushioned
users meanwhile ([DENIC's analysis](https://blog.denic.de/en/analysis-of-the-dns-outage-on-5-may-2026/),
[Cloudflare's write-up](https://blog.cloudflare.com/de-tld-outage-dnssec/)).
:::

Nobody running a `.de` website did anything wrong. Every name sits under a chain of zones you do
not run, and each can fail for you.

**One DNS provider is one point of failure.** On 21 October 2016, the Mirai botnet of hacked
cameras and home routers flooded Dyn, a large DNS provider, for hours. Twitter, GitHub, Netflix
and others became unreachable for many users, although their own servers were fine
([APNIC](https://blog.apnic.net/2016/10/26/quick-look-attack-dyn/),
[Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2016/10/27/dyn-ddos-post-mortem/)). The
standard answer is name servers from two independent providers in the NS set, both holding
identical records.

:::danger[Dangling records are a security hole]
A CNAME that points at a cloud resource you deleted, say `promo.example.in` pointing at an app
name on a hosting platform, is a **dangling record**. If anyone else can claim that resource name
on the platform, they now serve content, and can even get certificates, for your subdomain. This
is **subdomain takeover**
([Microsoft's guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/fundamentals/subdomain-takeover)).
Put "remove the DNS record" on every decommissioning checklist, and audit CNAMEs that point
outside your own zones.
:::

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

DNS is the right tool for one job, finding the current address for a stable name, and it is very
good at it. Its trade-offs come from the caching that makes it scale.

- **Freshness versus load and resilience.** Short TTLs mean fast changes, more queries, a bigger
  bill, and a shorter survival time when your DNS provider is down. Long TTLs mean the opposite.
  You choose per record.
- **DNS steering is coarse.** It works at the level of resolvers, not users, and every decision is
  cached. A load balancer with health checks reacts in seconds; DNS reacts in TTLs. Use DNS to
  choose a region and the load balancer to choose a server.
- **DNSSEC trades availability for authenticity.** It stops forged answers, and a signing mistake
  makes your name vanish for every validating resolver. Sign through a provider that automates key
  rollovers, or do not sign at all; a half-managed DNSSEC setup is worse than none.
- **Encrypted DNS moves trust.** DoH hides your lookups from the network and shows them all to one
  resolver operator.

When should you *not* lean on DNS? Where endpoints change every few seconds, such as autoscaling
pods, DNS caching works against you; a service registry or a load balancer fits better. And DNS
cannot make per-request decisions such as sticky sessions: it never sees requests.

:::note[From the trenches]
Teams usually discover their DNS habits during an outage. The pattern repeats: a record changed in
a hurry, a TTL nobody had looked at in years, an old server switched off too early, and an hour of
partial failures that fix themselves before anyone understands them. Before your next migration,
write down every record involved, its current TTL, every client that might cache it, and who can
change it at 3 AM. That ten-minute list prevents most outages of this kind.
:::

## Interview corner

DNS questions come up in almost every networking and system design interview, usually as a
warm-up. What separates a strong answer is knowing that DNS is a cache with rules, not a table,
and talking about how it fails.

1. **"What happens when you look up a name?"** Stub resolver, recursive resolver, then root, TLD
   and authoritative servers via referrals and glue, each layer caching. Mention that the root is
   rarely asked because referrals are cached for days.
2. **"You changed a DNS record and some users still reach the old server. Why?"** Caches hold the
   old answer for up to the old TTL; some clients ignore TTLs. Keep the old server running and
   watch its traffic; next time, lower the TTL a day ahead.
3. **"How would you use DNS for failover or global load balancing?"** Health-checked, short-TTL
   records with latency or geolocation routing, and the limits: failover takes at least a TTL and
   pinned clients never move, so pair it with load balancers.
4. **"Is DNS UDP or TCP?"** Both: UDP by default, TCP for truncated answers and zone transfers.
5. **"DNSSEC or DNS over HTTPS?"** DNSSEC proves records were not forged; DoH encrypts the
   conversation with your resolver. Different threats; neither replaces the other.

In system design interviews, DNS is where you say how users reach your system at all: a short-TTL
name in front of a global load balancer or CDN, and two DNS providers if the stakes are high.

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Your record has a TTL of 3,600 seconds. At 14:00 you change the address and set the TTL to 60 seconds in the same edit. When can you safely switch off the old server?</summary>

Not until about 15:00, and only after its traffic has dropped to zero. Caches that fetched the
record shortly before 14:00 got it with the old TTL of 3,600 seconds and may keep it until 15:00.
To make the cut-over fast you had to lower the TTL at least an hour (the old TTL) before the change.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A colleague created report.example.in at 09:30. At 09:20 they had tried to open it and got "no such name". At 09:35 it still fails for them but works for you. The zone's SOA has TTL 3600 and minimum 900. What is going on, and when will it fix itself?</summary>

Their resolver cached the negative answer at 09:20, for min(3600, 900) = 900 seconds, so the "no"
can last until 09:35, plus any OS or browser cache. Your resolver had never asked, so it fetched
the new record.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why can you not put a CNAME on example.in itself, and what do providers do instead?</summary>

A name with a CNAME may have no other records, and the zone apex always has SOA and NS records.
Providers offer ALIAS or ANAME records, or CNAME flattening: they resolve the target themselves
and answer with ordinary A and AAAA records. The standard alternative is an HTTPS record, which is
allowed at the apex, for clients that support it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Your service runs in Kubernetes and calls api.partner.com thousands of times a second. CoreDNS is overloaded and its logs are full of NXDOMAIN for names like api.partner.com.default.svc.cluster.local. Why, and what are two fixes?</summary>

The pod's resolver has `ndots:5`, so a name with only two dots is first tried with each search
suffix, and each fails with NXDOMAIN, for both A and AAAA. Fixes: use the fully qualified name
with a trailing dot (`api.partner.com.`), lower `ndots` for that pod with `dnsConfig`, or cache
lookups in the application or a node-local DNS cache.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Your DNS provider is down for 90 minutes. Users of a record with a 300-second TTL and users of one with a 1-day TTL have very different experiences. Explain, and say what serve-stale changes.</summary>

With the authoritative servers unreachable, resolvers can only use cached copies. The 300-second
record disappears for most users within five minutes; the 1-day record keeps working for nearly
everyone. Serve-stale keeps even the short-TTL name working for users whose resolver had it
cached, but never helps a name nobody had asked for yet.

</details>

## Recap

- DNS is a **tree of names cut into zones**. Each zone's owner runs authoritative servers; each
  parent delegates its children with **NS records and glue**.
- Clients read DNS through **layers of caches**: app runtime, OS stub, recursive resolver. Nothing
  pushes changes to them. After a change, old answers live for up to the **old** TTL: lower it
  ahead of time, change, and keep the old server until its traffic is zero.
- **"No" is cached too**: NXDOMAIN and NODATA live for min(SOA TTL, SOA minimum). Create records
  before anyone tests them.
- A **CNAME** must stand alone, so not at the apex, and a chain lives only as long as its shortest
  TTL. ALIAS, flattening and HTTPS records fill the gap.
- DNS runs over **UDP and TCP**; answers over about 1,232 bytes are truncated and retried over TCP.
  **DoT, DoH and DoQ** encrypt the hop to your resolver; **DNSSEC** signs the records and fails
  closed. **DNS steering** is real but coarse, because every decision is cached.
- In code: **never resolve once and keep it for ever**, cache with the record's TTL, use several
  resolvers with short timeouts, and serve stale answers when everything is down.
