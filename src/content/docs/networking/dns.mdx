## The question

You type `shop.example.in` into a browser, and about 15 milliseconds later your laptop knows that
the shop lives at the address 192.0.2.10. Nobody gave your laptop that number, and the shop's own
server was not asked. Then the shop moves to a new server. The owner changes the address in one
second, yet some users keep reaching the old server for an hour. Where did your laptop get the
number, and why can't the owner take it back?

The short answer: DNS, the Domain Name System, is the internet's directory of names. Your laptop
asks one nearby server, a **resolver**, for the address. The first time anyone asks that resolver,
it follows a chain of servers from the top of the name tree down to the server the shop's owner
runs. That server answers with the address and a number of seconds the answer may be kept, its
**TTL**. The resolver keeps the answer for that long and hands the copy to everyone who asks. That
is why most lookups take milliseconds, and also why a change waits: thousands of copies are out
there, each expiring on its own clock, and nobody can recall them.

## The idea in plain words

Follow one name through one lookup and one change. You run a small online shop at
`shop.example.in`. Its server has the address 192.0.2.10, and you have told DNS that anyone may
keep this answer for 300 seconds, five minutes. Ravi, in Bengaluru, opens the shop.

1. **Ravi's browser needs a number.** Computers on the internet find each other by numeric
   addresses, not names. Before it can send its first HTTP request, the browser must turn
   `shop.example.in` into an address.
2. **It asks the operating system, which asks a resolver.** Ravi's laptop is set up, usually
   automatically by his Wi-Fi router, to send DNS questions to one resolver: his internet
   provider's, or a public one such as Cloudflare's `1.1.1.1`. The question is one small message:
   "what is the address of `shop.example.in`?"
3. **The resolver has never heard of the name, so it starts at the top.** Names form a tree, read
   from the right: `in` sits under the root of the tree, `example.in` under `in`, and
   `shop.example.in` under that. The resolver ships with the addresses of the root servers. It
   asks one, and the root replies: "not mine; the `.in` servers know. Here they are."
4. **It asks a `.in` server**, run by the registry, the organisation in charge of all names ending
   in `.in`. Reply: "not mine;
   `example.in` is served by `ns1.example.in`, at 192.0.2.53."
5. **It asks `ns1.example.in`**, the server you run, or a **DNS provider** (a company that runs
   such servers for you) runs on your behalf. This server holds your
   records, so it answers: "`shop.example.in` is 192.0.2.10; keep this for 300 seconds."
6. **The resolver saves the answer and hands it to Ravi's laptop.** It stores the address in its
   memory with an expiry time five minutes away. The whole walk took about 120 ms: on our test
   network, lookups that had to make this walk took 105–141 ms. The browser now connects to
   192.0.2.10.
7. **Two minutes later, Ravi's flatmate opens the shop** through the same resolver. The resolver
   finds the saved copy, sees 180 of its 300 seconds left, and answers at once, in about 15 ms
   (12–20 ms on our network). It hands on the time left, 180 seconds, not the full 300.
   `ns1.example.in` never hears about the flatmate, or about the next ten thousand people who ask
   that resolver within those five minutes.
8. **At 300 seconds the copy expires.** The next person who asks sends the resolver back to
   `ns1.example.in` for a fresh answer. It skips the root and `.in` this time, because their
   replies came with much longer keep-times of their own and are still saved.
9. **Now you move the shop.** 200 seconds after the first lookup, you point `shop.example.in` at a
   new server, 198.51.100.20. Your `ns1` hands out the new address from that moment. But Ravi's
   resolver still holds its copy of the old one, with 100 seconds left, and keeps giving it out
   until it expires. You have no way to tell it to throw the copy away.

tree, and every question in the next 300 seconds stops at the resolver.

So the first lookup is the expensive one, and everything after it is a copy. Now multiply that by
the internet. There is not one resolver but a great many: every internet provider runs some,
and so does every company network and every public DNS service. Each one fetched its copy of your
record at a different moment, so each copy expires at a different moment.

When you change the record, nothing is sent to those resolvers. Each one carries on with its old
copy until that copy's time runs out, and then fetches the new one the next time someone asks.
People call this "DNS propagation", but nothing travels: it is many separate countdowns reaching
zero.

So the keep-time is a trade-off you choose for each name. A long one, say a day, means few
questions reach your server, and users keep working even if your DNS server is down for an hour,
but a change takes up to a day to reach everyone. A short one, say a minute, means a change
reaches everyone within a minute, at the price of many more questions and much less protection
when your DNS servers fail.

An everyday picture: you change your phone number and tell your friends. Friends who saved the old
number keep calling it until they next check with you, and you cannot reach into their phones to
fix it. DNS adds one rule: every saved number comes with a time after which it must be checked
again. The picture stops working in one place: a friend checks whenever they remember, while a
resolver checks only when its copy has run out and someone asks it for the name again.

## The building blocks

Seven ideas carry the rest of the page. Each gets a plain sentence, the reason it exists, a small
example, and then its precise version.

### Names and addresses

**What it is.** An **IP address** is the number that identifies a machine on the internet; a
**domain name** is the human-readable label that points to it.

**Why it exists.** Networks deliver data to numbers. People remember names, and a name also lets
the owner move to a different machine without telling every user a new number.

**Example.** `shop.example.in` points to 192.0.2.10. (The addresses on this page come from ranges
reserved for documentation, so none of them belongs to a real machine.)

**The technical version.** An IPv4 address is four numbers from 0 to 255; IPv6, the newer version,
uses longer addresses written like `2001:db8::10`. A domain name is a list of **labels** separated
by dots, read from right to left, starting at the top: the root (which has an empty label), then
`in`, `example`, `shop`. A name written with a dot at the end, `shop.example.in.`, is a complete
name that ends at the root. Without that dot, some software first tries adding endings of its own,
which matters in Kubernetes (Part 2).

### DNS records

**What it is.** A **record** is one fact about a name, stored on a DNS server: "`shop.example.in`
has the address 192.0.2.10".

**Why it exists.** DNS stores more than addresses: where to deliver email for a domain, which
servers are in charge of a part of the tree, free-text notes. Each fact needs a label saying what
kind of fact it is, and a keep-time.

**Example.** Records are often written one per line, in a format called a zone file:

```text title="one record, in zone-file form"
shop.example.in.   300   IN      A      192.0.2.10
; name             TTL   class   type   value
```

**The technical version.** Every record has those five fields. The class is always `IN`
(internet). The type says what the value means: `A` is an IPv4 address, `AAAA` an IPv6 one,
`CNAME` says "this name is an alias, ask for that other name instead", `NS` names the servers that
run a part of the tree, `SOA` holds the settings of that part, and `TXT` is free text. All records
with the same name and type, say three `A` records for `shop`, form one **record set**, which is
fetched, cached and expired as a unit.

### A cache

**What it is.** A **cache** is a saved copy of an answer, kept close to whoever needs it, so that
the next identical question is answered without asking the source again.

**Why it exists.** Asking the source is slow and puts load on it, and most questions repeat:
thousands of people ask for the same popular names every minute.

**Example.** The resolver's walk down the tree took about 120 ms; answering from its saved copy
took about 15 ms. If 10,000 people ask that resolver for `shop.example.in` within five minutes,
your server answers once, not 10,000 times.

**The technical version.** A DNS cache stores each record set under its name and type, with an
expiry time: the moment it was fetched plus the record's keep-time. A cache may drop an entry
earlier, for example when it runs out of memory (called **eviction**), but it is never supposed to
use one later. Caches exist at several layers: the resolver, sometimes the operating system, the
browser, the language your program runs in. Part 2 shows what each one does.

### The TTL

**What it is.** The **TTL** (time to live) is the number of seconds that anyone who receives a
record may keep using it without asking again.

**Why it exists.** Every cache needs to know when its copy is too old. Only the owner knows how
often a record changes, so the owner sets the TTL, record by record.

**Example.** A resolver fetches `shop.example.in` with TTL 300 at 10:00:00, so its copy expires
at 10:05:00. At 10:02:00 it hands the copy to a laptop with TTL 180, so the laptop's own cache
also expires at 10:05:00, not at 10:07:00.

**The technical version.** The TTL travels with every record as a whole number of seconds.
Caches count it down and hand on only the time left. It is an upper limit for a cache that
follows the rules, not a promise: Part 2 shows layers that shorten it, stretch it or ignore it.
And because nobody can cancel a copy before its time runs out, Part 2 calls each answer a
**lease**: permission to use an answer for a set time, which cannot be taken back.

### Resolvers and authoritative servers

**What it is.** An **authoritative server** holds the records for a part of the name tree and
answers only about them. A **recursive resolver** holds no records of its own: it finds answers by
asking authoritative servers, and caches what it learns.

**Why it exists.** Splitting the two jobs lets each owner run servers only for their own names,
and lets one resolver near the users serve millions of people from its cache.

**Example.** `ns1.example.in` is authoritative for `example.in`. `1.1.1.1` (Cloudflare),
`8.8.8.8` (Google), your internet provider's resolver and your company network's resolver are
all recursive resolvers. "Recursive" because it keeps asking until it has the final answer.

**The technical version.** Your program talks to neither directly. It calls the operating
system's `getaddrinfo()` function, part of a small library called the **stub resolver**. The stub
sends the question to the resolver listed in the machine's settings (`/etc/resolv.conf` on Linux)
and returns the addresses to your program.

### Zones and referrals

**What it is.** A **zone** is the part of the name tree that one operator runs from one set of
authoritative servers. A **referral** is a reply that says "not mine, ask these servers".

**Why it exists.** No single server could hold every name on the internet or keep up with every
change. The tree lets each owner run their own part, and referrals connect the parts.

**Example.** `example.in`, with every name under it, is your zone. A resolver with an empty cache
finds `shop.example.in` in three questions:

| Step | Resolver asks | Reply |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | a root server | a referral: "ask the `.in` servers" |
| 2 | a `.in` server | a referral: "ask `ns1.example.in`, at 192.0.2.53" |
| 3 | `ns1.example.in` | the answer: `192.0.2.10`, TTL 300 |

**The technical version.** The `.in` zone does not hold your records. For `example.in` it holds
only `NS` records naming your servers, the zone's **name servers** (another word for its
authoritative servers). This hand-over is called a **delegation**. Referrals are
cached for their own TTLs. The root hands out its referrals with a TTL of 172,800 seconds, two
days, so for two days the resolver's next lookup for any `.in` name starts at step 2. Part 2
opens delegations up.

:::tip[If you stop here]
- DNS turns a name into an address. Your computer asks a **resolver**; on a cache miss the
  resolver asks a root server, then the `.in` (or `.com`) servers, then the owner's server.
- Every answer carries a **TTL**: how many seconds anyone may keep it. Resolvers keep answers for
  that long, so most lookups take about 15 ms and the owner's server sees only the misses.
- A change is not sent anywhere. Each saved copy keeps the old answer until its TTL runs out, and
  nobody can recall it early.
- So a short TTL means fast changes and many queries; a long TTL means few queries, slow changes
  and survival when your DNS servers are down.
:::

## Going deeper

Part 1 had one resolver and one change. Real systems raise harder questions. Your DNS provider
stops answering for an hour. Users of a name with a one-day TTL never notice; users of a name with
a five-minute TTL are all failing by minute five, unless their resolver keeps handing out expired
copies. Now a different bug: the provider stays up but publishes the record with no addresses.
The failure spreads at exactly the same speed, handing out expired copies does nothing, and after
you fix the record users keep failing for another quarter of an hour. Why do these outages behave
so differently, and which numbers decide it?

The answer is in the lease from Part 1, once you add one more fact: every layer between you and
the user may shorten, stretch or ignore it. The rest of the page follows those leases through
migrations, outages and load balancing.

First, the load. Each cache asks at most once per TTL, so a popular name costs about *number of
caches ÷ TTL* queries at your authoritative servers. Take 5,000 resolver caches asking for your
API's name (an illustrative number):

| TTL | Queries reaching your name servers |
|---|---|
| 86,400 s (1 day) | 5,000 ÷ 86,400 × 60 ≈ 3.5 per minute |
| 300 s | 5,000 ÷ 300 ≈ 17 per second, 1,000 per minute |
| 60 s | 5,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 83 per second |

The same number sets two other things. It is the upper limit on how long a change takes to reach
caches that follow the rules. And it is how long users survive when your name servers are
unreachable, because cached copies are then all that keeps the name alive. A short TTL buys fast
changes and pays in query volume (DNS providers often bill per million queries) and in a
shorter survival time. No TTL is good at all three, so you pick one per record.

"5,000 caches" also hides something, which shows up as soon as you look at real resolvers.

**What anycast is.** **Anycast** means announcing one IP address from many places at once, so
each user's questions reach the nearest machine that answers to it.

**Why it exists.** A public resolver wants one address that everyone can type into their settings,
served from many cities, both for speed and so that one city failing does not take it down.

**Example.** A user in Mumbai and a user in Frankfurt both send questions to `1.1.1.1`, and two
different machines, thousands of kilometres apart, answer them.

**The technical version.** Behind each city there are many independent cache machines, and they
fetch at different moments, so the same name shows a different remaining TTL depending on which
one you reach. When we asked three public resolvers for `example.com` at the same moment in
October 2026, they reported 264, 81 and 300 seconds left. So "one resolver" is really many
caches, each with its own countdown.

## The obvious model, and why it breaks

The model most engineers carry is: *the TTL is how long a change takes to reach users.* Lower the
TTL when you change a record and the change arrives quickly. Code written with that model either
resolves the name once at startup or resolves it on every request.

To see both fail, we wrote a small authoritative server for this page. It runs on your own
machine, serves a made-up zone, `shop.example.test`, and lets a script play the operator who
changes records while clients run. In the code below, `resolve(API)` sends one question straight
to that server and returns the first address and its TTL:

```python title="naive_client.py"
    def address_for_request(self) -> str:
        return self.address


class ResolveEveryTimeClient:
    """Naive 2: ask DNS before every request. Always fresh, and a lookup on every request."""

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.lookups = 0
```

The demo gives the record a 2-second TTL so it runs in seconds. At 0.9 s the operator moves the
record to a new server; at 3.6 s, well past one TTL, the old server is switched off:

```text title="python naive_client.py (output, trimmed)"
t=0.0s  [ once] startup: api.shop.example.test. -> 203.0.113.10 (TTL 2 s, ignored)
t=0.9s  operator: new server is up, api now points to 198.51.100.20
t=3.6s  operator: waited longer than the TTL, switching off the old server 203.0.113.10
t=3.6s  [ once] request  9 -> 203.0.113.10   FAILED: connection refused (nothing runs there now)
t=3.6s  [every] request  9 -> 198.51.100.20  200 OK

resolve-every-time made 10 DNS lookups for 10 requests; the server answered 11 queries in total.
```

Resolve-once fails for ever. Its error, "connection refused", is what the operating system reports
when nothing is listening at an address, so it sends people off to debug the network instead of
DNS. Resolve-every-time is correct, and puts a DNS round trip (a question out, its answer back) in
front of every request. But the
model behind both is wrong in four more ways, and the rest of the page is about them:

1. **The TTL that matters is the one already cached.** Lowering it in the same edit as the change
   does nothing for copies fetched before the edit.
2. **Layers rewrite the TTL.** Resolvers cap it, and language runtimes replace it with their own
   number.
3. **Connections outlive DNS.** A connection that is already open never asks DNS again until it
   closes.
4. **"No" is cached too, and so are failures.** A name looked up before it existed, or during a
   bad publish, stays broken after the fix.

## How it works, piece by piece

### What each layer does to a TTL

Part 1 had one cache, the resolver. In a real request there can be four more holders of the answer
between the authoritative server and your code, and each has its own rule. So the useful question
is never "what is my TTL?" but **"what is the longest any holder of this answer might keep it?"**
Plan migrations, failovers and outages around that number.

Two of those holders need a word first. A **language runtime** is the part of Java, Node or a
browser that runs your code and gives it network functions; several of them keep a DNS cache of
their own. An **open connection** is a network connection to a server that a program set up once
and reuses for many requests. The figure follows one answer from the source down to your code;
the number on the right is how long each layer will keep using it:

<Figure caption="One answer with a 300-second TTL. The resolver passes on only the time it has left, the runtime applies its own rule, and an open connection keeps the address until it closes. The last two rows usually decide how long the old address stays in use.">
  <TtlLayers />
</Figure>

The authoritative server's TTL is only the first row. The rows below it are set by software you
may not control, and the bottom row is not DNS at all. That is why "we lowered the TTL to 60
seconds" so often ends with traffic on the old server an hour later. Here is each row in turn.

**Resolvers cap TTLs, in both directions.** Resolver software clamps the TTLs it receives to
configured limits. Two of those limits belong to features with their own sections below: a
*negative* TTL is how long a resolver keeps a "no such name" answer, and *serve-stale* means
answering from an expired copy when the authoritative servers cannot be reached. BIND and Unbound
are two widely run open-source resolvers. Their defaults, as of October 2026:

| Resolver | Longest positive TTL kept | Longest negative TTL kept | Serve-stale |
|---|---|---|---|
| BIND 9 | 604,800 s (7 days), `max-cache-ttl` | 10,800 s (3 h), `max-ncache-ttl` | off (`stale-answer-enable no`) |
| Unbound | 86,400 s (1 day), `cache-max-ttl` | 3,600 s (1 h), `cache-max-negative-ttl` | off (`serve-expired no`) |

Sources: the [BIND 9 reference](https://bind9.readthedocs.io/en/stable/reference.html) and the
[`unbound.conf` manual](https://unbound.docs.nlnetlabs.nl/en/latest/manpages/unbound.conf.html).
Both also have a *minimum* TTL option (`min-cache-ttl`, `cache-min-ttl`), off by default, which
some operators turn on to cut load. A resolver with a 300-second minimum turns your careful
60-second TTL into 300 seconds, and you will never see it in your own tests.

**Runtimes replace the TTL with their own number.** The standard `getaddrinfo()` call returns
addresses and no TTL at all, so anything built on it has to invent a keep-time:

- **Java** keeps successful lookups for 30 seconds and failed ones for 10, whatever the record
  says (`networkaddress.cache.ttl` and `networkaddress.cache.negative.ttl`); older versions, with
  some security settings turned on, kept them for ever
  ([OpenJDK `InetAddressCachePolicy`](https://github.com/openjdk/jdk/blob/master/src/java.base/share/classes/sun/net/InetAddressCachePolicy.java)).
- **Chrome** keeps an answer from the operating system for 60 seconds, because the operating
  system never told it the TTL. Chrome can also send DNS questions itself, with its built-in
  resolver, and then it uses the TTL, with a 60-second floor
  ([Eric Lawrence, 2022](https://textslashplain.com/2022/03/31/chromiums-dns-cache/)).
- **Node**'s `dns.lookup()`, which `fetch` and `http` use, keeps nothing. It runs
  `getaddrinfo()` on a pool of four background **threads** (separate lines of work that run
  alongside your code; this pool is called libuv's pool). Node also uses those four threads for
  file and crypto work, so slow DNS can stall unrelated work. `dns.resolve4()` with `{ ttl: true }`
  asks the network itself and returns real TTLs ([Node docs](https://nodejs.org/api/dns.html)).
- **glibc**, the C library behind most Linux programs, keeps nothing either. A Linux machine
  caches lookups only if it runs a local caching **daemon** (a program that runs in the
  background) such as `systemd-resolved` or `nscd`.

**Connections ignore DNS completely.** Setting up a connection costs round trips, so programs keep
connections open and reuse them. A **connection pool** is a set of open connections that a
program, typically to a database, hands out request by request. HTTP keep-alive does the same for
HTTP/1.1, and an HTTP/2 connection, or a gRPC channel (gRPC is a common way for services to call
each other), carries many requests over one connection. All of them look
the name up once, when they connect, and never again.

So a service with a pool of 20 connections that live for hours keeps talking to the old address
for hours, even if every cache above it switched within a minute. In a migration, the old server
must close its connections before clients will look the name up again. It can do that with an
HTTP/2 `GOAWAY` message (the server telling the client "finish what you are doing, then
reconnect"), with a maximum lifetime on the client's pool, or with a restart. Watch the old
server's open connections, not the clock.

### Changing a record: the old TTL decides

Part 1 showed that a change is not sent anywhere; each copy runs out on its own. One more fact
turns that into arithmetic: the copies were fetched at random moments within the last TTL, so their
expiries are spread evenly over the next full TTL.

Take a TTL of 3,600 s, one hour, and switch the old server off 10
minutes after the change. The copies expire evenly over the hour after the change, so by minute
10 only 10 of 60 minutes' worth have expired: roughly five caches in six still point at the old
server. Lowering the TTL in the same edit changes nothing, because the copies already out there
carry the old TTL. The only thing that helps is lowering it **at least one old TTL before** the
change, so that by the moment of the change every copy in the world is a short one.

The simulation below is 200 resolver caches holding one record. You choose the TTL before the move,
how early the TTL was lowered to 60 seconds (the **lead time**), when the old server goes off, and
the share of clients that, like the resolve-once client above, never look the name up again
(**pinned** clients). The defaults lower the TTL in the same edit as the address change; then slide the lead time up.

<MigrationSim />

The lead time is the whole trick. At zero, the sim switches the old server off with 176 of 200
caches still pointing at it, and 28% of all lookups in the next 80 minutes fail; the last cache
switches an hour after the change. With a lead of 60 minutes, which is one old TTL, every cache
holds a 60-second copy by the time of the change, the last one switches within about two minutes,
and the only failures come from the 2% of clients that never resolve again. A lead of 30 minutes
is half a fix: 68 caches still on the old address, 6% of lookups failing.

The price is in the query counter. With 60-second copies instead of one-hour ones, each cache asks
up to 60 times as often, and the sim counts about fifty times as many queries. That is why the TTL
goes back up once the move is done.

The full plan uses **`dig`**, a command-line tool that sends one DNS question to the server you
name after `@` and prints the whole reply:

1. One old TTL ahead (plus margin for resolvers that impose a minimum), lower the TTL to 60 s.
2. Change the address. Keep the old server running.
3. Ask your authoritative server directly (`dig @ns1.your-provider.example api.example.in`) to
   confirm the change, and ask at least two public resolvers to see what users get.
4. Make the old server close idle connections, then wait until its traffic is near zero. The tail
   is pinned clients: find them in its access log.
5. Switch it off and raise the TTL again.

**Changing name servers is slower still.** Look inside the referral from step 2 of Part 1's table.
The `.in` zone holds only the delegation for `example.in`: `NS` records naming the servers that run
the child zone. When those servers have names inside the child zone, there is a loop: to find the
address of `ns1.example.in` you must ask the `example.in` servers, which means `ns1`. So the parent
also hands out their addresses, called **glue**. (A zone hosted at a DNS provider names the
provider's servers, which live in the provider's own zone, so it needs no glue.)

The delegation therefore lives in two places: the parent zone holds `NS` records that point to
your servers, and your own zone holds its own copy. The top box below is the parent, the bottom
box is your zone:

<Figure caption="The parent zone holds only the delegation: NS records and the glue address of a name server inside the child zone. The child zone holds the real records, including its own copy of the NS records. The two copies have separate TTLs and can disagree.">
  <Delegation />
</Figure>

The `.com` servers hand out delegations with a TTL of 172,800 seconds, two days. So a resolver
that learned the old delegation an hour before your switch can keep asking the old provider for 47
more hours. The DNS standards are published as numbered documents called **RFCs** (requests for comments),
and one of them, [RFC 2181](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2181) (section 5.4.1), ranks the
child zone's own answer above the parent's referral. So a resolver that has asked
your old provider may keep the old provider's copy of your `NS` records instead. Keep the old
provider serving an identical zone for at least two days, and update the `NS` records in both
zones.

### Negative caching: "no" is cached, and so is "I could not find out"

To see where a "no" gets its keep-time, we need to look inside a DNS reply. Every reply starts with
a header carrying a **result code**, the way an HTTP response carries a status. After the header
come sections: the *answer* (records for the question) and the *authority* (records about who is
responsible for the zone), among others. Three result codes matter here: NOERROR (0), SERVFAIL (2,
"I could not get an answer") and NXDOMAIN (3, "no such name").

A resolver that asks about a name that has no records gets one of two negative answers:

- **NXDOMAIN** (result code 3): the name does not exist, for any type.
- **NODATA**: the name exists but has no records of the type asked for. There is no code for it:
  the answer is NOERROR with an empty answer section.

Caching "no" exists because most of these questions repeat: typos, scanners, retries, and the
extra lookups Kubernetes adds (covered under "How real systems do it") would otherwise all reach
the authoritative servers. But a "no" has no record to carry a TTL, so it borrows one from the
zone's SOA record.

**What the SOA is.** The **SOA** (start of authority) record holds a zone's own settings. Every
zone has exactly one, at its top.

**Why it matters here.** The authoritative server puts the SOA in the authority section of every
negative answer, and its last field says how long the "no" may be kept.

**Example.** Here is a negative answer, printed in `dig`'s format:

```text title="a negative answer for beta.example.in (illustrative, in dig's format)"
;; ->>HEADER<<- status: NXDOMAIN
;; ANSWER SECTION: (empty)
;; AUTHORITY SECTION:
example.in.  3600  IN  SOA  ns1.example.in. hostmaster.example.in. 2026102301 7200 900 1209600 300
; SOA TTL 3600, then: primary server, contact, serial, refresh, retry, expire, minimum (300)
```

**The technical version.** Serial, refresh, retry and expire are for **secondary servers**: extra
authoritative servers that hold copies of the zone and use these numbers to decide when to fetch
updates from the primary. The last field is the *minimum*. A resolver caches the "no" for the
smaller of the SOA record's own TTL and that minimum
([RFC 2308](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308)): here min(3,600, 300) = 300 seconds, further
capped by the resolver's own limit from the table above.

Many real zones use far longer values: in October 2026, `example.com` allowed a negative answer to
be cached for 1,800 s and `wikipedia.org` for 3,600 s. Our toy zone uses 5 seconds. The demo asks
for a name a moment before the operator creates it:

```text title="python ttl_cache.py (output, part 2)"
t=4.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: NXDOMAIN, cached for 5.0 s more
t=4.1s  operator: created beta.shop.example.test. -> 198.51.100.30
t=5.6s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 3.5 s more
t=7.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 2.0 s more
t=8.6s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: still NXDOMAIN, cached for 0.5 s more
t=10.1s  lookup beta.shop.example.test.: 198.51.100.30
```

The name existed from 4.1 s, and the cache kept saying "no such name" until its 5 seconds ran out.

One less known rule makes a negative answer reach further than the name you asked about. **An
NXDOMAIN covers everything below it**: if `beta.example.in` does not exist, nothing under it can,
so a resolver may answer NXDOMAIN for `api.beta.example.in` from that one cached answer
([RFC 8020](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8020)). Signed zones have a second rule of this
kind, which the DNSSEC section covers.

**Failures are cached as well.** When a resolver cannot get an answer at all (the servers time
out, return SERVFAIL, or send data that fails a DNSSEC check), it now has to remember the failure
for at least 1 second and at most 5 minutes, and not ask again meanwhile
([RFC 9520](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9520.html), December 2023). The reason is load:
without it, every user lookup turns into fresh queries to servers that are already struggling.
When Facebook's name servers disappeared from the internet in October 2021, Cloudflare saw about 30
times the usual queries for Facebook's names on its 1.1.1.1 resolver, as clients and resolvers
retried ([Cloudflare, 2021](https://blog.cloudflare.com/october-2021-facebook-outage/)).

:::caution[Never test a name before it exists]
Opening a link, pointing a monitoring probe at it or letting a deploy pipeline poll a hostname before you
create the record caches a "no" for the negative TTL, in every resolver that was asked. Create the
record first, then test it. Keep the SOA minimum at 60–300 s so a mistake heals quickly.
:::

### When the authoritative side fails

When your name servers stop answering, nothing breaks at first. Each cache keeps answering from
its copy, and fails only when that copy expires and it cannot fetch a new one. The copies expire
evenly over one TTL, so without help the share of users failing grows roughly as *time since the
outage ÷ TTL*: all of them after one TTL.

**What serve-stale is.** **Serve-stale** means that when the authoritative servers cannot be
reached, the resolver hands out its expired copy instead of an error.

**Why it exists.** When the DNS servers are down, the web servers behind the name are usually
fine, and the old address is almost always still right. An old answer beats no answer.

**Example.** A resolver fetched `api.example.in` (TTL 300) at 10:00:00, and your name servers went
down at 10:03. At 10:06 a user asks. The copy expired at 10:05 and the refresh times out. Without
serve-stale the user gets SERVFAIL. With it, the user gets the old address with a TTL of 30
seconds, so the client asks again at 10:06:30, and the same thing repeats until a refresh succeeds
or the resolver's stale window runs out.

**The technical version.** [RFC 8767](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767) (2020) defines it:
the resolver keeps trying to refresh in the background, hands out stale answers with a 30-second
TTL, and keeps expired data for one to three days. It is off by default in BIND and Unbound (the
table above); large public resolvers use some form of it, which is part of why outages look
different from different networks.

The simulation runs 200 resolver caches for one popular name through an outage of your name
servers. You set the record's TTL, the outage length and the share of resolvers that serve stale:

The TTL sets how fast the failure spreads; serve-stale sets how much of it users see. With a
300-second TTL and no serve-stale, every cache in the sim is failing by minute five. At 3,600 s,
58% of lookups fail after half an hour and almost all by the end of the hour. Serve-stale on half
the resolvers halves the failure, and on all of them hides it completely. And the moment the
servers come back, every failing cache recovers on its next lookup.

**Now publish bad data instead.** AWS keeps the DNS records for its DynamoDB database service up to
date with automation: one program writes plans (the list of addresses each name should have), and
several worker programs apply them. On 19–20 October 2025, a **race** between two of those workers (two programs
working at the same time, where the result depends on which finishes first) left the record for `dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com` with no addresses: a slow worker applied an
old plan at the moment a faster one's clean-up deleted that plan. From 11:48 PM PDT nothing could
resolve the endpoint, including AWS services that depend on DynamoDB. The record was restored by
2:25 AM, and clients recovered as their cached answers expired, by 2:40 AM
([AWS post-event summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/)).

An empty answer is a valid answer. Serve-stale applies only when the authoritative servers cannot
be reached, so it never kicks in. The "no addresses" answer is cached as a negative answer for the
negative TTL, which outlives the fix. The second sim keeps the servers up and publishes the empty

of lookups are still failing ten minutes later, and the last failure comes about 15 minutes after

that *writes* DNS: check that a plan is still the newest one at the moment you apply it, refuse to
publish an empty set for a name that has always had addresses, and have a tested way to put a

### How resolvers choose a name server

Every zone lists several name servers, and the resolver picks one for each query. How it picks
decides what a slow or dead name server costs you.

Resolvers keep a running **round-trip time** (RTT) for each server address: how long a question to
that server took to get its reply, averaged over recent queries. They mostly prefer fast servers,
but not only fast ones, because a server measured as slow once may have recovered.

Unbound's rule shows how this works. It picks at random among all servers within 400 ms of the
fastest, assumes 376 ms for a server it knows nothing about, and remembers what it learned for 15
minutes
([Unbound docs](https://unbound.docs.nlnetlabs.nl/en/latest/reference/history/info-timeout-server-selection.html)).
With three name servers measured at 20, 90 and 600 ms, the band runs from 20 to 20 + 400 = 420
ms. Queries are spread at random over the first two, and the 600 ms server gets none until Unbound
forgets its measurement and tries it again.

Three consequences:

- **Every name server must be equally strong.** A far-away or small server you added "as a backup"
  is inside the band whenever it is within 400 ms of the fastest, so it gets a real share of
  production queries. Put anycast behind every listed name server address.
- **A dead name server costs latency, not answers.** A resolver that tries it waits for a timeout
  (a few hundred milliseconds) and then tries another. Until its RTT data marks the server bad, a
  share of lookups that find nothing cached pay that wait. Our resilient resolver below shows the
  same cost: a dead first server added about 400 ms.
- **Two providers survive what one cannot.** When your DNS provider has a bad day (a flood of
  attack traffic, a broken release), every name it serves goes down with it. Because resolvers already spread queries
  across all listed name servers and fail over between them, listing name servers from two
  independent providers, both serving identical records, turns a provider outage into extra
  latency. The cost is keeping two providers in sync, which gets harder with DNSSEC, covered below:
  both must sign with keys the parent vouches for
  ([RFC 8901](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8901)).

### DNS as a load balancer, and why it is lumpy

**What it is.** **DNS load balancing** spreads users over several servers by returning several
addresses for one name (rotating their order each time, called **round robin**) or different
addresses to different askers.

**Why it exists.** It is the cheapest global load balancing there is. A **load balancer** is a
machine that sits in front of servers and spreads requests over them; DNS load balancing needs no
such machine in the path of the traffic, and it works for users anywhere.

**Example.** `shop.example.in` returns four addresses. If every user picked one at random, each
server would get a quarter of the traffic.

**The technical version.** Its unit of balancing is the problem. The authoritative server decides
once per **resolver cache** per TTL, and every user behind that cache gets the same answer until it
expires.

Two facts make that worse than it sounds. Resolver populations are lopsided: a few large
internet-provider and public resolvers carry most users. And clients often do not choose at random from the list.
glibc's `getaddrinfo()` re-sorts the addresses by a set of preference rules
([RFC 6724](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6724), configurable in
[`gai.conf`](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man5/gai.conf.5.html)), so the order DNS sent is not
always the order clients see, and many clients connect only to the first address.

The sim below puts four servers behind one name. Users sit behind resolvers whose sizes fall off
the way real ones do: one large, a few medium, many small. You set the number of resolvers, the
share of clients that take the first address, and the TTL:

<RoundRobinSim />

Three things to take away. First, the imbalance at any moment is set by how big the largest
resolvers are and how many clients take the first address, not by the TTL: with ten resolvers the
busiest server peaks at about 3.5 times its share, and some server is always above 1.5 times.
Second, the TTL sets only how long one server stays hot: up to 10 minutes at 300 s, under 2
minutes at 30 s, most of an hour at 1,800 s. Third, averages lie: over two hours the busiest
server looks only 1.19 times busier than fair, so dashboards built on averages miss the overload
that matters. Size servers behind DNS balancing for the peak, and put a real load balancer behind
each address.

**Failover through DNS is slow by construction.** A **health check** is a probe that a DNS
provider sends to your server every few seconds; when enough probes fail, the provider stops
handing out that server's address. Its speed is the sum of a chain. Route 53, Amazon's managed
DNS, checks every 30 seconds by default (10 seconds is an option) and needs 3 failures in a row,
so 3 × 30 = about 90 seconds to notice
([Route 53 docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-determining-health-of-endpoints.html)).
AWS recommends a TTL of 60 seconds or less on records tied to a health check
([Route 53 docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/resource-record-sets-values-latency.html),
as of November 2026). Add 60 seconds of TTL, then Java's 30 seconds or Chrome's 60, and open
connections that never move: two to three minutes is the realistic best case.

**Geographic answers see the resolver, not the user.** An authoritative server that answers by
location (often called **GeoDNS**) sees where the *question* came from, and the question comes from
the resolver. In the figure, two users in different cities ask through two different resolvers:

<Figure caption="Two resolvers ask for the same name and get the site nearest to each of them. The authoritative server sees the resolver's address, which is close to the user only when the user's resolver is.">
  <GeoDns />
</Figure>

So a user in Chennai whose public resolver reaches you from Singapore is sent to Singapore. One
extension tries to fix this.

**What ECS is.** **EDNS Client Subnet** (ECS) lets a resolver pass on part of the user's address
with the question, so the authoritative server can answer for the user's location.

**Why it exists.** It fixes the Chennai user's answer without the resolver having to move.

**Example.** For a user at 198.51.100.86, the resolver sends `198.51.100.0/24`: the first three of
the four numbers, with the last one hidden. That names a **subnet**, a block of 256 neighbouring
addresses (198.51.100.0 to 198.51.100.255). A **bit** is a single 0 or 1, and each of the four
numbers is 8 bits, so "/24" means "the first 24 bits", the first three numbers.

**The technical version.** ECS is one of the optional extras a query can carry, through a
mechanism called EDNS that the next section explains
([RFC 7871](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7871)). It splits the cache: each answer is now
valid only for one subnet, so a resolver serving users from 1,000 subnets may hold 1,000 copies of
one name, each fetched separately, and its **hit rate** for that name (the share of questions
answered from the cache) falls accordingly. Use DNS to pick a region; let a load balancer pick the
server.

### Size limits: UDP, truncation and TCP

This section is about how big a DNS message may be, so first the units and the two ways DNS
messages travel.

A **byte** is the unit computers measure data in: enough to hold one letter of plain text, so a
512-byte message is about as big as 512 characters. A **packet** is one chunk of data sent across
the network on its own, with a small header in front saying where it goes.

DNS can travel over two protocols. **UDP** sends one packet and does nothing more: no connection,
no confirmation, no resending. **TCP** first sets up a connection with a handshake (at least one
extra round trip), then delivers any amount of data reliably and in order. DNS starts with UDP
because one question and one answer usually fit in one packet each, so it costs a single round
trip. Both use **port** 53: the number that tells the receiving machine which program a packet is
for. (The [TCP vs UDP](/networking/tcp-vs-udp/) page goes deeper.)

The original standard capped UDP answers at 512 bytes, which is plenty for addresses: an answer
with one A record measured 72 bytes in our tests. But signed answers and long text records need
more, so an extension was added.

**What EDNS(0) is.** **EDNS(0)** ([RFC 6891](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6891)) is the
mechanism that lets a DNS query carry extra options, the most important being "I can accept a
bigger UDP answer".

**Why it exists.** The 512-byte limit was fixed in the original message format, and there was no
field to change it.

**Example.** `dig` prints a query's options as `; EDNS: version: 0, flags: do; udp: 1232`. Here
`udp: 1232` says the client accepts UDP answers up to 1,232 bytes, and `do` asks for DNSSEC
signatures (next section).

**The technical version.** The client appends a small pseudo-record, called OPT, to its query.
It is not a real record and is never cached; it carries the buffer size, the flags and options
such as ECS from the previous section.

A bigger buffer has a catch. Every network link has a largest packet it can carry: 1,500 bytes on
Ethernet, less over tunnels and VPNs. A larger packet is split into IP **fragments** that travel
separately and are put back together at the end. Lose one fragment and the whole answer is lost.
Only the first fragment carries the UDP port numbers, so many firewalls drop the rest, and an
attacker who times it right can slip in a forged later fragment.

Since [DNS Flag Day 2020](https://www.dnsflagday.net/2020/), a change that the makers of the major
DNS software agreed to ship together, resolvers advertise 1,232 bytes, a size that never needs fragmenting. The number comes from IPv6: every IPv6 link must carry
packets of 1,280 bytes, and 1,280 minus 40 bytes of IPv6 header minus 8 bytes of UDP header is
1,232.

An answer that does not fit is not sent in part. The server replies with only the header and the
**TC** (truncated) flag set, and the client asks again over TCP, which costs at least one more
round trip for the TCP handshake. What pushes answers past the line is mostly DNSSEC, the next
section, which adds signatures and keys to answers. Its key records (called DNSKEY) are large when
they use RSA keys rather than the smaller ECDSA kind, and while a key is being replaced the old
and new keys are published together.

The figure is a ruler of answer sizes in bytes, with the two UDP limits drawn on it. For now, read
only the sizes; the next section explains the key names:

<Figure caption="Answer sizes against the two UDP limits. Plain address records are tiny; signed key sets with RSA keys cross 1,232 bytes, especially during a key rollover when two keys are published; long TXT records cross it by far. Everything right of the red line needs TCP.">
  <SizeBudget />
</Figure>

The A record and the toy TXT record are our own measurements; the key-set sizes are from APNIC,
the internet address registry for the Asia-Pacific region
([Huston, 2021](https://labs.apnic.net/presentations/store/2021-11-25-rsa4096.pdf)). Our resilient


The UDP reply was 39 bytes of header and question with TC set; the same answer over TCP was 2,835
bytes. TCP is not optional: [RFC 9210](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9210) (2022) requires
every resolver and server to support it and every network to allow it. A firewall that blocks TCP
port 53 breaks only the big answers, typically DNSSEC-signed ones and domains with many long TXT
records, which makes it one of the more confusing partial outages. Keep hot names small: a handful of addresses, no long TXT records on the name your API clients look up.

### DNSSEC: what it buys and what it costs

Start with the attack DNSSEC exists for. A resolver accepts a reply if it matches its question in
three ways: the same question, sent back to the same **source port** the resolver asked from, and
the same **query ID**, a random number from 0 to 65,535 (16 bits give 2<sup>16</sup> = 65,536
values) that the resolver put in the question. Nothing else in a plain reply proves where it came
from.

So an attacker who knows a resolver is about to ask can flood it with forged replies, each
guessing a different ID and port: 65,536 IDs times tens of thousands of ports. That is a lot of
guesses, but they are cheap to send. One hit **poisons** that cache: every user of the resolver
gets the attacker's address for the whole TTL.

**What DNSSEC is.** **DNSSEC** ([RFC 4033](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4033)) adds
signatures to DNS records, so a resolver can check that a record came from the zone's owner,
unchanged.

**Why it exists.** Guessing the ID and port is the only protection plain DNS has. A signature
cannot be guessed.

**Example.** The idea uses a **key pair**: two linked keys. The **private key** stays with the
zone's owner and is used to make a signature over some data; the **public key** is published, and
anyone holding it can check the signature. Say the owner signs `shop.example.in A 192.0.2.10` and
gets a 64-byte signature. An attacker who swaps the address for 203.0.113.66 keeps the signature,
but the check with the public key now fails, so the resolver throws the answer away. Making a new
signature that passes needs the private key, which the attacker does not have.

**The technical version.** The zone's public key is published in the zone as a **DNSKEY** record.
Each record set gets a signature, an **RRSIG** record, computed over the set with the private key.
A signed answer arrives as the set plus its signature. Here is one, printed by `dig`, with the
fields of the signature labelled underneath:

```text title="a signed answer (illustrative, in dig's format, signature shortened)"
shop.example.in.  300  IN  A      192.0.2.10
shop.example.in.  300  IN  RRSIG  A 13 3 300 20261106000000 20261023000000 41520 example.in. Mg5r...
;                                 ^ covers the A set, algorithm 13 (ECDSA P-256),
;                                   valid 23 Oct to 6 Nov 2026, made with key 41520 of example.in
```

That leaves one question: why trust the DNSKEY? Whoever can forge the A record can forge a key
too. So the parent vouches for it, using a **hash**: a short fingerprint of some data, which
changes completely if the data changes by a single bit and cannot be turned back into the data.
The `.in` zone publishes a **DS** record holding a hash of `example.in`'s key, and signs that DS
with its own key. `.in`'s key is vouched for by a DS in the root, and the root's key is built into
every resolver that checks signatures (a **validating** resolver). Checking a name means walking
that chain from the top:

<Figure caption="Each parent publishes a hash of its child's key, and each zone signs its own records. A validating resolver checks every link from the root down; if any link fails, it answers SERVFAIL rather than an answer it cannot verify.">
  <DnssecChain />
</Figure>

Keys are replaced from time to time, a **key rollover**: publish the new DNSKEY next to the old
one, wait at least one DNSKEY TTL so every cache holds both, sign with the new key, and remove the
old one later. Most zones run two keys. The key-signing key (**KSK**) signs only the zone's DNSKEY
set and is the one the parent's DS points to, so replacing it means changing the DS at the parent.
The zone-signing key signs everything else and can be replaced without the parent.

**Signing a "no" makes one answer cover a range of names.** A signature can only cover data that
exists, and a name that does not exist has no data. So a signed zone sorts its names and signs the
gaps between them, as **NSEC** records. A zone holding `alpha`, `gamma` and `zulu` publishes
`alpha NSEC gamma` ("there is nothing between `alpha` and `gamma`") and `gamma NSEC zulu`, and a
question about `beta` gets the first one as its proof of NXDOMAIN.

A resolver may reuse a cached proof for any name it covers
([RFC 8198](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8198); BIND and Unbound both support it). It then
answers NXDOMAIN for `beta`, `delta` and `fox` from that one cached `alpha NSEC gamma` record,
without asking. Create `beta`, and a resolver holding that proof can deny it, until the proof
expires, even to users who never asked for it before.

DNSSEC protects only users whose resolver validates: in 2025 about 36% of users sat behind
validating resolvers, while only about 7% of domains were signed, with a DS record at their parent
([CircleID, citing APNIC, February 2026](https://circleid.com/posts/dnssec-doesnt-need-a-better-story-it-needs-a-better-tuesday)).
What it costs:

- **Bigger answers.** Every answer carries signatures. RSA and ECDSA are two families of signature
  maths; ECDSA's signatures are much smaller for the same strength. An RSA-2048 signature is
  2,048 bits, 256 bytes; an ECDSA P-256 signature is 64 bytes. With RSA keys, key-set answers
  cross the 1,232-byte line above and need TCP.
- **More lookups on a cold cache** (one that has nothing saved yet). The resolver must also fetch
  DNSKEY and DS records for every zone on the way down. They cache well, so the cost lands on cold caches and rarely used zones.
- **It fails closed.** A wrong signature anywhere in the chain makes the name disappear for every
  validating resolver's users, while every server is up. On 5 May 2026 DENIC, the `.de` registry,
  rolled its signing key with a new system. That system generated a different key on each of three
  **hardware security modules** (locked devices that hold private keys and sign without letting
  them out) but published only one of the keys. Validating resolvers returned SERVFAIL for `.de`
  names, including unsigned domains: for those, `.de` serves a signed proof that no DS record
  exists, an NSEC-style "no" like the one above, and those signatures failed too. The failure grew
  for about three hours as cached answers expired, until operators such as Cloudflare switched
  validation off for `.de` ([DENIC](https://blog.denic.de/en/analysis-of-the-dns-outage-on-5-may-2026/),
  [Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/de-tld-outage-dnssec/)).
- **Validation itself can be attacked.** KeyTrap (CVE-2023-50387; a CVE number is the public ID of
  a security flaw) was a flaw in the specification, patched in February 2024. A zone with many keys
  and signatures made the validator try every combination of key and signature, and one crafted
  packet could stall BIND for up to 16 hours
  ([ISC advisory](https://kb.isc.org/docs/cve-2023-50387),
  [research paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.03133)).

The root's own KSK was replaced in October 2026: KSK-2024 signs the root's key set from 11 October
2026 ([ICANN](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/ksk-rollover-en)).

:::note[From the trenches]
If you sign, sign through a DNS provider that automates key rollovers, use ECDSA P-256 (algorithm
13) to keep answers small, and treat every DS change at the registrar (the company you bought the domain from, which
passes it on to the registry) as a migration with a TTL: the parent's DS TTL, often a day, is how
long a mistake lives. A half-managed DNSSEC setup is
worse than none.
:::

## Choosing TTLs

Almost every DNS decision an application team makes is a TTL. The table uses a few terms not met
yet:

- the **apex** is the zone's own name, `example.in` itself;
- a **CDN** is a network of servers near users that serves your content (its own page is linked
  at the end);
- **weighted** records send a chosen share of users to each address, for example 10% to a new
  version;
- `MX` records say where email for the domain goes;
- `CAA` records say which certificate authorities (the companies that issue the certificates
  browsers trust for HTTPS) may issue certificates for the name;
- verification records are `TXT` records that prove to an outside service that you own the domain.

Where I would start:

| Record | TTL | Why |
|---|---|---|
| API or app endpoint you control | 300 s; 60 s from one old TTL before planned work | Moves are planned; load and outage survival matter the rest of the time |
| Health-checked failover or weighted records | 30–60 s | Their whole job is to change fast; pay the query cost |
| Apex (`example.in` itself) and `www` behind a CDN | whatever the CDN sets, often 300 s | The CDN's own names change behind it |
| MX, TXT, CAA, verification records | 1 hour to 1 day | Rarely change; long TTLs ride out a provider outage |
| NS records in your own zone | 1–2 days, to match the parent | Short child NS TTLs only add load |
| SOA minimum (negative TTL) | 60–300 s | A name tested too early heals within minutes |

**How to choose.** Long TTLs for what rarely changes, because they protect you in a DNS outage and
cost nothing. Short TTLs only where change speed is the point. And the TTL that matters on the day
of a change is the one you set one old TTL earlier.

## Build it yourself

Two pieces, each closer to production than the naive clients. Everything is standard library only
and runs against the toy server on your own machine. DNS messages are binary: fixed-size fields of
bytes, not lines of text like an HTTP/1.1 request. So a small shared module, `dnswire`, builds
questions and parses replies; the snippets call it as `w`.

**A cache that honours TTLs, including negative ones.** The interesting part is where the expiry
comes from: the shortest TTL in a CNAME chain for a positive answer, the SOA rule for a negative
one.

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="ttl_cache.py"
# runnable version: examples/dns/python/ttl_cache.py
    def _ask(self, name: str, rtype: str) -> Entry:
        _, query = w.build_query(name, rtype)
        reply = w.parse_message(w.ask_udp(*self.server, query))
        now = time.monotonic()
        # Follow any CNAMEs in the answer; the chain is only as fresh as its shortest-lived link.
        records = [r for r in reply.answer if r.type in (rtype, "CNAME")]
        addresses = [r.value for r in records if r.type == rtype]
        if reply.rcode == "NOERROR" and addresses:
            return Entry(addresses, "NOERROR", now + min(r.ttl for r in records))
        # NXDOMAIN (no such name) or NODATA (name exists, no records of this type).
        # Negative TTL = the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field (RFC 2308).
        soa = next((r for r in reply.authority if r.type == "SOA"), None)
        negative_ttl = min(soa.ttl, soa.data["minimum"]) if soa else 0  # no SOA: do not cache
        return Entry([], reply.rcode if reply.rcode != "NOERROR" else "NODATA", now + negative_ttl)
```

  <TabItem label="JavaScript">
```js title="ttl-cache.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/dns/js/ttl-cache.mjs
  async ask(name, type) {
    const { query } = w.buildQuery(name, type);
    const reply = w.parseMessage(await w.askUdp(...this.server, query));
    const now = performance.now();
    // Follow any CNAMEs in the answer; the chain is only as fresh as its shortest-lived link.
    const records = reply.answer.filter((r) => r.type === type || r.type === 'CNAME');
    const addresses = records.filter((r) => r.type === type).map((r) => r.value);
    if (reply.rcode === 'NOERROR' && addresses.length) {
      return { addresses, rcode: 'NOERROR', expires: now + Math.min(...records.map((r) => r.ttl)) * 1000 };
    }
    // NXDOMAIN (no such name) or NODATA (name exists, no records of this type).
    // Negative TTL = the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field (RFC 2308).
    const soa = reply.authority.find((r) => r.type === 'SOA');
    const negativeTtl = soa ? Math.min(soa.ttl, soa.data.minimum) : 0; // no SOA: do not cache
    return { addresses: [], rcode: reply.rcode === 'NOERROR' ? 'NODATA' : reply.rcode, expires: now + negativeTtl * 1000 };
  }
```
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

Read it like this. The method sends one question and notes the time on a **monotonic clock**, a
clock that only moves forward, so a change to the system time cannot make entries expire early or
late. If the answer holds addresses, the entry expires after the shortest TTL among the records
used, including any CNAME aliases followed on the way. Otherwise it is a "no", and the entry
expires after the smaller of the SOA's TTL and its minimum field, exactly the rule from the
negative-caching section. The rest of the class (not shown) checks the cache before calling this
method.

**A resolver that survives bad days.** The production-shaped version forwards questions to several
**upstreams** (the resolvers or servers it asks) with a short timeout each, checks that the reply
matches the question, retries over TCP on truncation, clamps silly TTLs between a floor and a
ceiling, and serves stale when everything is down:

<Tabs syncKey="lang">
  <TabItem label="Python">
```python title="resilient_resolver.py"
# runnable version: examples/dns/python/resilient_resolver.py
    def resolve(self, name: str, rtype: str = "A") -> list[str]:
        key, now = (name.lower(), rtype), time.monotonic()
        entry = self.cache.get(key)
        if entry and now < entry.expires:
            return entry.values
        try:
            values, ttl = self._ask_upstreams(name, rtype)
        except LookupFailed as err:
            if entry and now < entry.stale_until:  # serve stale: an old answer beats no answer
                entry.expires = now + STALE_TTL
                print(f"    ! every upstream failed ({err}); serving the expired answer for {STALE_TTL} s")
                return entry.values
            raise
        ttl = max(MIN_TTL, min(MAX_TTL, ttl))
        self.cache[key] = Entry(values, now + ttl, now + ttl + MAX_STALE)
        return values
```
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="JavaScript">
```js title="resilient-resolver.mjs"
// runnable version: examples/dns/js/resilient-resolver.mjs
  async resolve(name, type = 'A') {
    const key = `${name.toLowerCase()}|${type}`;
    const now = performance.now();
    const entry = this.cache.get(key);
    if (entry && now < entry.expires) return entry.values;
    let values, ttl;
    try {
      [values, ttl] = await this.askUpstreams(name, type);
    } catch (err) {
      if (err instanceof LookupFailed && entry && now < entry.staleUntil) { // serve stale: an old answer beats no answer
        entry.expires = now + STALE_TTL * 1000;
        console.log(`    ! every upstream failed (${err.message}); serving the expired answer for ${STALE_TTL} s`);
        return entry.values;
      }
      throw err;
    }
    ttl = Math.max(MIN_TTL, Math.min(MAX_TTL, ttl));
    this.cache.set(key, { values, expires: now + ttl * 1000, staleUntil: now + (ttl + MAX_STALE) * 1000 });
    return values;
  }
```
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

Read it like this. A fresh cache entry is returned at once. Otherwise the resolver asks its
upstreams (the full file tries each in turn with a 400 ms timeout and retries over TCP on TC). If
every upstream fails and the expired entry is younger than `MAX_STALE` (3,600 s in the example),
it hands that entry out again for `STALE_TTL` (30 s, the RFC 8767 value) before trying again. A
successful answer has its TTL clamped between `MIN_TTL` and `MAX_TTL` (1 and 300 s) and is stored
with both its expiry and its stale limit.

The demo's first upstream is a port where nothing listens, and later the toy server stops
answering altogether:

```text title="node resilient-resolver.mjs (output, trimmed)"

         api.shop.example.test. -> ["203.0.113.10"]  in 406 ms
t=0.8s  3. the authoritative server goes down; the cached answer expires

t=3.8s  4. a name we never cached, while everything is down
         failed, nothing stale to fall back on: 127.0.0.1:5399 ETIMEDOUT; 127.0.0.1:8053 ETIMEDOUT
```

The dead upstream cost a full 400 ms timeout before the healthy one answered, which is why the
timeout per attempt must be short. Step 3 is serve-stale doing its job; step 4 is its limit: it
can only replay answers it has already seen. Full code, the toy server and run instructions:

## How real systems do it

**Kubernetes multiplies every external lookup.** Kubernetes, a widely used system for running
programs across a cluster of machines, runs each program in a **pod** (one or more processes
sharing a network address). The cluster's own resolver is usually CoreDNS. To let a pod reach a
service by a short name such as `payments`, each pod's `/etc/resolv.conf` lists **search
suffixes**, endings the stub resolver tries adding to a name, such as
`my-namespace.svc.cluster.local`. It also sets `options ndots:5`
([Kubernetes docs](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/services-networking/dns-pod-service/)): a
name with fewer than five dots is first tried with every suffix.

So a lookup of `api.stripe.com` (two dots) is tried as
`api.stripe.com.my-namespace.svc.cluster.local` and with two more suffixes, each an NXDOMAIN,
before the real name. That is 3 + 1 = 4 names, and with A and AAAA asked separately, one lookup
becomes 8 queries to CoreDNS. Write external names with a trailing dot (`api.stripe.com.`), which
marks them complete, or lower `ndots` in the pod's DNS settings (`dnsConfig`).

**nginx resolves upstream names once, unless told otherwise.** nginx is a web server often placed
in front of applications to forward requests to them; those applications are its **upstreams**.
A hostname written directly in its
`proxy_pass` setting (where to forward) is resolved at startup and kept: resolve-once, in a config
file. As of October 2026, open-source nginx can re-resolve upstream servers since version 1.27.3
([nginx docs](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html)):

```nginx title="nginx: follow DNS changes for an upstream"
resolver 10.0.0.2 valid=30s;          # re-resolve at most every 30 s, whatever the TTL says
upstream payments {
    zone payments 64k;                # resolved addresses live in shared memory
    server api.partner-bank.example resolve;
}
```

nginx runs several worker processes, copies of itself that share the incoming work. The `zone`
line gives the addresses a place in memory that all of them share, so one lookup updates them all.

**Managed DNS sells the lease as a product.** Route 53's routing policies (weighted, latency,
geolocation, failover, multivalue with up to eight healthy addresses) are all ways of choosing
which lease to hand to which resolver
([Route 53 docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/routing-policy.html)).
Every one of them inherits the limits above: decisions per resolver, delivered one TTL late.

## Gotchas and war stories

**"It works on my laptop."** Your `dig` asks one resolver, which may have fetched a fresh copy a
second ago. After a change, ask the authoritative server directly for the truth, then two or three
public resolvers for what users see. `dig +norecurse @8.8.8.8 name` tells that resolver not to go
looking, so it shows only what it already has cached.

**A TTL change that never happened.** Some DNS dashboards apply the TTL to new records only, or
round it up to a plan minimum. Before a migration, check the TTL your authoritative server actually
serves, not the one in the form.

:::danger[Dangling records are a security hole]
A CNAME that points at a cloud resource you deleted, say `promo.example.in` pointing at an app
name on a hosting platform, is a **dangling record**. Anyone who can claim that resource name on
the platform now serves content for your subdomain, and can get HTTPS certificates for it: a
**subdomain takeover** ([Microsoft's guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/fundamentals/subdomain-takeover)).
Put "remove the DNS record" on every decommissioning checklist, and audit CNAMEs that point outside
your own zones.
:::

**Every name sits under zones you do not run.** The `.de` failure took down domains whose owners
did nothing wrong, and the October 2025 DynamoDB failure took down services that only depended on
an endpoint. Know which zones and which DNS-backed endpoints your critical path depends on, and
what their TTLs give you when they fail.

## Trade-offs and when not to use it

- **Freshness against load and resilience.** Short TTLs mean fast changes, more queries, a bigger
  bill and less time to survive a provider outage. You choose per record.
- **DNS steering is coarse.** It decides per resolver and reacts in TTLs; a load balancer decides
  per connection or request and reacts in seconds. Use DNS to choose a region.
- **DNSSEC trades availability for authenticity.** It stops forged answers for validating users and
  turns any signing mistake into a total outage for them.
- **Encrypted DNS moves trust.** DNS over HTTPS or over TLS encrypts the questions between you and
  the resolver. That hides your lookups from the network and shows them all to one resolver
  operator. It does not prove the answer is genuine; DNSSEC does that.

When should you not lean on DNS? Where endpoints change every few seconds, such as autoscaling
pods, every layer's caching works against you: use a service registry (a live list of which
servers are up, which clients query directly) or a load balancer with health checks. And DNS cannot make per-request decisions; it never sees requests.

## Interview corner

1. **"You changed a record and some users still reach the old server an hour later. Why?"** Caches
   hold the old copy for up to the *old* TTL; runtimes and pools keep it longer. Strong answers add
   the plan: lower the TTL one old TTL ahead, keep the old server until its traffic and connections
   drain, and check the authoritative server directly.
2. **"Your DNS provider is down. What do users see, and when?"** Nothing until copies expire, then
   failures grow with time ÷ TTL, unless resolvers serve stale. Follow-up: *"How do you design for
   it?"* Name servers from two providers, long TTLs on records that rarely change.
3. **"How would you do global failover with DNS?"** Health-checked short-TTL records, and the honest
   numbers: about 90 s detection plus a TTL plus runtime caches, with pinned clients never moving.
   Pair it with load balancers in each region.
4. **"Is DNS UDP or TCP?"** Both: UDP first, TCP for truncated answers above 1,232 bytes and for
   copying whole zones to secondary servers, and TCP support is mandatory.
5. **"Should we turn on DNSSEC?"** What it protects (forged answers, for validating users), what it
   costs (size, cold-cache lookups, fail-closed outages), and how to do it safely (automated
   rollovers, ECDSA).

## Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A record has a TTL of 3,600 s. At 14:00 you change the address and set the TTL to 60 s in the same edit. When can you safely switch off the old server?</summary>

Not before about 15:00, and only once its traffic and open connections have dropped to near zero.
Caches that fetched the record shortly before 14:00 got it with the old 3,600-second TTL. To cut over
quickly you had to lower the TTL at 13:00 or earlier.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A deploy pipeline polled report.example.in for 10 minutes before the record was created at 09:30. The zone's SOA has TTL 3600 and minimum 900. At 09:40 some users still get NXDOMAIN. Why, and when does it end?</summary>

Every resolver the pipeline's checks went through cached the NXDOMAIN for min(3600, 900) = 900 s,
so the last one, cached shortly before 09:30, can last until about 09:45, plus any runtime cache.
Resolvers that were never asked fetched the new record at once.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Your name servers are unreachable for 40 minutes. Record A has a TTL of 300 s, record B a TTL of 86,400 s. What happens to each, and what does serve-stale change?</summary>

Users of A start failing as copies expire and nearly all fail within five minutes; users of B
almost never notice, since few copies expire in 40 minutes. Serve-stale keeps A working for users
whose resolver had a copy, but cannot help a cache that never had one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A bug publishes your API's record with no addresses for 20 minutes. Your negative TTL is 3,600 s and your resolvers serve stale. How long are users affected?</summary>

Serve-stale does not apply: the servers answered, and an empty answer is valid. Every cache that
fetched during those 20 minutes cached "no addresses" for up to 3,600 s (or the resolver's cap,
one hour in Unbound), so users can fail for up to an hour after the fix. A short SOA minimum keeps
this tail short.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Four servers behind one name with round-robin DNS. Monitoring shows each server averaging about 25% of traffic, yet one of them falls over every few minutes. How?</summary>

The authoritative server decides per resolver cache, a few big resolvers carry most users, and many
clients take the first address. At any moment one server can hold several big resolvers' users,
and the hot spot moves every TTL, so averages look even. Size for the peak, or put a load balancer
behind each address.

</details>

## Recap

- A DNS answer is a **lease** you cannot recall. Plan for the longest time *any* holder might keep
  it: resolver caps, runtime caches (Java 30 s, Chrome 60 s) and open connections.
- After a change, copies live for up to the **old** TTL. Lower it one old TTL ahead, change, drain
  the old server's connections, then switch it off.
- **"No" is cached**: NXDOMAIN and NODATA for min(SOA TTL, SOA minimum), and resolution failures for
  1 s to 5 min. NXDOMAIN covers the subtree, and NSEC proofs cover ranges.
- In an outage, failures grow with **time ÷ TTL**; serve-stale hides an unreachable server but never
  an empty record, which also lingers for the negative TTL after the fix.
- Resolvers spread queries over the name servers they list: make each equally strong, and use two
  providers for critical names. DNS balancing is per resolver and lumpy; failover takes minutes.
- Answers above **1,232 bytes** go to TCP; DNSSEC adds size, lookups and a fail-closed mode.
